Host it yourself

serving 01 // their terms, declined // their servers, too

A platform’s rules are written for its advertisers, payment processors and app stores, and they change when those do. Each change takes a slice of what’s allowed, and the people who posted it, with it. A community built there owns nothing and has no say in what happens to it.

A den has one rulebook, written by the people who run it, like a friend hosting a game server. Dens gives them what they need to keep it: moderators, bans, deleting messages, and auto-delete so old conversations fade instead of piling up. It isn’t a place to threaten people or to trade in material whose making hurt someone; that’s illegal everywhere Dens is, and nothing here changes that. It is a place where adults decide for themselves what they’ll say, read and make, and answer to each other for it rather than to a policy written for someone else.

Hosting means forwarding a few ports on your router and pointing a domain at your IP. That’s the fiddly part, so there’s a step by step guide written for people who’ve never done either.

Encrypted and pseudonymous by default

serving 02 // not under their watch // smile, you're on camera. not here though

Dens seals your direct messages end to end, so only the two of you can read them. It knows you only as a username and a key that never leaves your computer: no email, no phone number, no account that follows you from den to den. And it strips the location and camera details out of your photos before they’re sent, and never writes your IP address to disk.

Pseudonymous, not anonymous{{ partial "landing/arrow.html" . }}

In a den you go by a name you pick, not your real one, and you never hand over an email or a phone number. But the den still sees your IP address while you’re connected, like any server does, so it’s no hiding place from whoever runs it.

under the hood

Open source{{ partial "landing/arrow.html" . }}

Every line is on GitHub under the MIT license. Read it, fork it, run it, rip it apart. If anything on this page is a lie, you can check.

Built for home internet{{ partial "landing/arrow.html" . }}

Dens is meant to run on a normal PC on a residential connection, where upload speed is precious. A den is sized for about a thousand members.

Why it asks for admin{{ partial "landing/arrow.html" . }}

Installing and updating ask for admin, but Dens itself never runs as admin. That’s how Dens gets its own locked-down system account: it runs while you’re logged out, and your keys sit in a separate process that your browser, your games and whatever that mod installer did can’t read without admin rights.

What seals your DMs{{ partial "landing/arrow.html" . }}

Standard parts, not homemade ones: X25519 and post-quantum ML-KEM-768 set up each DM’s key, and XChaCha20-Poly1305 seals what you send, all from Go’s own crypto libraries. How they fit together is Dens’s own design, public but not yet independently audited. Your key is made on your computer and the den never sees it, so it carries your DMs without being able to read them. See Privacy for what Dens protects you from and what it doesn’t.

Dens has nobody to pay back. There’s no company and no central server: each den runs on a computer belonging to one of its members, so the only running cost is a domain name. The downloads are built by GitHub Actions and served from Cloudflare R2, both free at this size. It didn’t need investors either. One person built it, working with AI, and every line is public, so the work is there to check. Dens is free software under the MIT license. Nobody makes money off your den, including me.
