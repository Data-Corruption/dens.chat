# Android

How Dens runs on Android phones. [design.md](design.md) has what every form shares.

An Android app joins dens from a phone. It hosts none, shares no screen, picks no speaker and has no push to talk. M12 to M14 build it: M12.1 built the app around the page, M12.2 its calls, M12.3 its notifications and M12.4 its releases and updates, and the rest of the design follows.

## Design

A spike on Android's emulator, a Galaxy S24 Ultra and a Pixel 9 Pro, all on Android 17, tried the design first, and what it found is below.

- *Shape.* The release's Go binary, built for Android, rides in the APK as `lib/arm64-v8a/libdens.so` and runs as a child process from the app's native library directory, where Android lets apps execute files; Syncthing-Fork does the same, targeting Android 17. A copy elsewhere in the app's files won't run. The page runs in a WebView. A thin Kotlin shell does what only Kotlin can on Android: the foreground service and its notification, the Keystore, permissions, the file picker, downloads (named from their `Content-Disposition`, since the service sends every one as `application/octet-stream`), notifications, audio routing, and handing each update to Android's installer. It talks to the service over the control socket, as `dens open` does, holding one stream as the shortcut helper does.
- *The WebView stays the page's.* The page gets no JavaScript bridge, so nothing in it reaches the shell, and the shell keeps the WebView on the page: every other address, a message's links and YouTube's own included, opens in the phone's browser.
- *The build* uses cgo and the NDK, for Android alone, so name lookups go through Android's resolver and honour its Private DNS; a pure-Go binary finds no resolver there. Pion's interface listing needs `-checklinkname=0` on Android. Android's seams join `internal/platform/host` as `_android.go` files, with `!android` on their Linux counterparts, since Go builds Linux files for Android too.
- *What Android changes.* Apps may lock only 64 KiB of memory since Android 14, 16 pages at 4 KB and 4 at 16 KB, so locked memory falls back to ordinary memory there, which Android swaps only to compressed RAM; Samsung allows 64 MiB. Android kills child processes past 32 across the device, so media jobs run at most two at a time, under the foreground service. Android's seccomp filter kills `rseq` and `io_uring_setup`, which Go doesn't make, and Android 12's also kills `faccessat2`, `openat2`, `close_range`, `clone3` and `epoll_pwait2`, which Go's standard library avoids there; code that calls them directly gets the process killed. Apps that target Android 17 ask for `ACCESS_LOCAL_NETWORK` to reach their own subnet, which the phone's Wi-Fi is; loopback, the control socket and addresses beyond the router don't need it, so the app asks only for a den that resolves to one on the phone's subnet. The shell sets `TZ`, which Go reads for its local zone, and `TMPDIR`.
- *A phone sleeps.* Go's timers run on `CLOCK_MONOTONIC`, which stops while the CPU is suspended, and a phone off screen suspends between packets, 95% of the time on a desk. So nothing waits on a timer for a time on the wall clock: the session's renewal checks the wall clock each time the den's ping wakes the phone, as a timer set for ten minutes before the hour fires hours late there. And a dropped connection, after which no ping comes to wake the phone, asks the shell to hold it awake while it reconnects, with an alarm behind that. Durations in the logs come from the wall clock.
- *What the page loses.* Android's WebView has no `getDisplayMedia`, so the page hides sharing, while watching others' shares works; no usable `setSinkId`, so the shell routes audio; no notifications, so the service decides what to notify and the shell posts it; and no Clipboard API, refused even after a tap, so the page copies through `document.execCommand('copy')`. Android 12's own WebView, 91, doesn't know the CSP's `'wasm-unsafe-eval'`, which RNNoise needs, so the app checks the WebView's version against a floor and asks for an update below it.
- *Calls.* Chromium freezes a hidden page a minute after its sound stops, which suspends its audio contexts and pauses its audio elements: a call stops sending, and stays silent once the page thaws. So while the page has the microphone, the shell tells the WebView its window shows, whatever the screen does, and the page plays its audio elements again whenever it thaws from a freeze. Chromium fixes a stream's use as it opens it: one opened while the microphone is open, on a phone with an echo canceller, plays as a call's, and one opened before it as media, under the media volume and never on a Bluetooth headset during a call. The members' voices, played through audio elements, played as media too. So the page plays a call through an audio context it makes once the microphone is open, which a WebView lets start without a tap, and the call then follows the route the shell sets with `setCommunicationDevice`: a headset where one is connected and the loudspeaker otherwise, as Chromium starts a call too, or the earpiece when the member asks for it.
- *Keys.* The data key is wrapped with a Keystore key, hardware-backed where the phone has the hardware, whose material never enters the app: in StrongBox on both phones the spike tried, unwrapping in 34 to 50 ms, and with the phone locked too. The shell unwraps the data key at start and hands it to the child on its standard input. Android's backups and its transfers to a new phone leave Dens's data out, since the Keystore key can't go with them: moving phones is to be a Dens backup and restore, as between computers, which the app doesn't take yet, since its shell would have to wrap the backup's key.
- *Staying connected.* A foreground service holds the den connections while the app isn't on screen, with the notification that comes with it, as Google-free messengers do, and takes the microphone's type too while the page has the microphone. It kept a den's socket through a night of Doze, and on a sleeping phone the den's ping every 30 s, about 140 wakes an hour, costs about 1% of the battery a night. Swiped from recents, the app runs on; killed, the service starts again. Battery set to Restricted cuts the app's network as soon as it leaves the screen, and Android stops its service soon after, while Samsung's deep sleep kills the app outright. So the app says so when Android reports it restricted, and the install guide asks Galaxy owners to put Dens among Never auto sleeping apps, since Samsung puts unused apps to sleep by default. The service's own connections are all that keeps the app connected: no push service wakes it, so a phone that stops Dens hears nothing from its dens until Dens runs again.
- *Distribution.* The APK on the release host is how the app is installed and how it's updated: the member downloads it in the phone's browser, and from then on an updater in the app downloads each update, checks it as an install does, and hands it to Android's installer, where the member taps to install it (see Updates). Android 12 and later let an app update itself without a prompt, but Play Protect calls an app that asks for that, through `UPDATE_PACKAGES_WITHOUT_USER_ACTION`, harmful, and passes one with `REQUEST_INSTALL_PACKAGES` alone. Play Protect's verdict sticks to a package and its key, so development builds use a package name and key of their own. Installing from the browser, Android warns that the file might be harmful, asks to let the browser install apps, and offers Play Protect's scan. Not Google Play for now: its rules for new developers, its declarations for foreground services and its ban on apps updating themselves cost more than its reach is worth to Dens yet. Samsung's Auto Blocker, on by default on Galaxy phones that shipped with One UI 6.1.1 or later, blocks installing from the browser, and the updater's installs with it, until the member turns it off, so the install guide says how, and to leave it off.
- *Signing.* Every update must carry the signature of the key the app was installed with, so the release's key can't be replaced if lost: it lives in CI, encrypted, as secrets of an environment only `main` deploys to, and offline in two places, and a release's build refuses an APK that key alone didn't sign (release.md's The APK's key). Android 17's hybrid signature (scheme v3.2, a classical key with ML-DSA) counts as a rotation from it, so it can be added later.
- *Google's developer verification.* Dens doesn't register. Once Google's verification covers apps installed outside its participating stores, as it has in Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand since September 30, 2026, and plans everywhere in 2027, a certified phone installs an app from a developer it hasn't verified only through its "advanced flow", set up once per phone: Developer options, a day's wait, then on for 7 days or for good. ADB still installs anything. While the flow is off, updates to such an app fail, so the install guide says to choose for good, and the updater, when Android refuses an update for its developer (`EXTRA_DEVELOPER_VERIFICATION_FAILURE_REASON`, from Android 17), says where to allow apps from unverified developers, with a button to Developer options. Phones without Google's certification, such as those running GrapheneOS or LineageOS, check nothing, and the guide says so. Registering would make the app's ordinary install depend on Google's registry, which Google can withdraw from an app country by country, where the design has nothing depend on a company staying friendly; and it can't be taken back, since Google keeps the identity it was given. Not registering can be: when two signing keys claim a package name, Google gives it to the key with more than half its installs.
- *The spike* (`spikes/android/README.md` at commit df2cd7e) tried this design on the emulator and both phones, and its findings are the measurements above.

## The build

`./scripts/build.sh --android` builds the development APKs (`scripts/build/android.sh`): `out/android-arm64.apk` for phones and `out/android-amd64.apk` for the emulator, in about 20 seconds once warm, leaving the rest of `out/` as it is. Dens is built for each with cgo through the NDK's clang for API 31, the oldest Android the app runs on, with a development build's flags, stripped as a release's are, and `-checklinkname=0`; a binary with a segment aligned for less than 16 KB pages is refused. The APK is made without Gradle: `aapt2` compiles the resources and links them with the manifest (the code names no resource, so there's no R class), `kotlinc` compiles the shell, `d8` makes its dex with Kotlin's standard library, Python's `zipfile` adds the dex and `lib/<abi>/libdens.so`, and `zipalign` and `apksigner` finish it. `vendor.sh android` pins what it takes: NDK r30, the SDK's build tools and platform for Android 17, Kotlin 2.4.21 and Temurin's JDK 21. It needs an x86_64 host, with Python and unzip.

- A build without Gradle has no Android Lint, so `scripts/build/apicheck.py` stands in for its NewApi check. It lists every framework call in the shell's classes newer than API 31, from the SDK's API database, a call on one of the shell's own classes counting as one on the framework class it extends, and fails on any that `android/newer-apis.txt` doesn't list: those the shell makes behind a check of `Build.VERSION.SDK_INT`.
- A development build is `chat.dens.Dens.dev`, labelled Dens (development): the link renames the manifest's package, makes the app debuggable, and lays its name over the release's. Its version code counts the minutes since 2026 began, so each build updates the last, and its version name is the binary's. It's signed with a key of the developer's own, `~/.config/dens-dev/android.p12`, which the first build makes outside `out/`, since a phone takes an update only signed with the key its first install was.
- A release's (`android_release`, which `ci.sh --execute` runs from v1.5.0) is `chat.dens.Dens` for arm64, around Dens built with the release's flags and identity, at the release's version code (release.md), and signed with the release's key, from the keystore `ANDROID_KEYSTORE` holds, which `ANDROID_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD` opens: the password reaches `apksigner` through the environment, and the keystore is written to a directory of its own, removed as the build ends. The release pipeline's build refuses an APK signed by anything but the key `android/release.pem` names. The Android e2e's fixture releases are built the same way, for the emulator, with a key, an identity and a Sigstore of their own (see Testing the app).
- The third-party notices name Kotlin's standard library, which the dex carries whole, and the code from GWT, Guava, the ThreeTen backport and Boost in it.

## The form and its paths

On Android, `app.FormOf` gives the Android form (`layout.Android`), for a development build too, since the shell runs either: a development build differs only in its package, key and page port. It's a session form, as the Flatpak is, in the app's own files:

| | Path |
| --- | --- |
| Instance root | `<files>/dens/<name>`, where the shell names the app's files directory (`/data/user/0/chat.dens.Dens/files`) in `XDG_DATA_HOME` |
| Control and data directories | `<root>/control` and `<root>/data`, the client's cache too: Android empties an app's cache directory when storage runs low, even under the running app, and the cache's database can't lose its files |
| Control endpoint | `<root>/run/control.sock` |
| The wrapped data key | `<root>/control/datakey.keystore`, which the shell writes and reads, and the service checks as it checks its own |

Every directory is `0700` and every file `0600`, as for the other session forms. The shell also sets `HOME` to the files directory, where Go on Android would take `/sdcard` for home; `TMPDIR` to `<cache>/tmp`; and `TZ` to the phone's time zone. The instance config names the app's UID, the one account the control endpoint answers, and gives the page port 8484, or 18484 for a development build, which shares the phone's loopback with a release's.

Its start follows the Flatpak's (flatpak.md's Starting) but for the snapshot: Android installs no older version over a newer one, so the start keeps none. It waits up to 30 seconds for the lifecycle lock, for a Dens the shell started before that is still stopping. `install`, `update` and `uninstall` refuse the form, since Android installs, updates and removes the app, and so does `restore`; hosting is refused, and the settings say it needs Dens installed on Linux or Windows.

## The shell

`android/`: Kotlin with no AndroidX, in `chat.dens.shell`, around Dens's binary, which runs from the native library directory.

- *The service.* A foreground service of the `specialUse` type runs Dens (`libdens.so service run`) with the environment above and the data key on its standard input, which the shell closes once the key is written; its output goes to `<files>/shell/dens.out`, each start marked. When Dens ends, the shell starts it again after 2 seconds, doubling up to a minute while it keeps ending within a minute of starting; once it has so ended three times, the page's place says why, with Dens's last words, and opening the app, or its Try again, cuts the pause short. A start Dens refuses (`host.ExitRefused`) isn't tried again until the app is opened, and the page's place says why. `BOOT_COMPLETED` and `MY_PACKAGE_REPLACED` start the service once the app has run, and Android starts it again should its process die. Its notification, in a channel of its own, says it keeps the dens connected; until the member allows notifications, Android lists the service among those running rather than showing it.
- *The key.* The shell makes the data key as the instance is made: 32 random bytes, wrapped with an AES-256 key the Keystore generates, in StrongBox where the phone has one, with no authentication, so it opens with the phone locked, as after a restart in a pocket. The wrap is GCM, with the file's format and the key's use as associated data. The shell never makes a key over data that exists: a wrapped key gone beside lifecycle state, a Keystore key gone, or a wrap that won't open stops the service, and the page's place says so.
- *The stream.* The shell keeps one stream with Dens over the control endpoint (`OpShell`), opening it once Dens answers and again whenever it closes. Dens's first line, `hello`, gives its version and the page's address, which the activity loads, and each side pings every 15 seconds, as on the shortcut helper's stream. From M12.3, Dens sends `notify`, a notification to post, `unnotify`, those to take back, and `notifying`, whether the member turned any on; the shell sends `front`, whether the page's activity shows, and `notifies`, whether Android lets it post the member's notifications, as the stream opens and as either changes (see Notifications).
- *Its log*, `<files>/shell/shell.log` and Android's, holds events and sizes, never a name, a message, an address or a token.

## The page in the WebView

The launcher's activity shows the page in a WebView with no JavaScript bridge.

- *Pairing.* Once the stream says where the page is, the shell asks the page's API whether the WebView's session cookie still opens it, and where it doesn't, asks Dens for a pairing link (`OpPair`) and loads that, so the member never sees a token. It asks again as the app is opened, so a page logged out pairs again, and the Unpaired screen says to open Dens again. The WebView writes its cookies out lazily, so the shell flushes them two seconds after each load, and as the activity pauses.
- *The page's origin alone.* The main frame navigating anywhere else, as a message's link or `window.open` does, opens the address in the app that takes it, the phone's browser for most; YouTube's player plays in its frame. The page's permission requests are refused, but for the microphone, which the page's own origin gets for its calls (see Calls). The WebView sends Google no usage metrics, and its Safe Browsing is off, since it loads the page and YouTube's player alone.
- *Files.* The page's file input opens Android's picker, for several files where the page takes several. A download goes to the shell, which fetches it from Dens with the page's cookie and saves it to Downloads through MediaStore, under the name the `Content-Disposition` gives, its `filename*` first, kept to a name with no folder in it, typed by its extension, and synced to storage before a toast says it's saved.
- *Full screen* comes through `onShowCustomView`, the bars hidden until a swipe. *Back* leaves full screen, then walks the page's history, then closes the activity, which leaves Dens running. *Edge to edge:* the window draws under the bars on every version, as Android 15 and later have every app's, and the shell sets the WebView's margins to the bars, the cutout and the keyboard, so the composer stays above the keyboard.
- *The WebView's floor* is version 111: Tailwind's styles need Chromium 111, which knows the CSP's `'wasm-unsafe-eval'` too. Below it, the page's place says so, with a button to the WebView's page in the store, and Dens runs all the same.
- *Backups and transfers.* `allowBackup` is off, and the data extraction rules leave everything out of a cloud backup and out of a transfer to a new phone, which would carry a wrapped key with no Keystore key to open it.

## Calls

- *The microphone.* The page's `getUserMedia` reaches the shell's `onPermissionRequest`, and a request for the microphone alone, from the page's own origin, is the only one granted. The shell asks Android for `RECORD_AUDIO` where the app hasn't it, and the first time, for `POST_NOTIFICATIONS` beside it, since the call's notification holds the buttons that move its sound, and Android 13 and later show the service's notification only once the member allows notifications. Refused the microphone, the shell refuses the page, and a toast says where to allow it. Granted, the service takes the microphone's foreground service type beside its own, which Android needs to let the page record off screen, and the shell grants the page.
- *The call's end.* Android's recording callback says whether the app records: it names no app, but a phone records for one at a time. Ten seconds after it says the recording stopped, unless it starts again first, as when the member picks another microphone, the call is over, and the service drops the microphone's type. A page granted the microphone that never records, as when it couldn't open it, is over ten seconds after the grant.
- *Held.* From the grant until the call is over, the activity's WebView (`HeldWebView`) tells Chromium its window shows, whatever the screen does, so Chromium never freezes the call's page; after, it passes the window's visibility on as it is. Back at the page's first address moves the app behind the others during a call, as Home does, rather than closing the page and its call with it.
- *The route.* The WebView's Chromium routes a call itself as the microphone opens: to a wired headset, then a USB one, then a Bluetooth one if the app may see it (`BLUETOOTH_CONNECT`, which Dens doesn't ask for), and otherwise the loudspeaker; and it routes again only as one of those comes or goes. As the page first records, the shell routes the call with `setCommunicationDevice`, which needs no permission: to a wired headset, then a Bluetooth one, then the loudspeaker. Chromium routes its own way again as each new recording opens, as when the member picks another microphone, so the shell routes the call again then, to where the member last chose in the call, or where it goes by itself. A headset that connects during the call takes it, and when the headset the call plays on goes, the call goes where it would by itself, rather than to the earpiece, where Android puts it; the shell acts half a second after the change, so its word comes after Chromium's. The call's notification says where its sound plays, with a button for each other route Android has, the earpiece among them, which reaches the service and leaves the page as it is. On the S24 every stream of the call played as a call's, under the call's volume, and each move opened the call's streams again on the new route, which costs what the call sends a moment: 120 to 200 ms between the loudspeaker and the earpiece, and 700 ms onto a Bluetooth headset, as the voice processor's lag in the call's notes shows.
- *The page.* The service tells the page its form lets audio start without a click (`data-autoplay` on the page's root), and the page then makes its voice processor's audio context, and its call's, once the microphone is open, rather than in the click that joins. Each member's voice plays through a gain in the call's context, at the level the member chose for them, under the voice settings' call volume, and silent while they're deafened. The call volume, on every form, is there since the S24's loudspeaker plays a call too loud even at the call volume's lowest step. Chromium decodes a member's stream only while a media element plays it, so their element plays it too, muted. Chromium keeps the output stream that played a call's members for five seconds after the call ends, and hands it to the next call's; on the S24, whose call streams play on AAudio's MMAP output, one opened before the call ended won't start again (`AAUDIO_ERROR_DISCONNECTED`), and Chromium then drops it, which left every other quick rejoin silent. So a call that begins within six seconds of the last one's end plays its members' elements afresh two seconds after they start, once that stream has gone, and Chromium opens a new one. In every browser, a page that thaws from a freeze (`resume`) plays its call's elements again.
- *Logs.* The page's notes on its call add what it sent a second since its last summary, what its encoder aims at, where the browser says (Chromium does), and how far its voice processor's clock has fallen behind the page's since the call's first summary, from the processor's context's output timestamps: a renderer short of CPU renders less than the time gone by, and what the call sends has gaps, where Opus's rate adapting to the network lowers its aim. They note the page frozen and thawed too. The shell's log has the grant, each route and why it moved, and the call's end.

## Notifications

The service decides what notifies the member, and where (design.md's Notifications); on Android, the WebView has no notifications, so the shell posts them.

- *Posted* (`Notes`): each in a Messages channel of its own, at high importance, with its title, the den's name above it, and the start of the message, under the channel's key, so a channel's newer notification replaces its last. A den's notifications group under a summary of the den's, which goes once none of them is left. A tap opens the page's activity at the message: it loads the page at that address, or pairs it there where its session no longer opens it, and the page opens the DM at the message, which it takes from the fragment. The shell loads only a path of the page's form, a den or a channel's message.
- *Taken back* as Dens says, when the member reads the channel on any of their devices, or the message, its channel or the den goes; posted ones stay across Dens starting again, and the next stream takes them back.
- *Asking.* Once the member turns a kind on, Dens says `notifying`, and the activity asks Android for `POST_NOTIFICATIONS`, on Android 13 and later, while it's in front, unless it asked before: at the member's first call it asked beside the microphone (M12.2), and a member who refused then isn't asked again. The settings then say Android doesn't let Dens show them, and where to allow them, as the shell reports with `notifies`: notifications allowed for the app, and its Messages channel not turned off.
- *In front.* The activity tells Dens as it starts and stops (`front`), and Dens counts the page on screen only while it's in front: through a call the shell holds the page as shown whatever the screen does, so a DM the page shows during a call still notifies once the app is behind the others.

## Updates

The service checks the release host its release names, once a day while the member lets it, as an installed instance's update check does (`App.ChecksForUpdates`), and readies what it finds (`internal/app/apk.go`); a build without a release identity, as a development build, updates from nothing.

- *Downloaded and checked.* From the newer release, the service downloads the checksums and their cosign bundle first, and checks the bundle as `cosign verify-blob` would, against the release identity the build carries and the Sigstore trusted root it embeds (`release.TrustedRoot.Verify`): a Fulcio certificate naming the release workflow and its OIDC issuer; the checksums' digest, signed with the certificate's key; and Rekor's signed promise of the entry for that signature, whose time falls within the certificate's ten minutes. Only then does it download the version marker and the phone's APK, `android-<arch>.apk`, and check both against the checksums. It reaches the release host by https, but for a test release's on loopback, takes each file only up to a size, and downloads into a directory of its own, which takes `<root>/update`'s place once all of it checks out.
- *Kept.* What's in `<root>/update` has checked out: each start checks it again, offers it while it's newer than the running version, and removes it otherwise, as after the update installed. A download cut off tries again within the hour, and a release that doesn't check out a day later, so a bad APK on the host isn't downloaded each hour.
- *Offered.* The service tells the shell over its stream (`update`, with the version and the APK's path), as the stream opens and as an update becomes ready, where the member wants notices of updates. The shell posts it in an Updates channel of its own, at default importance, which alerts once an update: Android 17 bundles an app's quiet notifications, the service's among them, under one whose tap only opens the app. The page's home shows it too, with an Install button, which the service passes to the shell (`install`).
- *Installed.* The notification's tap, or Install, has the shell write the APK into a `PackageInstaller` session for the app's own package that requires the member's action, and commit it. Android asks the member, the first time, to let Dens install apps, then to confirm the update; it takes the APK only signed with the key the app was installed with, at a higher version code, and once it has, ends the app's processes and starts its service again (`MY_PACKAGE_REPLACED`). The session's status reaches `UpdateReceiver`: the confirmation starts from it while the app is in front, and otherwise the notification asks for the tap that confirms it; a member who cancels finds the update offered again; and a refusal is posted with what Android said, or, for a developer it couldn't verify, where to allow apps from unverified developers.
- *Settings.* The settings' Updates card checks once a day and shows the notices, as an installed Dens's does, and says the phone downloads what it finds.

## Testing the app

`./scripts/test.sh -android-e2e` runs `scripts/test-android-e2e.sh`, in two containers that stay between runs. A development den runs behind the pinned Caddy, with its internal authority, in `dens-browsers`, which `scripts/test/browser/setup.sh` makes (`scripts/test/android/den.sh`). The emulator runs in `dens-android`, which `scripts/test/android/setup.sh` makes: Debian 13 with `/dev/kvm`, adb, the emulator, and system images for Android 17 and 12 from vendor.sh's verified downloads (`vendor.sh android-emulator`), which `box.sh` unpacks there, and Node.js for the drivers. The emulator's sound card hears silence and plays to nowhere, so a call's page records as on a phone, and Android reports it to the app. The harness builds the den's binary and the APKs first, unless given `--no-build`.

In Android 17's emulator the app installs, makes its key in the Keystore and pairs its page without a token. The page takes a local password and joins the den as a new member each run, the app's Dens trusting the den's authority through `SSL_CERT_FILE`, which a development build takes from `<files>/shell/env`. A message goes each way from the composer; a phone photo with GPS goes as its smaller copy, and saved from the viewer, reaches Downloads whole, with no GPS, camera or time in it as the pinned exiftool reads it. The page joins a call with the den's owner, desk, in Chromium in `dens-browsers` with a fake microphone that speaks: Android asks for the microphone and notifications, which `phone.sh allow` taps as a member would, the service takes the microphone's type, and the call goes to the loudspeaker. Desk goes quiet, and the screen goes off for two and a half minutes: the page stays shown and unfrozen, sending and receiving, its voices playing through its call's context, and hears desk when it speaks again before the screen comes on; ten seconds after the page leaves, the app lets the page and the microphone go. `call.mjs` drives each side, and judges the call from what they saw. Then the member and desk check a DM's digits, and the member turns DMs on in the page's settings: Android isn't asked again, since the member allowed notifications at the call. A DM from desk while the app is behind the others posts its notification, in the app's Messages channel, and its tap in the shade opens the page at the message; the next, read through the den, is taken back. Dens killed under the shell starts again, and the app force-stopped keeps its page's session. A link opens outside the page, and Back leaves it. The host package's tests, which hold Android's seams, pass in the emulator as the app. Then the release's app updates itself, from fixture releases that `scripts/test/android/releases.sh` makes: v99.0.1-e2e and v99.0.2-e2e for the emulator, signed with a key of their own, their checksums signed by a Sigstore of their own (`internal/platform/release/sigstoretest`), whose trusted root the builds embed in place of Sigstore's, with an identity of their own. Installed from adb, v99.0.1-e2e finds v99.0.2-e2e on its release host, which the container serves on the emulator's loopback through `adb reverse`. Served first with checksums another workflow signed, the updater refuses them before downloading the APK; served as signed, once Dens starts again, it downloads and checks the APK, the shell posts that it's ready, and the notification's tap hands it to Android's installer, whose Update the e2e taps, as the member allowed Dens to install apps: the app becomes v99.0.2-e2e, and Android starts its service again. In Android 12's emulator Dens runs, and the page's place asks for a newer WebView, and the release's app updates itself as in Android 17's. `check.mjs` does the page's steps as a member would, through `drive.mjs`, which drives a development build's WebView over the DevTools protocol, as the browser checks drive Chromium. The emulators stop at the end, and logs land in `out/android-e2e-logs/<run>/`.

`scripts/test/android/phone.sh SERIAL` works a phone through the same container's adb, over wireless debugging: `pair` and `connect`, `install arm64`, `log`, `pull` (the shell's files and Dens's logs, through `run-as`) and `drive`. In a call its member joined on the phone, `call.mjs watch` samples the page's side every 5 seconds, and `bg.sh SERIAL [SECONDS]` turns the screen off for ten minutes, or as long as it's given, sampling the page and the app's audio as Android plays it, then says whether the page hid or froze, what went each way and where the call played. Each step's manual test runs on the S24 Ultra as well.
