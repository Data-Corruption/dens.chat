# Dens — Design Doc

Sep 25, 2026 · @Matthew Pombo

Tags such as (M3) name the milestone that built something, or will. [milestones.md](milestones.md) lists them, with the plan for the one under way.

## Overview

Dens is a self-hosted chat app for Linux and Windows with text, voice and screen share, built so small communities can talk without a platform mining or owning their conversations. One install hosts at most one den (community). Every install is also a client: a local Go service that serves the chat UI to the user's own browser on localhost.

**Goals**

- One den per install, sized for about 1,000 members, 500 online, 10 in voice and 2 screen shares.
- No central service. Identity lives on the user's machine; dens are independent servers.
- Credible exit: users and dens can move machines, and nothing depends on a company staying friendly.
- Linux and Windows for both client and den, from one codebase with a small platform layer. Linux is the recommended platform because its service sandbox is stronger, and the docs say so.
- Works in any modern browser; tested on LibreWolf, Ungoogled Chromium, Waterfox and Brave.
- Manageable self-hosting: storage limits members can see and manage, no email dependency.

**Non-goals**

- End-to-end encryption for channels. The den, and so its owner, can read channel messages and files, and the docs say so. DMs are the exception: from M1.7 they're end-to-end encrypted (see End-to-end encryption).
- Protection from targeted investigations or state actors.
- macOS support, multi-tenancy, federation between dens.
- Custom permission systems, forums, threads, bots or other feature rabbit holes in v1.

## Privacy model

Dens offers living-room privacy: a closed door, not a bunker. The branding pillars are ambient privacy, local-first and credible exit.

| Protects against | Does not protect against |
| --- | --- |
| Platforms mining or monetizing conversations | The den owner, who can read channel messages and files, and sees who DMs whom and when |
| Data brokers and ad profiling | Members screenshotting or repeating things |
| Bulk data requests to a large provider | A compromised device (client or den) |
| History disappearing when a company changes its terms | A targeted investigation or state actor |
| Casual filesystem scanners and backup snoopers (local encryption) | Traffic analysis: the den sees member IPs |
| The den owner reading DMs and the photos in them (end-to-end encrypted from M1.7) | Members who compare codes over a channel the den's owner controls, or with someone pretending to be the other member, and a den that hands out keys of its own to members who never compare (M16.1) |

The public docs include a short philosophy section: being able to say dumb things, joke, vent and make art without a permanent searchable record is part of being human. They recommend Linux over Windows, Ungoogled Chromium, and not posting identifying details for low-stakes use, and point to Signal, Qubes OS and similar tools for higher-stakes threat models, noting that none of them are bulletproof.

Design rules that follow from this:

- Collect less: no IP addresses written to disk by default, and Caddy access logs stay off.
- Delete for real: deletions remove rows, with SQLite `secure_delete` on.
- Let conversations evaporate: an optional den-wide message retention period.
- Say plainly who can read what: in onboarding and the docs, once, and on the page by marking what's end-to-end encrypted (M8.3).

## Architecture

Dens is one static Go binary, forked from Sprout, that always runs the client and optionally runs a den. It runs as a hardened system-level service under a dedicated account, never as the person's desktop user: a systemd unit on Linux and an SCM (Service Control Manager) service on Windows (see [installed.md](installed.md)). The Flatpak (M9) and the Android app (M12) are the exceptions: they join dens and host none, and run as the person, in their session or under the app (see [flatpak.md](flatpak.md) and [android.md](android.md)).

**Traffic paths**

| Path | Transport | Carries |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Browser ↔ local service | HTTP + WebSocket on loopback, at `localhost` (client listener) | UI, local API, signaling relay |
| Local service ↔ remote den | HTTPS + WSS via the den's Caddy | Auth, messages, presence, files, signaling |
| Local service ↔ own den | HTTP + WebSocket to the den listener on 127.0.0.1 | The same, for the den this install hosts |
| Browser ↔ den SFU | UDP (DTLS-SRTP), TCP fallback | Voice and screen share media only |
| CLI ↔ local service | Unix socket (Linux), named pipe (Windows) | Pairing tokens, admin commands, backups |
| Local service ↔ YouTube | HTTPS | A linked video's title and picture, for a member who turned YouTube players on (M4.1) |
| Browser ↔ YouTube | HTTPS, in a sandboxed frame | YouTube's player, once that member plays a video (M4.1) |

The browser never talks to a den over HTTP. All den content reaches the page through the local service, so it only ever runs under the localhost origin.

The owner's client reaches its own den over loopback, not through the public domain. That works before DNS and Caddy are set up, and on routers that can't route to their own public address (no hairpin NAT). The den still proves its identity key at every login, so loopback loses no authentication. Once the den moves to another computer and this install removes its copy (M10), its client reaches the den at its address, as any member's does (see Backup, restore, move). The client-to-den protocol is specified in [protocol.md](protocol.md).

**Client UI.** The page is a Preact app (about 10 KB) built by the pinned esbuild into one hashed bundle. Preact injects no scripts or styles at runtime, so the CSP stays `script-src 'self'` with no inline code. The stylesheet carries every DaisyUI theme, which a member picks in the settings and the browser keeps; until they pick one, the page follows the device's light or dark preference, with Light and Dark (M3.3). Every link the page draws, a message's, a cover's or its own, is set apart by its color, and underlined only under a pointer that hovers or with the keyboard's focus, by one rule for DaisyUI's `link` in the utilities' own cascade layer, which outranks DaisyUI's, nested within it (M14.1); a touch screen's tap leaves no underline behind. The page has no header (M3.3): a den's channel list holds the way home, at the left of the den's name, which shows the den's status only while it isn't connected, and the cog for the settings sits in the member's panel at its foot; home has a cog of its own, fixed at the bottom left, and a development instance's tab names it. The page keeps one WebSocket to the local service, and one that hasn't opened within a few seconds counts as down, so the page says it's out of touch rather than looking fine while it hears nothing. Views reload once it's back, and a reload applies again what changed while it was out, since the snapshot it fetched may be older. Every request names the version of Dens that served the page, and a service of another version, as one an update restarted, refuses it, so the page loads again from it rather than ask in a shape it may not know (M9.3). WebSockets, both den and local, use `github.com/coder/websocket`: small, context-first and without dependencies, since the standard library has none.

**The page's parts (M8.3).**

- The settings are a dialog over whatever the page shows, so a den and a call stay in view, in five tabs, which the phone layout lists as pages (M13.3, see The phone layout): Appearance, Animations (M15.1, see Profiles), above the theme, whose long list of themes would push it out of sight; Chat & media, compressing photos and videos, links and YouTube's players; Notifications & sounds, what notifies the member and the page's sounds (M14.2, see Notifications and Sounds); Voice & video, the microphone and speaker, noise suppression, how the member sends their voice, their shortcuts and how they share their screen; and System: Hosting (M9.1), whose switch, Host a den on this computer, stands beside a line on what a den needs and the hosting guide's i, with the address Caddy forwards to and the media ports while hosting is on; in the Flatpak, On this computer, with Remember on this computer, starting at sign-in and Quit (M9.3); Backup (M9.3); the cache, update checks, the local password, logging, signing this browser out, or in the Android app this page, and the version.
- Home is a launcher. Each den is a row: a dot for its state, named in words when it isn't connected, who the member is there and their role, a large Open and a small Manage. Sign-ins and approvals waiting on this device show above the list, and Add a den takes one pasted thing, an invite or a den's address, and goes on a screen at a time. Creating the den this install hosts is a card above the list until its owner account exists, once hosting is on (M9). While hosting is off, the den's row says so and leads to the switch, and so does the end of Add a den where this install can host one (M9.1, M13.3).
- Manage is a dialog for each den, in two parts, which the phone layout lists as pages (M13.3). The den's name at the top of its list opens it, marked ▾, as a server's name opens its menu in other chat apps, and on the phone layout at the list of its sections; Home's Manage opens it too, and the cog beside the member's name stays the app's settings, so what's the den's and what's this device's Dens stay apart (M14.3). This den holds General, with the den's address and Den ID for every member and the owner's edits; Invites, Bans, the Soundboard (M14.3) and Emoji (M15.2), for staff; and Limits, the owner's. Their edits gather under one Save changes bar, and closing with changes unsaved asks first. You in this den holds Devices, Password and recovery, and Your files. A den that isn't connected shows General alone.
- On a den's page the member list starts open on screens wide enough to show it beside the channel, and channels' kinds, the soundboard, the lock on DMs and the shield on staff-only channels are drawn icons of one size, not emoji. The lock is end-to-end encryption's alone, a DM's, a group's or a call's, so a staff-only channel has a shield (M16.4).
- Dates take one form, the medium one in the reader's locale, as Oct 12, 2026, with the time where it matters, as when an invite expires.

**The page's words (M8.3).** The page explains itself where it can, so the docs needn't (see Public site):

- Helper text is one line. The label does the work, and details, such as 2,560 pixels or 30 ms of delay, go in a tooltip.
- It uses the words people know, such as compress, cache, resolution and shortcuts, and no implementation names: noise suppression, not RNNoise; the DM recovery key, the name other encrypted apps taught people, for the DM seal.
- It describes only what applies: the page knows its browser and system, and shows a figure, such as how much the cache holds, rather than a sentence about it.
- A warning comes with the action it's about, in its confirmation, rather than ahead of it in a paragraph.
- What takes more than a line, such as where to compare codes and why, or what disappearing messages do, is the guide's: the page says what to do, minimally, and links to the guide, which opens in a new tab, rather than explaining in place (M16.4).
- Nothing says the den or its owner can see something, which members take as given in a place someone else hosts; the page marks what's end-to-end encrypted, and the docs say who can read what, once.

**Two listeners, not one**

Keep the client listener and den listener separate. Caddy forwards remote requests from 127.0.0.1, so on a single listener every remote den user would also reach the local-only client API (vault, pairing, admin) unless routing is perfect. Separate listeners make that mistake impossible:

- **Client listener:** `127.0.0.1:<client-port>` and `[::1]:<client-port>`, fixed port, strict `Host` and `Origin` checks, only ever used by the local browser. It binds both loopbacks: with only IPv4 bound, another local user can bind `[::1]` on the same port and catch browsers that resolve `localhost` to IPv6.
- **Den listener:** `127.0.0.1:<den-port>`, only exposed through Caddy, serves only den routes. It is TCP on both platforms: Caddy runs as its own user and couldn't reach a socket inside the private state directory.

**Pairing the browser**

- The desktop user runs `dens open`, and the CLI connects to the control endpoint.
- The service authorizes the caller by the identity the OS reports for the connection (`SO_PEERCRED` UID on Linux, the pipe client's token SID on Windows) against the one desktop user recorded at install. The installer records the sudo caller (`SUDO_UID`) on Linux and the signed-in console user on Windows, with a `--user` flag to override either. No group membership is involved, so pairing works straight after install without logging out and back in.
- On Linux the socket is connectable by any local user (mode 0666) and the peer check does the authorization, as the system D-Bus does. On Windows the pipe's DACL grants SYSTEM and the service SID full access and the recorded user read and write-data rights only (`0x12019b`). Never grant the user `GENERIC_WRITE` on the pipe: it includes `FILE_CREATE_PIPE_INSTANCE`, which lets the user add server instances and pose as the service. The service reads the caller's SID by impersonating it at identification level, which needs no `SeImpersonatePrivilege`.
- The service keeps a listening pipe instance open at all times, creating the next instance before serving the current one, so no other process can re-create the name between connections.
- The CLI verifies the other end too. On Linux the peer UID must be the service account's. On Windows the pipe's server process ID must match the one the SCM reports for `dens-<name>`, and the service creates the pipe with `FILE_FLAG_FIRST_PIPE_INSTANCE`, so it fails closed if another process claimed the name first.
- The service returns a one-time token and its client port. The CLI opens `http://localhost:<port>/#token=…` (`xdg-open` on Linux, `ShellExecute` on Windows).
- The page lives at `localhost`, not `127.0.0.1` (M4.1): YouTube's player won't play for a page at an IP address (see YouTube players). Browsers resolve `localhost` to loopback themselves, never through DNS, and treat it as a secure context, as they do `127.0.0.1`. A browser that loads the page at `127.0.0.1` or `[::1]` as a document is redirected to `localhost`, keeping the path and, since browsers carry a fragment across a redirect, the pairing token. Scripts and tests, which don't load it as a document, get their answer where they asked.
- The page exchanges it for an `HttpOnly`, `SameSite=Strict` session cookie and clears the fragment. On the first pairing, the page then asks for a new local password before anything else (see Local identity).
- A paired session lasts 30 days from its last use; the service extends it at most once a day. Its cookie is named for the client port (`dens_session_<port>`), because cookies aren't scoped by port and each instance has its own.
- The port comes from the authenticated service while it holds that listener on both loopbacks. Neither platform lets another account bind the same address and port, so no separate port-ownership check is needed. On Windows another account can bind the wildcard address on that port, but loopback connections still reach the more specific socket.

**Files in and out**

- Uploads come from the browser's file picker, drag and drop, or a paste; downloads use `Content-Disposition: attachment` so the browser saves them as the desktop user.
- The service never needs write access to the user's home directory, which removes the Downloads permission step from install.

**Third-party notices (M3)**

The binary includes others' code: Go's standard library and modules such as Pion and the SQLite driver; FFmpeg, zlib and libaom in the media module; RNNoise; Preact, Tailwind and DaisyUI in the page; and Unicode's emoji data, which the page's and the den's list of emoji is made from (M15.2). Most of their licenses ask that copies of the binary carry their notices, and the LGPL also asks for FFmpeg's source, which each release publishes.

- `scripts/notices.sh` gathers every license into one file that the binary embeds: those of the Go modules it links, from the module cache, and those of the inputs `scripts/vendor.sh` pins. The file is committed, so a new dependency shows up in review as a new license, and CI writes it again and fails if it differs.
- `dens licenses` prints it, and the page's settings link to it.

## Local identity and encryption

Each install has one local user, stored in an encrypted vault, and one random data key that encrypts it. The data key is wrapped twice: once bound to the host for unattended boot, and once by the user's password for backup and moving machines.

**What the vault holds**

- The list of joined dens: URL, den identity key fingerprint, display name.
- One Ed25519 keypair per den, plus the current session token for each.
- The DM seal (M1.7).
- Each den's record of the identities the member's Dens kept there, and those they verified (M16.1).
- Local settings and preferences.

Sensitive fields in SQLite are encrypted one by one with the same data key (XChaCha20-Poly1305): on a client, the vault; on a den, message and DM text, and the names of uploads. A den's uploads are encrypted with it too (see Files and media), and so is everything a client caches from other dens, in a database and directory of the cache's own (M6; see The client's cache). This is a speed bump against scanners and backup snoopers, not protection from root or a live compromise, and it doesn't hide anything from the den owner, whose install holds the den's key. Metadata such as timestamps and IDs stays plaintext so queries work.

**Envelope encryption**

1. Install generates a random 256-bit data key.
2. A host-bound copy lets the service start without a password:
   - Linux: an encrypted credential loaded with `LoadCredentialEncrypted=` in the unit. systemd decrypts it at start, bound to the host key or TPM, and hands it to the service in `$CREDENTIALS_DIRECTORY`.
   - Windows: a machine-scope DPAPI blob in the state directory, whose DACL allows only the service account, SYSTEM and Administrators. The service decrypts it at start.
   - The Flatpak (M9): none, so it starts locked until the member types the local password, unless they ask it to remember. Then the app's secret from the Secret portal, kept in their keyring, wraps a copy, or the key is kept in a file where the portal can't be trusted (see [flatpak.md](flatpak.md)).
   - Android (M12): a Keystore key, which the app's shell uses to unwrap the data key at start (see [android.md](android.md)).
3. A second copy is wrapped with a key derived from the local password (Argon2id) and kept in the storage root.

The privileged CLI does the host wrapping (at install and restore); the service only unwraps. Password wrapping and all field encryption are shared code.

**Setting the password.** Installers never prompt, so they stay non-interactive. The first `dens open` leads to a page that sets the local password, and the service wraps the data key with it. `dens password` does the same from the command line, or changes the password given the current one, so a server never needs the page (M10); the Flatpak keeps its password in its page, whose first asks whether to remember its key. Until a password exists, the service runs on the host-bound copy alone, and backup and key export stay disabled.

Because this is a root-managed system unit on Linux, it avoids the unprivileged `systemd-creds --user` path entirely. `LoadCredentialEncrypted=` arrived in systemd 250, which becomes the Linux minimum version. Every supported distro ships 252 or newer, and the platform spike confirmed encrypted credentials on 252 through 262.

**Backup, restore, move**

- `dens backup` writes a consistent SQLite snapshot (`VACUUM INTO`), the uploads directory, and the password-wrapped data key. Never a raw copy of a live WAL database. It leaves out the client's cache, which holds only copies of what dens hold (M6). While a backup is written, the den holds back taking deleted files off the disk, so the archive has every file its snapshot names (M8). The archive is a tar at gzip's fastest level (M10): the uploads are sealed, so they don't compress, and the default level takes them at an eighth of the speed, 116 MiB/s against 950 with Go 1.26.7, which releases build with.
- A backup cut short is never kept as whole (M10). A body on the control endpoint comes in frames and ends with an empty one, so `dens backup` fails on one that stops partway and keeps nothing, and each write has a minute, so a long archive streams to its end; the command waits up to an hour for the database's snapshot, before the archive's first byte. The page's download drops its connection when the archive fails, so the browser marks the download failed. The service says at least how large the archive will be before it streams: `dens backup` refuses a folder without room for it, and the page shows the size.
- The settings' Backup exports the same archive as a download (M9.3), which the Flatpak needs: its CLI's working directory is inside the sandbox, not the member's. After the local password, a client route gives a link that works once, for a minute, which streams the archive.
- The data directory's `tmp`, which holds files only while the service works on them (uploads arriving, scratch files, a backup's snapshot), is emptied as the service starts, so nothing a crash left there outlives it (M8).
- `dens restore <file>`, run as root or Administrator, asks for the password, unwraps the data key, and re-wraps it for the new host (`systemd-creds` or DPAPI). Data is never re-encrypted. The archive's database comes before its uploads, so restore checks the password and the key as soon as it's out, before any upload; and it refuses a backup the disk hasn't room for, keeping a gigabyte free as uploads do, rather than holding archives to fixed sizes, which a den an owner lets hold more would outgrow, each upload with a preview being two files (M10). Restore follows the same lifecycle steps as an update, and a backup from an older version migrates when the service starts; one from a newer version is refused. A session form's restore runs as its user, stopping its service first (M9.3); the Flatpak's restored instance starts locked, and opens with the backup's local password.
- Pairing doesn't carry over: the service clears the restored data's browser sessions on its first start, so browsers pair again with `dens open`.
- Nor does hosting (M9.1): a restored backup starts with hosting off, whatever it held, so a copy of a den never goes online by itself, as when a backup is restored to read it. Its owner turns hosting on with the backup's local password.
- A den moves to another computer with its **export** (M10), not a backup. A backup holds the whole install, the client's accounts in every den it joined, with their device keys and DM seals, included, so a server restored from one would be the same devices as the owner's computer: online at once, and signing that device out from either signs out both. And the install a den left would keep its copy, which its client reaches on loopback whether hosting is on or off, so it would never follow the den. `dens den export`, and the settings' Move the den to another computer, write an archive in the backup's format whose database holds the den alone (`database.SnapshotDen`): the den's tables, `den` and `den_*`, as they are, and the vault's check value and data key sealed under the local password; every other table empty, the vault's DM seal gone, and a new install's settings. Those rows go with `secure_delete` on, and the copy is vacuumed, so no free page keeps them. It holds every file the den holds, DMs' included, with no way to leave them out: once the copy the den left is removed, and members' caches drop with the new sync epoch, nobody would have them any more. Its manifest says it's a den's export, and names the den. An export refuses while hosting is on, so nothing reaches the den after its snapshot; the page's Move turns hosting off first, after the local password. It's recorded in the configuration (`DenExported`), and turning hosting on forgets it, after a confirmation that another computer may host the den by now, and members would then reach whichever one its address points at.
- Restoring an export is restoring a backup: hosting starts off, no browser is paired, and the den starts a new sync epoch. The server's client holds no account, so it connects to nothing, and home's Finish setting up shows only while the den has no owner, which the dens view says, so a server holding an export offers nothing to set up. The restore names the export, and ends with `dens den hosting on` where a backup's says to pair a browser. A restore replaces everything the instance holds, so an export goes only where nothing would be lost, as a new server's, or an instance of its own on a computer whose Dens is in use: restore refuses one over an instance that hosts a den or has joined one, as soon as the export's database is out, saying what the instance holds, and pointing to an instance of its own, or an uninstall where nothing in it needs keeping, which is the deliberate way to empty an install, so no flag forces the restore. The same den, on an instance that has joined none, is a move done again with a later export, and restores. Restore runs as root, so it reads the instance's database without leaving a file beside it that the service account couldn't open: read-only while the service runs, through the WAL and index its connections keep, and as immutable once it's stopped, when closing has folded the WAL in; a WAL a crash left without its index it doesn't read, and refuses. A backup still replaces any instance, since replacing what's there is what restoring one is for. The Flatpak, which hosts no den, refuses an export before any upload is written. An older release restores an export as a backup, since it ignores the manifest's new field.
- The install a den left deletes its copy (`dens den remove`, the settings' Remove from this computer) once another computer serves the den: with hosting off, an export since hosting was last on, and the den answering at its address from this computer and proving its identity there, as the challenge of Manage's Test does, which with hosting off here means another computer serves it. A refusal names the check that failed, and for the address what to try: the other computer behind Caddy, the name pointing at it and the router forwarding there, or, where a router can't reach its own public address, a hosts entry with the server's local one. Then, after the local password, and with hosting held off, one transaction deletes every den table's rows, with `secure_delete` on, and the log is checkpointed; the uploads go, as backups being written let them; and the den forgets its identity, its key and what it held in memory of its members, so `OwnDen` reports none. The client's connection to the den starts again at its address, as any den's would, keeping the account there, owner or not, with its history and DMs, without a restart. The record of the export goes too. Files a crash leaves behind go as the service next starts, which tidies the uploads with no den as with one.
- `dens den hosting on` and `off` turn hosting on and off over the control endpoint, as the settings' switch does (M10), so a server needs no browser: on takes the local password, and prints the den's address, the port Caddy forwards to and the media ports, or the port that's held and the installer flag that moves it; off asks first, which `--yes` skips. `dens status` shows the switch, why a den doesn't serve, and an export not followed by hosting.
- A restored den starts a new sync epoch on that first start, so its members' clients drop what they cached from it (M6; see What changed while away). Its ID counters, but the change log's, move on to the restore's time in microseconds (M11): the backup's are where they were when it was made, so a message, channel or member made after the restore could otherwise take the ID of one the restore lost, which a link to a message, in another den or outside Dens, where no epoch reaches, would then open. The time is past every ID the den gave out, counted from 1, or from an earlier restore's time at fewer than a million a second.
- Backups have the same format on both platforms, so a client or den can move between Linux and Windows.
- Changing the password re-wraps the data key only.
- Lost password and lost machine together means the local data is gone by design. Den accounts can still be recovered with recovery codes.

**Re-authentication for sensitive actions**

There is no idle lock in v1; the OS screen lock covers someone at an unlocked desktop. Instead, a paired browser must re-enter the local password for sensitive actions: exporting a backup, viewing or exporting keys, viewing the DM seal (M1.7), changing the local password, restoring, turning hosting on, which opens ports to the internet, having the Flatpak remember its key (M9), and exporting the den or removing its copy (M10). Someone at an unlocked machine could read chats, but not take the identity with them. An optional idle lock can come later.

**Service hygiene**

- The data key and vault stay in memory only in the service process; nothing sensitive goes to logs.
- Core dumps are disabled (`LimitCORE=0` on Linux; `dens.exe` excluded from Windows Error Reporting), and key material is held in locked memory (`mlock` or `VirtualLock`) so it isn't swapped to disk: the data key, the seeds of the den's identity key and of each device key, and each den's DM seal. Go's Ed25519 caches expanded keys through weak pointers, which can't point outside the Go heap, so each signature expands a short-lived copy of the key and clears it. A DM key is opened from its sealed copy only while a message or file is sealed or opened, and cleared after. The member's identity comes from the seal as it's used, and its private keys are cleared after, but for the copies X-Wing's library keeps, which go with the garbage collector; a DM key sealed to an identity, once it opened, is kept in memory sealed with the seal, as M1.7's copies are, so it opens without X-Wing as it's used (M16.1).

## Authentication

Members log in to a den by signing a challenge with a per-den Ed25519 key, fully transparently. The password is a fallback for new devices, recovery codes are the fallback for a forgotten password, and TOTP is cut from v1.

**Den-side auth is simple because the local service holds the credentials.** The browser never sees den tokens. A Go client can send `Authorization` headers on the WebSocket upgrade, which browsers can't, and it can re-sign a challenge any time. The key does the job refresh tokens usually do, so there are no refresh tokens, rotation or reuse detection.

**Joining (invite redemption)**

1. The invite is a versioned string (dens1: plus base64url of the den URL, identity key fingerprint and single-use code) that the member pastes into a Join box. No invite links in v1, so nothing suggests a central account or server.
2. The client generates a new Ed25519 keypair for this den and pins the den fingerprint.
3. It sends the code, username, a password verifier (see below) and the public key, signed with the new key. The den stores a hash of the verifier and the key.
4. The den returns 10 recovery codes, shown once and stored hashed.

**Password verifiers**

The raw password never leaves the client. For each den, the client derives `verifier = Argon2id(password, salt = SHA-256("dens-den-password-v1" ‖ den_id ‖ username))` (t=3, m=64 MiB, p=4, 32 bytes) and sends that, and the den stores `SHA-256(verifier)`.

- A malicious or breached den learns nothing it can replay at another den, so reusing one password across dens, including the local password, is safe.
- Offline guessing against a den's database costs a full Argon2id per guess, paid by the attacker.
- The den never runs Argon2id itself, so login attempts cost it almost no CPU.
- Usernames can't change after joining, since they salt the verifier. Display names can.

**Normal login (invisible to the user)**

1. Client sends its own random nonce and requests a challenge. The den returns a 32-byte nonce for the client (60 seconds, single use; with 100,000 outstanding, the oldest goes, rather than the challenge being refused, M8) and signs both nonces and its own public address with the den identity key. The client aborts if the signature doesn't match the pinned fingerprint, and sends nothing more to an address the den didn't sign (see Den identity and moving domains).
2. Client signs `"dens-auth-v1" ‖ den_id ‖ key_id ‖ nonce`. The context string and den ID stop a signature being replayed elsewhere.
3. The den verifies the signature and issues an opaque 256-bit session token (stored as SHA-256), valid for 1 hour.
4. The WebSocket upgrade carries the token. Before expiry the client re-signs and sends an `auth.renew` frame on the open socket.
5. Revoking a key or banning a member deletes its sessions, and the den closes those sockets immediately.

**Fallbacks**

- **New device:** username and password verifier plus a new public key. The den registers the key, labels it, and tells the member's other sessions, whose chat shows that a new device signed in. From M1.7, one of the member's other devices must also approve it (see Approving new devices).
- **Forgotten password:** username, one recovery code, a new password verifier and a new public key. The code is spent, the new password replaces the old one, and every other device is signed out.
- **Change password:** from a signed-in session, with the current password or a recovery code. It signs out every other device: a device key outlives the password that added it, so anyone who had the old password may still hold one.
- **New recovery codes:** from a signed-in session, with the password, so a stolen session can't make codes to keep the account with.

A new device names the den with any invite from it, even a used one, which pins its identity, or with its address, which trusts the key the den proves there. Trusting an address is safe for the password, since a verifier made for one den is worthless at another. The page shows each den's ID, the start of its identity, so a member can compare it with someone else's.

Each den on the home page has a **Devices and password** section. It lists each registered key with its label, when it was added and when it last signed in, and signs any of them out, this one included, closing their sockets at once. It also changes the password, which signs out every other device, and makes new recovery codes. A device that was signed out says so and offers to sign in again.

**Approving new devices (M1.7)**

From M1.7, a password alone doesn't add a device: one of the member's other devices approves it, which makes their own devices a second factor with no authenticator app.

1. The new device signs in with the username and password as now. The den holds it as pending for 10 minutes and asks the member's other sessions, which show the request with the new device's label.
2. The new device and one of the member's existing devices, the one they approve from, run an exchange of one-time keys through the den: X25519 and ML-KEM-768 used together, so it stays safe if either is broken, and ML-KEM guards against traffic recorded now and decrypted later by a quantum computer. The new device starts it with its sign-in. It runs in commit-first order, three messages: a commitment to the new device's key, the existing device's key, then the new device's key. The new device's key is fixed before it sees the other's, and the other's is sent before it sees the new device's, so a den in the middle can't choose keys that make both sides' digits match; it gets one blind guess. Each device derives a 32-digit check code from the exchange and shows half of it, and keeps showing it until the other device no longer needs it: the new device until it's signed in, and the approving one, after it approves, until the member is done. The member types each device's digits into the other, so a request from someone else, whose screen the member can't see, can't be approved by accident; and if the den had swapped in keys of its own, the digits wouldn't match.
3. The existing device seals the member's DM seal with the exchange's key and sends it through the den, which carries it but can't open it. Only then does the den register the new device and start its session. The new device makes the member's identity from the seal (M16.1), so it holds what their other devices do, with nothing more to hand over.
4. Refusing a request, or letting it expire, keeps the device out. A refusal also tells the member to change their password, since someone has it. A new password, starting over, or leaving the den cancels what's waiting.

- Both checks matter: the existing device mustn't hand the seal to anyone else, and the new device mustn't take a seal from anyone else, or DMs it starts would be sealed under a key the den knows.
- The device that joins with an invite needs no approval. A member with no other device signs in with a recovery code, which needs none, and types their saved seal to read their DMs: the codes are the one way in without a device. With neither devices nor codes, the account can't be recovered.
- The den enforces approval, but DM privacy doesn't rest on it: a device the den lets in on its own gets no seal, so it can't read DMs, and the identity it would publish isn't the member's, which shows to everyone who kept theirs as a change, and in any comparison of codes (M16.1).
- Approval keeps its typed digits from M16.1, though every DM opens without any: a device the den slipped past it would take the seal, and with it every DM, and no comparison of codes would show it, since the DMs themselves were never touched. Fewer digits would do, since the den gets one blind guess, but an updated device would then show fewer than one that isn't asks for.

**Den identity and moving domains**

- Creating a den generates its Ed25519 identity key. It is stored encrypted with the den's data key and included in den backups.
- Clients store each den under its key fingerprint; its address can change.
- The den signs its public address into every challenge answer. A client that reached it at another address sends nothing more there: it runs the challenge again at the signed address, and moves there only if the den proves the same key.
- So an owner moves a den by changing its address in the den's settings, and keeps the old name pointing at the den for a while. A connected client moves as soon as the den announces the new address and proves itself there; one that was off moves at its next start, through the old name. A member whose install was off for longer enters the new address by hand, where the same check applies.
- The signed address also stops a relay. A server at an address the den left, or any other, can pass the den's answers along, but the address in them sends the client to the den itself before it sends a proof, a password verifier or a token.
- A passing check means the new server runs from the den's backup, unlocked with its local password. Normally that's the owner; it cannot rule out someone who stole both.
- A den chooses the addresses its members' clients ask, so a hostile den could turn its members into a crowd asking someone else's server, or their own machines' services on loopback. So a client checks a new address for a den at most once a minute, never follows a den from an `https` address to a plain-`http` one, nor from an address elsewhere to one on its own computer, and follows no redirect from a den, which the protocol never sends (M8).

**Why cut TOTP**

TOTP protects against a stolen password. Here the password is only used when adding a device, every such login is announced to the member's existing devices, and online guessing is rate-limited, with each guess costing the guesser an Argon2id computation. From M1.7 a new device also needs approval from one of the member's other devices, which are a second factor already. The key, which does daily logins, is unphishable and never leaves the machine. TOTP adds setup friction for little gain; it can be added later with `pquerna/otp` as an option on the password fallback only.

**Rate limits**

- Password and recovery attempts: per account and per IP, 10 and then one every 6 minutes; IPs held in memory only. A signed-in member's password checks, to change it or make new codes, count against the account's.
- Challenge requests and invite redemption: per IP.
- Messages: per member and channel, and from M8 one budget per member across every channel and DM, in messages and in bytes, so spreading messages over many channels or DMs doesn't multiply what a member may send. Message text counts against no storage limit, so this budget is what bounds how fast one member can grow the den's database.
- Unknown usernames and wrong passwords get the same error, and the check is a constant-time hash comparison either way, so neither the reply nor its timing shows which usernames exist.
- A request's body has a minute to arrive, as an upload has for each read, so a client that sends slowly can't hold the den's handlers forever (M8).
- A sound played from the soundboard counts toward the member's writes, past the wait the owner sets between their sounds (M14.3).

Limiter memory is bounded. Token buckets live in fixed-capacity LRU maps of 100,000 entries, a few MB each: when one is full, the least recently used bucket goes. IPv6 addresses are keyed by their /64 prefix so rotating addresses doesn't create new buckets. A flood from enough addresses can push a bucket out and start it over, which buys a password guesser little: a password alone doesn't add a device (M1.7), and each guess costs them an Argon2id. The per-account limit is keyed by username and refills on its own, so it never locks an account, since lockouts let an attacker lock victims out: a stream of guesses can hold up the member's own password sign-ins, but never their devices, which sign in with keys. The nonces the den hands out for sign-ins are bounded the same way: with 100,000 outstanding, the oldest goes, rather than the den refusing every sign-in (M8). A client uses its nonce within moments, so a flood can't outrun it.

## Den features

Three fixed roles, one level of channel groups, text and voice channels, and DMs. Anything finer-grained is a second den.

**Roles**

| Role | Can |
| --- | --- |
| Member | Read and post in visible channels, DM other members, join voice, upload within limits |
| Moderator | Everything a member can, plus delete members' messages, remove and ban members, create invites, manage channels and groups, see staff-only channels |
| Owner | Everything a moderator can, plus manage moderators, den settings and limits |

Channels can be marked staff-only (moderators and owner). There is no other visibility control. Staff act only on those of a lower rank, so moderators can't remove, ban or delete the messages of other moderators or the owner. Their powers stop at DMs, whose text they can't see: in a DM, only a message's author deletes it (M8).

**Handing the den on (M8).** The den's machine has the last word on who owns it. Its desktop user, with the local password, makes a one-time owner invite with `dens den owner-invite`, which lasts an hour. Whoever redeems it joins as the owner, and the owner before them becomes a moderator. It hands on the owner's role, not the den: the den stays on the machine it runs on, which keeps the last word, since it can make another owner invite at any time. Moving the den to someone else's machine is its export, restored there, after which this machine removes its copy (M10; see Backup, restore, move). An owner invite is also the way back for an owner locked out of their account, as by signing out their own device with no recovery codes left: they join again under a new username. `dens den owner-invite` refuses while hosting is off (M9.1), when nobody could redeem the invite.

A den can also start on a server, from the command line (M10). `dens den create NAME ADDRESS`, with hosting serving and the local password, creates it with no account on that computer, and prints a one-time owner invite, which whoever will own it redeems from their own Dens, as any owner invite; `dens den owner-invite` makes another once it lapses. Until it's redeemed the den has no owner, and the server's page, if anyone opens it, offers to finish setting it up. On a computer with a desktop, Create your den on the page does the same and joins as the owner there.

**Leaving, removal and bans**

- Removing a member (a kick) signs them out everywhere at once and takes them off the member list. Their record stays, so their messages keep a name, and nobody else can take their username.
- A removed member can come back only with a new invite, since invites are the only way in. They come back as themselves, history included, by giving their den password with their old username.
- A ban does the same and keeps that username out, even with a valid invite. It can also delete their recent messages, in DMs too from M8, since the den deletes a DM's messages without reading them and that's the only way to clear DM spam, and revoke the invite they joined with. The UI offers removal and banning as one action with a ban option.
- Lifting a ban takes staff of the rank of whoever placed it, or higher: a ban the owner placed, only the owner lifts (M8).
- A ban can't stop someone from joining under a new name with a new invite: the den keeps no IP addresses, and each join uses a fresh key. Invites are the real door, which is why the removal dialog offers to revoke the one they used.
- Leaving on your own works like a removal, without the kick.
- Leaving needs the den to answer. A den this computer can't reach, as one whose server is gone for good, comes off it from Manage's Leave den without the den hearing of it, as one that signed this device out or let its member go does (M10), whoever the member is there: this computer forgets its keys there and what it cached, and the account stays in the den, so if the den comes back, the member signs in again as from a new device. A den that answers is left with Leave, and the den this computer hosts goes only by moving it. Home's row doesn't offer it, since a den on a desktop that sleeps is offline as a matter of course, and nothing drops a den by itself after a while, which would need a record of when it last answered.

**Structure**

- Channel groups are one level deep and contain text and voice channels. A member folds a group by its name, and unfolds it the same way; the browser keeps which are folded, by den (M11). A folded group still shows the channel that's open and any voice channel with a call in it, and its row shows its other channels' unread state and mentions.
- Staff open a channel's settings from the ⋯ beside it in the channel list (M4.2), as members' rows in a call have one, which shows on hover or focus, and always on touch screens: its name, its group and its place in the list, whether it's staff-only, a text channel's description, a voice channel's bitrate, and deleting it. A group's ⋯ opens the group's the same way. A voice channel can't be opened, since clicking it joins its call, so its ⋯ is the way to its settings.
- With a mouse, staff drag a channel to another place in its group or into another group, and a group among the groups (M17.2), by HTML's drag and drop, as the message pane takes files. A channel dropped beside another lands above or below it, by which half of its row the pointer is over, and one dropped on a group's heading goes first in that group, or past the last of the channels in no group onto that list; a line above or below a row, or a ring round a heading, marks where it would land. The drop sends what the settings' Move up, Move down and Group send, the channel's new place in its list (`PATCH` with `position` and, where it changes, `group_id`), which the den already takes, and a group's its place among the groups; a drop where the channel or group is already sends nothing. Touch screens and keyboards keep Move up and Move down. A drag the list didn't start, from another window or with files, lands nowhere in it.
- Text channels can have a description in the same markdown subset, up to 4,000 characters. Its first line shows next to the channel name, and a click expands or collapses the rest.
- DMs are one-to-one between members of the same den, stored on the den and end-to-end encrypted (see End-to-end encryption). Closing a DM hides it until a new message arrives in it, and that follows the member across devices, like read positions.
- Group DMs are DMs of three to ten members (M16.3), listed among the DMs and end-to-end encrypted as a DM is, with a name of their members' choosing, which the den sees only sealed (see Group DMs). They count toward no channel limit; a member is in at most 100. Closing one hides it as a DM's closing does.

**Profiles**

- Each den has its own profile for each member, since identity is per den: a display name and a bio of up to 300 characters in the markdown subset. Dens can't link a member's profiles across dens.
- Bios travel only when a profile card opens, which keeps snapshots small for 500-member dens, and so do the badges a member earned (M17.5; see Events).
- A profile can carry a picture and a banner: uploads the member crops in the page to a square and to three times as wide as high, zooming and dragging the image in a frame. The page draws the framed part into a new image, which leaves the original's metadata behind, and uploads that like any other image. Until someone sets a picture, their avatar is a circle colored by their member ID with the first letter or digit of their username, so every device draws the same one without the den storing or sending anything.
- A picture or a banner can be animated (M15.1). The crop dialog frames an animated GIF or WebP by its first frame, as it frames a still, and says it keeps moving; the page then sends the file as it is, with the part framed in its pixels, to the member's local service, which makes each frame again as a GIF of the picture's size and uploads that (see Animated pictures under Files and media). An animated picture is 256 pixels square at most, and an animated banner 600 × 200, half a still's sides, since each of a GIF's frames costs its full size, and they're at most 2 MiB and 4 MiB. An animated PNG, rare among pictures, frames as a still of its first frame. A member's picture says whether it's animated (`animated`, beside its size), from what the den keeps of every image.
- Animated pictures play as Appearance's *Animations* says: Always, or On hover, which the browser keeps, as it keeps the theme; Always unless the system asks for less motion (`prefers-reduced-motion`), and On hover then. On hover plays a picture while the pointer is over its row, not over the picture alone: its message, or the one a reply quotes, its member in the list or a call, its DM in the list or a DM's header, the member's own panel; a profile card, the profile beside a DM and the member's own as they edit it play theirs while they show, since the member opened them. A touch screen has no hover, so there On hover plays pictures in profiles alone, a finger's touch, which browsers report as a hover, not counting. An animated picture that isn't playing shows its preview, the den's still of its first frame, which the picture covers while it plays, so it never blinks out as it starts. A GIF in a message stays as it is: its preview in the list, the file in the viewer.
- A member who leaves or is removed loses their pictures along with their devices, so staff removing someone also takes down what they had on their profile.
- A profile card shows its member as a card (M17.6). Their banner runs across its top to its edges, or, where they have none, a band of the theme's quiet color; their picture sits across the banner's foot, ringed in the card's own background, so the two read as one; and their display name and username go under it. Beside the display name, before the verified check, a crown marks the owner and a shield a moderator, in the icons' style, each with its tip, Owner or Moderator; a member has none. They're drawn at 80% of the text's color, darker than the icons' 60%, which falls under a graphic's 3:1 against the member list in some themes, as the `calls` check measures in every theme. The member list's rows take the same marks, their tips on a mouse's hover, since a row is a button that opens the card, which a tap gives. No role shows as a pill.
- The badges a member shows go beside their names, under Badges, wrapping into as many rows as they take; on a card narrower than about 26rem, as on a phone, under the names (see Events). Under a thin line comes the bio, as text rather than in a box like a field, and under it, quietly, Member since, or No longer in this den since, with a calendar's icon.
- One button stands out on a card: Message on another member's, and on one's own Edit profile, with Notes beside it (M17.3). The rest is under ⋯, a menu as a message's is, which touch and the keyboard reach, a sheet on the phone layout: Compare codes, or Compare codes again (M16.1); the owner's Promote to moderator and Demote to member; and Remove from den…, or Ban… for one who left, for staff of a higher rank. A card's title shows nowhere; it names the dialog for screen readers. The profile beside a DM takes the same look, with Compare codes, Add people and the DM's disappearing setting under it.

**Names**

- Usernames are unique, 2 to 32 characters of `a-z`, `0-9` and `_`, lowercased on entry and fixed at join.
- Display names are free-form Unicode up to 32 characters. Control characters and Unicode's format characters (`Cf`, the bidi overrides and zero-width ones among them) are stripped, so a name can't disguise itself or reorder the text around it, and so are the fillers that render blank (U+115F, U+1160, U+3164, U+FFA0 and U+2800); a name must keep something visible (M8).

**Messages**

- Plain text with a small markdown subset, attachments, edits, deletes and replies. No raw HTML, ever.
- The subset: bold, italic, strikethrough, inline code, code blocks, quotes, spoilers, @mentions, bare URLs, and the den's emoji by name (M15.2, see Emoji). There are no `[label](url)` links, so a link always shows where it goes, but for a link to a message, whose chip shows where it points in Dens, from what the member's own Dens knows (see Links to messages). The page parses it into Preact nodes whose text is always text, never markup, and a test fails on any script that uses `innerHTML` or another way to turn a string into markup. Every message is someone else's input, so the parser takes time in proportion to a text's length, whatever its delimiters, and the page parses each text once rather than on every render (M8). Each line's code spans are found first, in one pass, and each delimiter's closers in one pass the first time it opens; a code span takes precedence over a delimiter around it, as CommonMark's do, in the den's copy of the parser too (`CleanLinks`). At 4,000 characters the den's worst case, unclosed openers among links it rewrites, takes 4 ms where the parser before took 74; at 16,000, four times a message's limit, the page parses a hostile line in about a millisecond where it took up to 700.
- A reply quotes its original, with the author and first line, and a click jumps there. The den sends that preview with the reply, so the quote reads without the original loaded, however far back it is. Once the original is deleted, the reply says so.
- A message's actions are in a menu that opens from a ⋯ at its top right (M11): Reply, Add reaction (M15.3), Edit, Copy text, Copy link, Remove reactions, staff's (M15.3), and Delete, each where the member may. The ⋯ shows on hover or focus, and a smiley beside it, shown as it is, opens the emoji picker to react (M15.3, see Reactions); it takes the focus while hidden, so Tab reaches every message's, and the channels' ⋯ and those of members in a call follow the same rule, showing always on touch screens, which have no hover. A touch screen's messages have no ⋯: a long press opens their menu (M13.2, see The phone layout). The menu opens below the ⋯, or above it where the list has more room there; the arrow keys, Home and End move through it, and Escape, Tab or a press elsewhere closes it, Escape giving the focus back to the ⋯. Edit takes the focus to the message with the caret at its end, Reply to the composer, and copying says so beside the ⋯ for a moment. Right-clicking stays the browser's own, for copying text and links.
- Deleting asks first, in a dialog that shows the message's author and first line, and says it goes for everyone, with its files, for good. Staff deleting another member's message see whose it is, by name and username. The dialog opens on its Delete button, so Enter deletes, and Escape or Cancel gives the focus back to the ⋯.
- A message's text that runs taller than about 16 lines, such as a pasted log or a wall of blank lines, shows its top under a fade, with Show more; Show less folds it back. It's held at that height from its first paint, so folding never moves the list, and the list's bounds and pages count it at that height. Nothing limits lines beyond the 4,000 characters: the den can't read a DM, and a hostile den or sender wouldn't keep a limit, so the page folds whatever arrives.
- Each member's read position per channel lives on the den, so it follows them across devices. Channels show unread state and mention counts: a channel or DM with messages unread has its name bold at full strength and a pill at its row's left edge, which tells it from one read in any theme and on a phone, while one read is a little fainter, at 85%, which keeps it at 4.5:1 or more against the list in every theme but valentine, whose text is 4.97:1 at full (M15). Notifications are each member's to turn on, by kind (M12.3; see Notifications).
- The den counts mentions and the page highlights them by one rule, written out in `protocol.md` and tested on both sides against the same cases. Mentions in code or inside links don't count, so a link to someone's profile doesn't ping them. Staff's @everyone mentions every member who can see the channel (M15.4, see @everyone).
- Edits carry the revision they were made against, and the den refuses a stale one. Nobody's edit silently overwrites another's, whether it comes from a second device or a co-editor. The member sees the newer text, with their own draft kept to reapply.
- **Shared messages (M1.6):** when posting, the author can name up to 20 other members who may also edit the message, among those who can see the channel. Editors can't delete it, and only the author changes who may edit. It suits shared lists and plans.
- **Task checkboxes (M1.6):** lines starting with `[ ]` or `[x]` render as checkboxes in any message. Anyone who may edit the message can tick one, which the den applies as a change of its own, so two people ticking different boxes at once never lose a tick. A tick names its task's text, so one against a list that changed meanwhile is refused rather than landing on another line. Everyone else sees the boxes read-only.
- The composer's + opens a small menu above it, or a sheet on the phone layout (M13.2), for attaching files, choosing who else may edit, and in a channel, making the message a poll (M15.4, see Polls). A smiley in the box's corner opens the emoji picker, and the box completes emoji and mentions as the member types (M15.2, see Emoji). A ? beside the smiley opens a guide to writing a message: Enter, Shift+Enter and Ctrl+Enter, the markdown subset, emoji and completing, mentions and staff's @everyone, links and how they're shortened, task lines and who can tick them, shared editing, ↑ to edit the last message, replies, files and polls (M3.3).
- Enter sends a message, or saves an edit, except with the caret inside a code block, after an opening fence of three backticks with no closing one before it, where it starts a new line, as Shift+Enter always does; Ctrl+Enter sends from anywhere (M11). With a finger, ↵ always starts a line, and a button sends (M13.2, see The phone layout). The fence the caret ends counts, so Enter after an opening one starts the code, and after a closing one sends. The message box, and a message being edited, show their focus by a darker border alone, without DaisyUI's ring outside it, which doubled the border.
- Delete removes the row and its files; `secure_delete` overwrites the freed pages. A delete event tells clients to purge caches, and the den keeps the message's ID and channel, never its text, author or files, for 30 days, so a client that was away drops its copies when it comes back (M6; see What changed while away). Each member the message counted for, in a DM or a group every other member and in a channel those it mentioned, hears their read state: their unread count without it, and where it was all that lay past their read position, the position past it, since a client knows a channel's newest message only as messages come, and a DM whose last message went unread would otherwise stay unread until its next snapshot (M16.4).
- Optional den-wide retention, off by default: messages older than a period the owner sets are deleted with their files (M5; see Retention).
- Members react to messages with emoji, which show as chips under each, a DM's sealed by its members' clients (M15.3, see Reactions).
- Messages show as Appearance's *Message density* says (M17.2): Default, as they always have, or Compact, which shows each as a line of its time, its author's name and its text. The name floats at the text's start, so the text wraps under it, and a quote beside it starts past it, its bar too; a reply's quote sits above, and its files, players, polls and reactions below, from the name's start. Every message shows its name, not only a run's first, and none its author's picture, nor does the quote. Phones get it too, with the same long press. The browser keeps the choice, as it keeps Animations, and until the member makes one it's Default.
- A channel's message may be a poll, its text the question, whose members vote on its choices (M15.4, see Polls).
- A DM's or a group's messages may disappear a while after they're sent, as any of its members sets (M16.4, see Disappearing messages).
- Removal and bans revoke all of a member's keys and close their sockets at once (see Leaving, removal and bans).

**Emoji (M15.2)**

A den has emoji of its own, which staff add, beside Unicode's. Members write them by name in the markdown subset, pick them from a picker beside the composer, and complete them as they type.

- *The den's emoji.* An emoji is one of the den's named files (see The soundboard), of kind `emoji`: a name, an image, and who added it and when. Its name is 2 to 32 of `a-z`, `0-9` and `_`, lowercased on entry as a username is, so `:name:` reads the same in every message, and unique among the den's emoji; a sound may share it, each kind having names of its own. Staff, the moderators and the owner, add an emoji in Manage's Emoji section, under This den beside the Soundboard, by an image and a name, which the page suggests from the file's, and there rename or delete each, the section saying what a rename and a delete do to the messages that wrote it. Its image opens to every member, since every page draws it, where a sound's opens to staff alone. As a sound's does, the image counts toward the den's space, not its uploader's, and stays through retention, the sweep of waiting uploads and its uploader leaving.
- *Its image.* An emoji shows at 48 pixels at most, so its image is at most 128 on its longer side, for dense screens, its shape kept and never enlarged. The page draws a still one into a new PNG of that size, as it crops a picture, which leaves the original's metadata behind; an animated GIF or WebP goes to the member's local service as it is, which makes it a GIF of that size by the path animated pictures take (see Animated pictures under Files and media), with `emoji` as its picture. The den takes a still image every page draws, a PNG, JPEG, GIF or WebP, or an animated GIF, which only a member's own service makes; at most 128 pixels each way, and 256 KiB, or 512 KiB animated; an upload of the staff member's own waiting to be used.
- *The owner's limit,* in Manage's Limits, an Emoji section beside the Soundboard: how many emoji the den keeps, 50 by default, 0 to 500. It holds as an emoji is added: lowering it leaves the emoji already added. 0 turns emoji off and keeps them: messages show their names as text, the picker and completion offer none, and nobody adds one. The den keeps it in a column of its own row, `emojis`, the fourth migration (see Data across versions), and sends it in its info as `emoji_limits`, at 0 too, so a page tells emoji turned off from a den older than them, which sends none and shows no Emoji section.
- *In the text.* The text holds an emoji's name, `:custom_heart:`, as Slack's does, rather than its name and ID, `<:custom_heart:123>`, as Discord's does. A notification's text, an older page, a DM's sealed text and Copy text read it as it was written, the markdown subset gains nothing its readers can't read, and the den never parses emoji. The cost: renaming an emoji turns its uses back into text, and a name deleted and given to another emoji shows the new one in old messages.
- *The rule,* the page's alone: `:`, then 2 to 32 of `a-z`, `0-9` and `_` in any case, then `:`, the first colon not after a letter, digit or `_` and the second not before one, so a time such as 10:30:45 holds none; never in code or a link. Within a delimiter's span the span's start counts as no letter, so `_:name:_` is an emoji in italics. A name the den has shows its image, `:name:` in its tip and as its words, so copying a selection with it copies its name and an image that doesn't load reads as written, in a square box of the line's height that the image fills, contained, as it loads, so the list never moves; one it hasn't stays text. A spoiler hides the images in it until it opens. A message of nothing but emoji, ten at most, Unicode's or the den's, with only spaces between, shows them at 48 pixels: the page tells a Unicode emoji by the browser's own `\p{RGI_Emoji}`, so it needs no list for that. Emoji show wherever the markdown subset does: messages, reply quotes, bios and channel descriptions; and a DM, which lives on its den, has its den's.
- *Fetching them.* A page that showed an emoji by fetching its image would tell the den that it showed, and in a DM, which emoji a member was sent. So the member's local service keeps every image of the den's emoji in its cache, with the preview of each that moves: it fetches those the cache lacks as the list arrives in `ready`, and as an emoji is added or its image changes, and serves the page an emoji's image from the cache alone. One the cache let go of, as by its eviction or its being emptied, brings every one the cache lacks at once, so the den can't tell which showed. While emoji are off, no message shows one, and Manage's list fetches each as it shows. The cache records an emoji's files as the emoji's (`named`, beside a message's and a member's), so a deleted emoji takes them, live, and after a snapshot that no longer lists it; the cache's format is 3 for it, so a cache of format 2 starts again from empty (see The client's cache). The install's own den isn't cached, its files being on its disk. The owner's count bounds what this takes: at most 500 images of at most 512 KiB.
- *Unicode's emoji.* The picker lists them, and the den checks reactions against them (M15.3), from one list. `scripts/emoji.sh` runs a generator, `scripts/emoji/`, over Emoji 18.0's `emoji-test.txt` and CLDR 49's English annotations of single emoji and of sequences, all three pinned in `scripts/vendor.sh` by SHA-256. It writes, for the page, `internal/ui/assets/emoji.json`: the groups, and each fully qualified emoji with its name, its group, CLDR's keywords beyond its name's words, and where it takes a skin tone its form in each of the five, the forms whose name gives one tone, so an emoji of two people gets the one where both have it; and for `denproto`, `emojilist.go`, every fully qualified emoji, skin tones and all, the forms of two different tones among them, which `denproto.IsEmoji` asks. Both are committed, one emoji a line, and CI writes them again and fails on a difference, as it does for RNNoise's module; a Go test checks the two lists agree. Emoji draw in the system's font, as the rest of the text does, so no image set ships; where nothing but emoji shows, in the picker, completion and a message of emoji alone, the system's color emoji font comes first, since some text fonts, as DejaVu Sans, draw the older emoji in black and white, which Chromium takes over a color font named after them. The list stops at the newest Emoji version, which each line of `emoji-test.txt` names, that Windows 11 and Noto Color Emoji both draw: 16.0, which Windows 11 has drawn since 24H2, while 17.0 reaches it only as an update rolls out; a phone too old for one shows a box. The page fetches its list, 138 KB, once its picker or a completion first needs it, from the hashed address the page's shell names (`data-emoji`), rather than carrying it in its script. Unicode's license is in the notices.
- *The picker.* A smiley in the composer's box, beside the guide's ?, drawn as the page's icons are, opens it above the box, and on the phone layout as a sheet from the screen's foot, as the page's menus are there, its search and its buttons staying at the sheet's top as the emoji scroll (see The phone layout). At its top are a search, over names and keywords, every word starting a word of either, best first: the name exactly, the name's start, its words', then keywords; a button for each section, which scrolls to it; and the skin tone, ✋ in it, which the browser keeps, for those that take one. Below, a block of the member's most used emoji, up to four rows, then the den's, by name, then Unicode's, by group, nine to a row, the name of the one under the pointer or the focus at the foot. A pick goes in at the caret, or in place of what's selected, and the picker closes, but with Shift: a Unicode emoji as itself, in the member's skin tone where it takes one, so the text holds the emoji, and a den's as `:name:`, which the box, being plain text, shows by name. A mouse's picker starts in its search, where Enter picks the first found; a finger's doesn't, since its keyboard would cover the emoji, and the box takes the focus back after a pick. Escape or a press elsewhere closes it.
- *Most used.* The browser keeps a score for each emoji the member uses: picked in the picker, put in by completing, sent in a message, a phone keyboard's among them, or added as a reaction (M15.3). Each use adds one, once: an emoji the picker or completion put in counts as it goes in, and the message it went with counts only the emoji it holds beyond those. A Unicode emoji counts without its skin tone, which the member's choice puts back, and one the list doesn't have counts as nothing; a den's counts by its ID, in its den alone, keeping its score through a rename, and its score goes once the den no longer has it. Every score halves in two weeks, so a favorite stays at the top while one used once fades; 300 are kept, the lowest dropping first. The picker's first block and the quick reactions (M15.3) both go by it, the quick reactions filled out with 👍 ❤️ 😂 😮 😢 🎉 while the member has used fewer.
- *Completing.* In a channel or a DM, typing `:` and two characters where an emoji may start, by the rule's first colon, lists above the box the den's emoji whose names match, then Unicode's, by the picker's search, `_` matching a space, eight at most. In a channel, typing `@` where a mention may start, by the mention rule, lists the members who can see it but the member, by a username starting with what follows, then by a word of the display name. Nothing completes in code or a link. The first is marked; ↑ and ↓ move through the list, Enter or Tab puts the marked one in, with a space after it, and Escape closes it until the text changes; a click or a tap puts one in, keeping the box's focus, so a finger's keyboard stays up.
- *Animated emoji* play as Animations says (see Profiles): always, or on hover over their message, and in the picker under the pointer; Manage's list plays each under the pointer, and a bio, in a profile, plays them while it shows. One that isn't playing shows its preview, the den's still of its first frame, which the image covers while it plays.

**Reactions (M15.3)**

A reaction is a member's emoji on a message: one of Unicode's, from the list the picker shows (see Emoji), or one of the den's. A message holds at most 20 different emoji. Reactions notify nobody, and each counts toward its member's other writes.

- *In a channel.* The den keeps a row for each, a member's emoji on a message, checked against Unicode's list (`denproto.IsEmoji`), or as one of the den's emoji by its ID while emoji are on, so a reaction carries no text: no bidi characters, no words spelled in letters. A message holds at most 10,000 reactions in all besides, so one with them stays well within a frame and a page of history. History pages carry a message's reactions, each emoji with the members who reacted with it, in the order each emoji came and each member reacted; live, `reaction.added` and `reaction.removed` each carry one member's one emoji, which a page applies to that emoji's set, so applying one twice changes nothing, where resending a message's whole set on every tap would spend the den's upload. The same reaction again, or one taken back that isn't there, changes nothing and tells nobody. The rows go with their message, deleted or past the den's retention, and with one of the den's emoji as staff delete it, whose `emoji.deleted` has the pages drop its chips. Reactions wait their turn with edits on the den, so an edit's message carries its reactions as they were, and the events go out in the order the changes were made.
- *In a DM.* The den can't read a DM, and reactions in the clear would tell it how one member answered the other, so each member's reactions on a DM's message are one set, which their client seals with the DM key, as it seals the DM's messages: bound to the den, the DM, the message, the member, the key's ID and the set's revision, so the den can't move it to another message, member or DM, or pass an old set off as the newest; and padded to one length, so its size doesn't say how many it holds. It names the den's emoji by name, as a DM's text writes them. The den keeps it in a row for each message and member, replaced whole against the revision it was made against, as an edit is, so a member's two devices don't drop each other's reactions: a set made against another revision is refused as a conflict that carries the set as it is, and the client tries again on it. It sends the set to both members as `dm.reactions`, and in history with its message. A client opening one keeps only Unicode's emoji from the list and names the den's emoji may have, each once, at most 20, and drops anything else; one it can't open shows none. The den sees that a member's reactions changed, and when, never what they are; a set stays once made, empty or not, so it doesn't say that either. Nothing more changes once the other member has left.
- *What changed while away.* A reaction records its message in the change log, as an edit does, though its revision stays, so an install that was away fetches it again with its reactions, and an edit under way meets no conflict (see What changed while away).
- *Staff* take one emoji's reactions off a channel's message, those of the members they outrank, as their other powers reach, from the message's menu, Remove reactions, which lists its emoji and how many of each they may take (`reaction.cleared`, naming whose went). A DM's are its members' alone. A ban that deletes a member's recent messages takes the reactions they added in that time too, in channels, each told as taken back; a DM's set stays, since the den can't tell one reaction in it from another.
- *On the page.* Under a message, after its files and covers, its reactions show as chips, each emoji with its count, marked where the member reacted; a click adds the member's or takes it back, and a tip says who: You first, then names, up to 15, then how many more, on hover or focus. A den's emoji shows as its image while emoji are on, and as `:name:` while they're off, as a message shows it; one that a DM's set holds by a name the den no longer has shows as that name. A + at the row's end, a smiley beside the message's ⋯, shown as the ⋯ is, and the ⋯ menu's Add reaction open the picker beside them, above where there's room and below otherwise, or as a sheet on the phone layout; a pick reacts, and an emoji added counts toward the member's most used (see Emoji). On a touch screen the long press's sheet opens with a row of quick reactions at its top: the member's six most used, filled out with 👍 ❤️ 😂 😮 😢 🎉 while they've used fewer, the den's left out while emoji are off, each marked where they reacted, and a + for the picker. A tap on a chip adds or takes back, and a long press on it says who, which takes the place of the message's own long press. A message's first reaction gives it a row, and more may wrap it onto another; above what the member reads, neither moves the view (see Message list). An older page shows no reactions; a den older than reactions, which came with its emoji, sends no `emoji_limits`, and the page offers none there.

**Polls (M15.4)**

A channel's message may be a poll, whose members vote on its choices. Its text is the question, which a poll must have, so notifications, reply quotes and older pages read it.

- *Making one.* The composer's + gains Poll in a channel, which opens the poll's editor above the box, where a reply and files stand, the box holding the question, which its placeholder asks for: 2 to 10 choices, each up to 80 characters of plain text, cleaned as display names are, though each of Unicode's emoji stays whole, its joiners and tags kept, and no two alike in any case; whether a member may pick more than one; how long it runs, 1 hour, 4 or 8 hours, 1 day by default, 3 days, or 1 or 2 weeks, which the den takes as any whole number of hours from 1 to 336; and its two rules, Totals show, then Anonymous voting, which asks who voted for what as anonymity: Yes, never shown; During the poll, shown once it ends; or No, shown with the totals. Cancel takes the poll away. Polls go in channels alone, since a DM's would have its votes sealed from the den that counts them, for a poll of two; and without files or other editors, since neither the message nor its question changes once it's posted. The den keeps a poll in a row beside its message, its choices sealed with the data key as the text is, and each vote, a choice a member picked, in a row of its own, the sixth migration (see Data across versions).
- *Who voted for what:* Never, the default, anonymous voting's Yes; Once it ends, During the poll; or Always, No. It's fixed as the poll is posted, and shown on it, since a voter decides with it in mind, and an author mustn't reveal votes cast under Never. The den holds to it: it sends who voted for what to nobody under Never, to everyone at the end under Once it ends, and with the totals under Always. Never keeps it from members, not from the den, which keeps who voted for what to count each member once and let them change their vote; the poll's tip says so.
- *When the totals show:* As votes come in, the default; or After voting. As votes come in shows everyone the totals as they change, and a member changes or takes back their vote until the poll ends. After voting shows a member the totals once they've voted, and makes their vote final: changing it after seeing them would let a member move to the winning side. The poll says so before they vote. The den holds to this too: until the poll ends it sends the totals only to those who voted, and to the rest only how many have. So a poll as the den sends it, in history, in what changed while away and in its events, is as its member may see it, their own votes among it.
- *Voting.* The den checks that the poll is open, the choices are its own, one of them unless several are allowed, and under After voting that the member hasn't voted; the same vote again changes nothing. A vote counts toward the member's other writes. The member's own sessions hear their vote at once, with the poll as they now see it; everyone else hears the totals in batches every second and a half, as presence is batched, so a busy poll sends an event a batch rather than one a vote, each batch to those who may see it: under After voting, an audience of the poll's voters among those who see its channel, beside the hub's audiences of one member and of two, and the rest of the channel's, who hear how many voted. A vote changes its message without changing its revision, as a reaction does: it records the message in the change log (see What changed while away), and waits its turn with edits and reactions, so its events go out in order with the batches and the end.
- *Ending.* The den closes a poll at its end, by a timer for the next to end, set as hosting starts and as polls are posted and ended, and sends everyone who sees the channel the final totals, with who voted for what where the poll shows them then; a poll past its end reads as ended before the timer's turn comes, and takes no vote. Its author ends it early, which moves its end to then. A poll can't be edited once posted, neither its question nor its choices, nor a task in its question ticked, so no vote stands under a question it wasn't cast for. It goes with its message, deleted or past the den's retention; a member's votes stay when they leave, as their messages do.
- *On the page.* Under its question, the poll shows each choice, with a bar of its share of those who voted once the member may see the totals, and their own marked; how many voted, and how long it has left, or that it ended; and its rules in a few words, with a tip of a sentence or two: Anonymous, Votes show when it ends or Votes show, and with After voting, Results after you vote, votes final. While the member may vote, each choice is a radio button, or a check box where several may be picked, whose row is its label, and Vote votes; once they've voted as votes come in, Change vote and Remove vote; and its author ends it early with End now, which asks once more. Where the poll shows them, a choice's voters show on hover or focus over its count, and by a tap on a touch screen, You first. The page applies the den's events and the answers to the member's own votes as they come, the newest last, an ended poll keeping its final totals, and holds the view where it is as a poll above it changes height (see Message list).
- *Older ones.* An older page shows the question alone. A den older than polls is older than emoji, which came with them, and sends no `emoji_limits`, so the page offers Poll only where it has them.

**@everyone (M15.4)**

- Staff, the moderators and the owner, send `@everyone` in a channel, where the mention rule finds it (see Messages). It counts as a mention of each member who can see the channel but the author, a row each as for a mention by name, so the unread mention counts, Home's and older pages' among them, need nothing new; a den of 500 members writes 500 rows for it, which go with the message. The den marks the message (`everyone`), so the page highlights the @everyone for every reader, as it highlights a mention of the reader, and the member's service tells it from a mention by name.
- It counts while the text holds it and staff put it there: an edit that keeps it keeps the mark, a co-editor who isn't staff takes it out of a shared message but can't put it in, and an edit recounts its rows, as for a name, mentioning neither the author nor the one who edits. Notifications come as a message arrives, so an edit that adds it notifies nobody, as for any mention.
- From anyone else it's a mention by name, of a member named `everyone`: the name is a valid username, so a den may have one from before, who keeps it, and new members can't take it, the den answering as for a name that's taken.
- Completing `@` offers it first to staff in a channel, and the writing guide says who sends it and how to silence it.
- *Silencing it.* The Notifications card has a switch under Mentions, @everyone, on by default, which applies while mentions are on: off, an @everyone notifies nothing and plays no sound, and turning it off takes back what it showed, though an @everyone that names the member too notifies as their mention. Muting the den or the channel silences it, as it silences mentions. Either way it counts toward the channel's mentions, since leaving it out would take a second count in read states. With the text hidden, its notification says it mentioned everyone.

**Events (M17.4)**

A den's events: a game night, a watch party, a meeting, each at a time and in a place, which members mark Interested to be told as it starts.

- *The Events row,* at the top of the Den tab's list, opens the den's events in the channel's place (`/den/<den>/events`), on the phone layout a pane of its own as a channel's is: those live first, then the rest by their start, each a card. The row says Live while one runs, else how many are to come; an ended event leaves the list. The den's name still opens Manage, since events are every member's and Manage mostly staff's.
- *An event* has a name, up to 100 characters, cleaned as a poll's choice is, each of Unicode's emoji kept whole; a description, up to 2,000 characters in the markdown subset; an optional banner, cropped three times as wide as high and checked as a profile's banner is, an upload of whoever set it, which a newer one, or canceling the event, deletes; a start, in the member's own time zone in the form, up to a year ahead, or this minute, which starts it at once; a length from 5 minutes to a week, from a list; and a place: one of the den's voice channels, or a line of text, up to 100 characters. The den keeps what members wrote of it, its name, description and place's text, sealed with the data key, as a message's text, in a row of its own, with its banner, voice channel, maker, times and state; who's interested is a row each; and the den's limit for members' events is a column of its own, the twelfth migration (see Data across versions). An event that ended stays, out of the list, with its banner, for its cards and its badge (M17.5), keeping whether its voice channel was staff's alone once the channel goes, so it stays where it was seen.
- *Who makes them.* Staff, and members as far as the owner lets each, in Manage's Limits: how many events that haven't ended each member may have, 0 by default, which leaves events to staff, up to 10; lowering it leaves the events made. A den holds 50 that haven't ended, to come and live, so the list, which every snapshot carries, stays bounded. A member changes and cancels their own, and staff those of members they outrank, by the rule calls and messages use; the maker isn't marked Interested by making one. An event in a staff-only voice channel is staff's alone, its banner too, and follows its channel: as the channel turns staff-only or back, or staff move an event into or out of one, members who aren't staff start over from a fresh snapshot, as a channel's visibility has them do, and the count of those interested holds only those who see it. Deleting a voice channel cancels its events to come and ends its live one. A member who leaves or is removed takes their events to come, banners and all, and their marks on the rest; their live events go on to their end.
- *Live,* from its start to its end. Its maker or staff end it early with End now, or move its end from the form, whose start then stays as it is, never before now and within its length's limits. One that started can't be canceled, only ended. The den's timer, which ends polls and deletes disappearing messages, starts and ends events, set for the next start or end as events are made, moved, canceled and ended, and as hosting starts, when it catches up: an event that passed whole while the den was stopped ends without starting, and one partway through starts then. From M17.5 it gives badges too, as each member's stretch in a live event's call comes due.
- *Interested.* Each member's mark, on the card, which counts toward their other writes. Everyone who sees the event sees how many, the card's count, and their own mark; nobody sees who. The den keeps who's interested to tell them as it starts, and forgets it as the event ends. Marking Interested asks the browser to show notifications, where it hasn't asked and events notify, since a browser asks only in answer to a click.
- *Starting.* As an event starts, the den sends `event.started` to its interested members, an audience fixed as it's made, numbered like other events so a socket that resumes soon after still hears it from the ring, and `event.updated`, live, to the rest who see it. The member's Dens notifies them under Events (see Notifications), and logs the event's ID, which says a missed notification wasn't the den's; a Dens that wasn't connected hears nothing later, its next snapshot showing the event live. The den pushes the start rather than each Dens timing it, since Go's timers stop while a phone sleeps; on Android it comes as a message's notification does, and the shell's tap opens the event's path (see android.md).
- *On the page.* A card shows the banner, the name and Live while it runs; when, in the member's time zone, as Today, Tomorrow or the day, its start's and end's times, or while live, until when; where, a voice channel's name, which a live event's Join joins, a primary button so it stands out (M17.6), with the staff-only mark where its channel is staff's, or the line of text; the description; who made it; Interested, pressed while marked, and how many are; and to whoever may change it, Edit, and Cancel… or End now, each asking once more. + Event, for a member who may make one, opens the form, a sheet on the phone layout. A notification's click or tap opens the events at the event, lit for a moment. The page applies the den's events to its view as they come, and each Dens keeps the events with the den's state, so they show while the den is away, but for those past their end by the clock, which the den would have ended.
- *Older ones.* An older Dens ignores the events, their routes and `event_limits`; a den older than events sends no `event_limits`, so the page offers no Events there.

From M17.5 an event's link shows its card where it's posted, and an event in a voice channel can carry a badge, which members earn by being in its call:

- *Links and cards.* An event's card in the pane has Copy link, which copies the den's address with the den's ID and the event's in its fragment, `#e/<den ID>/<event>`, as a message's link has `#m/` (see Links to messages). In a message, such a link shows as a chip naming the event, after its den's name when it's another den's, which opens it in its den's events, and under the message, after its files, as the event's card: its banner, its name, which opens it too, Live or Ended, when, in the member's time zone, where, its badge, and until it ends how many are interested, with Interested, which marks it as the pane's does. A message shows three cards at most, none for a link in code or a spoiler, and a reply's quote and a preview show the address. The card of one of the open den's events to come comes from its view; any other its Dens answers from the events it keeps, so the den hears of an event to come only as it always does, and asks the den only for the rest, by `GET /api/events/{id}`, an ended one among them, which says it ended and that nobody's interested any more. Once the event changes, the card reads it again. A link to an event the member can't see, or that's gone, which look alike, gets no card, and its chip says so at a click; one in a den they haven't joined names its host, as a message's does. A card in a DM asks the den for an event the member's Dens doesn't keep, as any other does, which tells the den which ended event they looked at, and nothing of the DM.
- *Badges.* Staff attach one to an event in a voice channel, making or editing it: a square picture, cropped as a profile picture is and drawn at an emoji's size, 128 pixels, or an animated GIF the member's local service makes from the crop as it makes an animated emoji, checked as an emoji's image is; and a title of up to 40 characters, cleaned as the event's name is. Members who aren't staff can't, the event's maker among them. The badge shows on the event's cards, with its title. Staff change its title or picture, or take it off, until the event ends, after which it stays as it was; an event with a badge stays in a voice channel, so moving it elsewhere takes the badge off, and canceling it, or its going as its maker leaves or its channel is deleted, takes the badge too. The picture is one of the den's named files, of kind `badge`, as an emoji's is, so it stays the den's whoever added it, opening to everyone who sees the event and counting toward nobody's space; its name is its file's ID, since titles repeat, and the title is sealed with the data key, as the event's own text is, in a row of the badge's own.
- *Earning one.* A member in the event's call for a minute at a stretch while it's live earns its badge, once, their call held while their connection is away or not. The den times each stretch from when the member's call joined the channel, which the call registry holds in memory as long as the call lasts, or from the event's start for one there before; it writes only the award, the badge, the member and when, in a row of its own, so its database holds nothing of who was in the call. The den's timer gives each badge as its stretch comes due, a stretch that comes due only after the event's end earning nothing, and the den tells nobody. A development instance's den takes a stretch of three seconds, for the `events` check. Badges and their awards are the thirteenth migration, with an event's staff-only mark (see Data across versions).
- *Showing them.* A member's profile card shows the badges they earned, latest first, their pictures under Badges, each with a tip: its title, its event's name and the event's day, in the member's time zone. They travel with the profile card, as its bio does, never in `ready` or a member's record, and only those whose events the viewer sees, so a staff-only event's badge shows to staff alone, even once its channel is gone; a card opened while the den is away shows none. An animated badge plays as an emoji does. Each member shows or hides each of theirs (M17.6): Edit profile lists every badge they earned, each with an eye at its top right, open while it shows and struck while it's hidden, which a click or a tap turns at once, as a picture changes; their card then shows only those shown, to everyone, the member included. It keeps a card tidy and nothing more, which the guide says: the den still knows who earned what. The den keeps the mark on the award (`hidden`, the fourteenth migration), leaves a hidden badge out of everyone's copy of the profile but the member's own, which marks it for Edit profile, and tells nobody; a badge starts shown. A badge's tip on an event's card says how it's earned: "Join during the event for a minute to earn this badge!"

**Notifications (M12.3)**

A member is notified of a DM, and of a message that mentions them, once they turn that kind on; until then no message notifies them, on any form. An event they marked Interested notifies them as it starts unless they turn events off (M17.4), since marking it asks for that. The service decides, not the page, since a page may be hidden, frozen by its browser, or closed.

- *What notifies.* A message another member sends, as it arrives: in a DM with DMs on, or in a den's channel with mentions on, where it mentions the member by the rule the den counts mentions by (see Messages), or is staff's @everyone, with its switch on (M15.4, see @everyone). Not in a den or channel the member muted, and not while a page shows that channel on screen. A message notifies once, though the den may send it again after a dropped connection, and not once the member read it. What arrives while the install can't reach the den shows as unread when it's back, not as notifications.
- *One a channel.* A channel's newer message replaces its notification where it shows, so a busy DM is one notification, with its newest message, rather than a pile, and a member flooding a channel floods nobody's screen.
- *What it says.* Who sent it, and for a mention, where; the den's name; and the start of the message, 120 characters at most, as plain text (`denproto.Plain`): the markdown subset's marks go, each spoiler, found as the page's renderer finds it, shows as "(spoiler)", and control and format characters go, as from names. With the text hidden, it says only that someone sent a message, or a mention. A DM's text is opened on this computer, as for the page. Nothing about a notification is logged but its den's, channel's and message's IDs.
- *Where it shows,* in one place: a page the browser lets notify, the one on screen most lately, through the browser's notifications; with none, the desktop's helper, as the desktop's own (see Global shortcuts); and on Android, the app's shell (see android.md). Each page tells the service whether it's on screen and whether the browser lets it notify, beside the channel it shows in each den, which it told the service for typing already. On Android a page counts as on screen only while the shell says the app is in front, since the shell holds it as shown through a call (M12.2).
- *Taken back.* Reading the channel takes its notification back wherever it shows: the member's read position on the den passing it, from any of their devices, or their own message there. So does the message being deleted or passing the den's retention, the channel being deleted, or the den being left; and muting a den or channel, or turning a kind off, takes back what it showed.
- *An event's start* (M17.4) notifies once, where a message's would show, under a key of its own (`<den>/event-<id>`): titled with the event's name, saying it's live and where, a voice channel's name or, unless the member hides the text, the place its maker wrote, and opening the den's events at it (`/den/<den>/events#event=<id>`). Nothing on screen keeps it from showing, since the event may be off the page's screen. Muting the den silences it, as does turning Events off; reading channels doesn't take it back, and its end does, or its den being left.
- *A click or a tap* opens the page at the message: a browser's notification brings its tab forward and opens the channel there; the helper's opens the page in the default browser, paired as `dens open` pairs it, with the message in the fragment beside the token, which the page takes from the address bar; the shell's opens it on the page that shows, by a fragment that says where, so a call there goes on, and otherwise loads the page at it (M13.1). A Windows toast's click opens its address by itself, which works from the Action Center after the helper has gone, but carries no token, since a token lasts two minutes and the address would sit in the Action Center: the browser opens it paired only if it was already.
- *The settings* are the service's, in its configuration, so every browser paired with the instance shares them, as it shares the voice keys: DMs and mentions each on or off, and staff's @everyone with mentions (M15.4), Events, on unless turned off (M17.4), kept as `eventsOff` so settings from before read as on, the text shown or hidden, and what the member muted, by den and by channel, DMs included, which goes with a den the install leaves and with a channel the den deletes. The settings' Notifications & sounds tab turns the kinds on, which asks the browser to let the page notify where it hasn't asked, and says where they show, by what the browser, the helper and Android allow. Once a kind is on, Events among them, so by default, the bell beside a den's name mutes the den, and the bell in a channel's or DM's header mutes it; the list marks a muted channel or DM with a struck bell.
- *The page's stream* carries the status of the notifications, `notifications`: the settings, the sounds' among them, the sounds the member replaced with files of their own (M14.2), and on Android, whether Android lets the app post them; each notification the page shows, `notify`, and what it takes back, `unnotify`; and a message whose sound the page plays alone, `sound` (M14.2). The page sends `page`, whether it's on screen and may notify, as either changes and again after a reconnect.

**Sounds (M14.2)**

The page plays seven sounds, each one of Kenney's Interface Sounds, which are CC0: CC0 asks no credit, and the notices credit Kenney all the same, with CC0's text. A member turns each off, sets its volume, or replaces it with a file of their own.

| Sound | Plays when | Kenney's file | Default volume |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Message | A DM or a mention arrives, where the service places its sound | Drop 4 | 60% |
| Joined | A member joins the call, the member too | Confirmation 1 | 35% |
| Left | A member leaves the call, the member too, however the call ends for them | Question 2 | 30% |
| Share started | A member of the call starts sharing their screen, the member too | Maximize 6 | 50% |
| Share ended | A share in the call ends, but by its sharer leaving | Minimize 6 | 40% |
| Microphone on | The member's microphone opens | Drop 3 | 65% |
| Microphone off | The member's microphone closes | Bong 1 | 85% |

- *The files* are the page's assets (`assets/sounds/<name>.ogg`, Ogg Vorbis, 60 KB together), served by the client listener as its others are, at the hashed addresses the page's shell names in `data-sounds`. The default volumes even the sounds out by their measured loudness, each over the 400 ms that holds it, to about -24 LUFS, under a call's voices; a member's own set volume takes a default's place.
- *The call's* follow the call: the den's word on who is in it and who shares, and the member's microphone, which is open while neither they nor staff muted them, and by push to talk while they hold it. The member coming in, once the den has them in the call, plays Joined, and going out Left, without the microphone's sounds; others coming and going play them too, as do shares starting and ending, the member's own among them, but a sharer leaving takes the share with them, which plays Left alone. A change plays each sound once, however many members it takes in, and moving to another call plays its Joined alone. The microphone's sounds follow it whatever moves it: the keys, the helper, the call's button or controls, deafening, which mutes, and staff muting the member; a change that leaves it as it was, as deafening while muted, or push to talk held while muted, plays nothing, and others' mutes play nothing. A deafened member hears none of the call's sounds, their own joining and leaving among them, but their microphone's, which answer what they did.
- *Where they play:* as the call's voices do. In a browser, from an element of each sound's, on the speaker the member chose (`setSinkId`), from the start each time. Where the page may play without a click, as in the Android app, through the call's context once there's one, which Android routes as the call's, to the earpiece, the loudspeaker or Bluetooth (M12.2); each sound is decoded for the context as it starts, so the first plays without waiting. The member's own Left plays once the call's audio has gone with its context, from an element, and outside a call the message's plays from one too. A browser plays a page's sound only once the member has clicked in it, so a tab untouched since it opened plays none, as it plays no call.
- *The message's* is placed by the service, beside its notification (see Notifications), so a message makes one sound: a DM, or a message that mentions the member, in a den and channel they haven't muted and that no page shows on screen, of a kind they turned on, as for its notification: a kind's switch is whether such a message reaches the member at all, sound and notification. A page that shows its notification through the browser plays the sound with it, and asks the browser to show it silently (`silent`, which Chromium honors, and Firefox from 132); a notification the helper or Android's shell shows has the desktop's or Android's own sound, and the page plays none; and with nothing to show it, as when the browser won't let the page notify and no helper runs, the sound goes alone (`sound` on the page's stream) to the page on screen most lately, and with no page open, nothing plays. On Android the page plays a sound alone only while the app is in front: behind, the shell may hold the page as shown through a call, or it may be frozen, and play what it missed as it thaws. A message sounds once, as it notifies once, though the den may send it again after a dropped connection. Android's notification sound stays Android's: it's set per notification channel in Android's settings and fixed when Android makes the channel, so making Kenney's its default would take new channels and drop what members set on the old; the settings say where to change it.
- *The settings,* in Notifications & sounds, beside the Notifications card: a Sounds card, its sounds grouped Messages and Calls, each with its switch, ▶ to hear it, its volume, 0 to 100% in steps of 5, which plays it once set, and Replace…, or once replaced, Reset. The service keeps each switch and volume in its configuration beside the notifications' settings (`types.Notifications.Sounds`), and only what the member changed: a sound back on at its default leaves them, so a later default reaches it. Every page hears them with the notifications' status, so every page paired with the install shares them.
- *A member's own sound* is a file of theirs in a sound's place: at most 1 MiB, of a type `internal/media` names audio, which the service checks, and decoding in the browser, for at most 10 seconds, which the page checks before sending it, as that takes decoding it. The service keeps it in the client's `sounds` table, by the sound's name, sealed with the data key and bound to that name, with when it was set, which the status names as its version (`own`) and the page asks for it by. It stays on the install: the client router alone serves it, at `/api/sounds/<name>`, as the audio it is, sandboxed and never cached, as a den's files are, takes it by `POST` with its bytes, and drops it by `DELETE`, Reset, writes passing the `Origin` check as every write does. A backup holds it; a den's export, which holds the den alone, doesn't. The table is the first migration since v1.0.0 (see Data across versions).

**Retention (M5)**

An owner can have the den forget old conversations: with retention on, every message older than a period they set is deleted, with its files. A new den has it off.

- The period is a number of days, from 1 to 3,650, set in the den's settings beside the upload limits. Before a change takes effect, the settings say how many messages it deletes at once, and how much space their files take.
- It covers channels and DMs alike. The den can't read a DM, but it knows when each message was sent, which is all retention needs.
- A message's age counts from when it was sent. Edits don't extend it, and neither do replies: a reply to a message that has passed the period says the original is gone, as with any deleted message.
- The den deletes messages within the hour after they pass the period, the way it deletes anything: rows removed with `secure_delete` on, and files taken off the disk. It deletes the oldest first, in the order they were sent, a few hundred per transaction, so a first pass through years of history never holds the database for long. A change of period starts a pass at once, and so does starting the den, so one restored from a backup deletes what passed the period meanwhile.
- Clients drop what passed the period too. After each pass the den sends one event, not a delete for every message, naming the last message it deleted: every message up to it, in every channel, is gone. Each client drops the messages it holds up to that one, and the files on them, from memory and from its cache (M6). `ready` says how far retention has reached, so a client that was away drops them as it comes back. A channel whose last message went holds none, so nothing in it is unread.
- Members see the period on the den's card on the home page, and a channel's history starts with a line saying how long messages are kept, where it would otherwise say the channel starts.
- Profiles, channel descriptions and uploads waiting to be sent aren't messages, and retention leaves them alone. A backup keeps what the den held when it was made, and outlives the period until it's deleted; the docs tell owners so.
- A DM's or a group's message that disappears goes at its own time (M16.4, see Disappearing messages), and retention takes it first where the period passes sooner.

**Disappearing messages (M16.4)**

Any member of a DM or a group sets its messages to disappear 1 hour, 1 day, 1 week or 4 weeks after they're sent, or Off, which a new one starts at. What members ask of disappearing messages is that they're gone, from the den and every device, so that's what Dens does: they stay sealed under the DM's or the group's key, as every other message there is.

- *The setting.* Beside the DM, under its other member's profile, or beside the group, under its members: Disappearing messages, a list of the five, which applies to the messages sent after it. A pick asks first, since it applies to everyone in the conversation, in a dialog that says what the new time does and links to the guide. A line in the conversation, at the time it was set, says who last set it, on which day, and to what: "On Nov 5, 2026, Bob set messages to disappear 1 day after they're sent", or turned them off; the day stays said once the messages around it are gone. The header shows the time in a few letters beside the lock, 1d, which opens what's beside it. A DM whose other member left takes none, as it takes no message.
- *The den knows it,* as it must to delete by it: it keeps the time in the clear, with who set it and when, and a time counts from sending, which it knows and every member sees alike. Its word on who set a group's can't be checked as a group's changes can, but it deletes the messages either way. A development instance's den takes any time of seconds up to four weeks, for the browser checks.
- *Each message* carries when it goes (`expires_at`), its channel's time as it was sent added to its sending, which an edit doesn't extend. Its row shows a timer beside its time, whose tip says when, and on a touch screen its sheet says it.
- *Deleting.* The den deletes each as its time comes, as its author's delete would: its row, with its files and reactions, `secure_delete` overwriting them, `message.deleted` to its members, and the change log, so an install that was away drops it as it comes back. It runs on the den's timer, the one that ends polls, set for whatever comes due next, sooner as a message under a time is sent, and again as the den starts, so one restored from a backup deletes what passed meanwhile; a pass deletes 500 a transaction. A reply to one says it's gone, as for a deleted one.
- *Unread.* A message counts toward its DM's or group's unread count as any does. As one goes, each member it counted for hears their read state, as for any delete (see Messages): their count without it, and where what went was all that lay past their read position, the position past it, so a DM whose last messages went unread isn't left unread, while one still holding a message unread from before a shorter time was set stays so.
- *Each member's Dens* drops what it holds at its time, by the computer's clock, whether the den can be reached or not: the cache lets it go with its files and the replies' quotes of it, and keeps out a copy past its time that a page or an event brings; its pages drop it and its notification is taken back; history leaves it out, and one that arrives past its time notifies nobody. A page drops it as its time passes by its own timer too, so a den offline shows none of it.
- *What it doesn't do.* A den changed to keep copies would keep their sealed keys too, which anyone who later held a member's DM recovery key, or a device of theirs, could open. Forward secrecy for them would take keys each device makes for itself, changed every day, a second way of sealing keys beside identities, which loose-ideas.md keeps; the privacy model already leaves compromised devices and targeted investigations outside what Dens protects against. Anyone in the conversation can copy a message before it goes, which the setting says.
- *Older ones.* The den's info says it deletes them (`disappearing`), and the page offers the setting only there. An older Dens in a DM or a group with a time shows its messages without saying they go, and drops them as the den deletes them, by `message.deleted` while connected and the change log after, but not while it can't reach the den.

**Compact links (M3)**

When a message is sent or edited, or a channel's description or a member's bio is set, the den rewrites the links in it. Links to Reddit, YouTube and X, and Amazon's product pages, become one short form, which keeps only what identifies the post, video or product, and every other link loses its tracking parameters. In a private DM the sending service rewrites the text before sealing it, since the den can't read it. A rewritten link stays in the text as an ordinary URL, so everything that reads text reads it as before: the markdown subset, mentions, tasks, reply previews, edits and sealed DMs.

| Site | Kept | Written as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| YouTube | video ID, start time (`t`) | `https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=…&t=…` |
| Reddit | post ID, optional comment ID | `https://www.reddit.com/comments/…`, shown on `old.reddit.com` for members who prefer it |
| X | status ID | `https://x.com/i/status/…` |
| Amazon | product ID (ASIN), on the same store | `https://www.amazon.…/dp/…` |

- Tracking parameters come off every link: each site's own (YouTube's `si`, X's `s` and `t`, Reddit's `share_id`, Amazon's `ref` and its search and referral parameters), and a fixed list of those that track on any site, such as `utm_*`, `fbclid` and `gclid`. The protocol lists the forms each site's links take, and every parameter.
- Amazon's referral tag (`tag`) comes off with the rest, as it would with any link cleaner: it pays whoever shared the link, and tracks the click to them.
- A Reddit share link (`reddit.com/r/…/s/…`) stays as it is, apart from tracking parameters. Its code is Reddit's own record of who shared the link, and only Reddit can turn it into a post ID, which would mean Dens fetching from Reddit. Amazon's short links (`amzn.to/…`, `a.co/d/…`) stay as they are for the same reason.
- Hosts match exactly, as a URL parser reads them, so a lookalike link is never rewritten into a real one.
- The page shows links without knowing the rule, so the rule can take in more sites later without a protocol change. Links get shorter too, often by 20 to 80 bytes, but that's a side effect.
- No link previews in v1, apart from YouTube's players for members who turn them on (see YouTube players). Fetching a preview would reveal the den's or members' IPs to whoever runs the linked site, which any member can choose. Could be an opt-in feature for v2. Previews would take a fixed height, with their content scaled to fit, so they never shift the message list.

**Links to messages (M11)**

Copy link, in a message's menu, copies the den's address with the den's ID, the channel and the message in its fragment: `https://den.example.com/#m/<den ID>/41/90210`. A link opens by the den's ID, which the den proves with its identity key on every connection, so it still opens after the den moves to another address, and never in another den that takes the old one; the address is there for a browser, and to name a den the member hasn't joined. A browser never sends a fragment, so a link opened outside Dens tells the den nothing of which message. The den's ID is no secret: its invites carry it, and anyone at its address can ask for its key.

- In a message, a link in that form shows as a chip naming where it points, with the address on hover: the channel, as `#general`, or `@bob` for a DM, after the den's name when it's another den's, `#unknown` for a channel gone or out of sight, and the host of a den the member hasn't joined. Another den's names come from its state on this computer, fetched when a chip first needs them, and again once its channels or members change. Reply quotes and previews show the address, as they do any link.
- A click opens it once the local service has checked it, as a reply's quote opens its original: the den and the channel, where the page loads the history around the message and lights it for a moment, or, in the channel already open, the jump in place. When it can't, the chip says why, beside it, for a few seconds: the member hasn't joined that den; the channel is gone or they can't see it, which look alike, so a staff-only channel stays hidden; or the message is gone, deleted or past the den's retention. The check asks for the page `around` the message, one message long, which holds the message itself while it's there.
- A link into a den that's offline opens the channel from the cache, at the message if this computer kept it, and otherwise at the newest it kept, saying why.
- The chip's words are the member's own Dens's, never the sender's, and a click never sends the browser to the address, so a chip can't dress one link up as another. A click with Ctrl or Shift does as the browser would, opening the address, where the den answers with a line saying it's a den on Dens, whose links open in Dens.
- A channel's and a message's IDs are the den's to give, once each: deleting a message or a channel leaves its ID unused for good, and a restore moves the counters on (see Backup, restore, move), so a link never comes to name another message.
- An event's Copy link (M17.5) writes the same form with `#e/` and the event's ID, `https://den.example.com/#e/<den ID>/12`, which shows as a chip naming the event and as its card under the message (see Events).

**YouTube players (M4.1)**

A member can play a linked YouTube video where it's posted, in the message list, rather than in a new tab. It's off until they turn it on in the settings' Links section, beside where Reddit links open, since it means their Dens and their browser contact YouTube. The browser keeps the choice, as it keeps the other.

- With players on, a YouTube link in a message gets a cover under the message, after its files: the video's picture with its title and channel over it, as links to the video and the channel on YouTube (M14.1), and a play button. Only links in the one form the den writes count (`https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=…`, with `&t=` and a start time if there is one), each video once, and at most three per message. A link in a spoiler gets none, and reply quotes, bios and channel descriptions get none either.
- The cover takes a 16:9 box, 400 pixels wide at most, before anything in it loads, and the player takes the same box, so the message list never moves.
- Clicking the cover swaps in YouTube's player from `www.youtube-nocookie.com`, which plays at once, from the link's start time. The title and the channel's name, which lie over the cover's play button and take the pointer only where they're words, open the video, at the link's start time, and the channel on YouTube in a new tab, as a message's links do, while a click anywhere else on the cover plays it (M14.1).
- One video plays at a time: starting another puts the first back behind its cover. A playing video carries on while its message is scrolled out of view, so music and picture-in-picture keep going, and stops once its message leaves the part of the list that's loaded, or the member opens another channel.
- A video whose owner turned off embedding says it plays only on YouTube, with its link, instead of offering to play.

*The cover.* The member's local service fetches the title, channel and picture once a cover comes on screen, and the page shows them from its own origin, so its CSP lets in no image or connection from YouTube.

- The service asks YouTube's oEmbed endpoint for the title and channel, and `i.ytimg.com` for the picture, at addresses it builds from the video's ID: 11 of `A-Z`, `a-z`, `0-9`, `-` and `_`. It follows no redirects, reads at most 64 KiB of an answer and 256 KiB of a picture, and gives up after 10 seconds.
- The title and channel are cleaned as display names are, and shown as text. The channel's address, oEmbed's `author_url`, is kept beside its name only as an https address on `www.youtube.com`, written out again from its parts, and the page links the name to no other address either; without one, the name stays text (M14.1). Nothing else in oEmbed's answer is used, the ready-made player it offers least of all. The picture must be a JPEG, which `internal/media` reads and strips, and the page gets it with the headers of a member's file, so the browser doesn't keep it.
- The service sends no cookies, referrer or user agent. It holds what it fetched in memory for an hour, and a failure for a minute, up to 16 MiB in all; asks once for a video that several covers want at the same time; and never logs a video's ID, which comes from a message.

*Who learns what.* The setting says so, in fewer words:

- YouTube sees the member's IP address for each video whose cover comes on screen, in channels and in DMs, and which videos they play. `youtube-nocookie.com` holds YouTube's cookies back until a video plays; after that, the browser's own rules on third-party cookies decide whether YouTube can tie the play to a Google account.
- Several members of a den looking up one video at about the same time could let YouTube guess they know each other.
- In a DM, a cover tells YouTube which video the member was sent. The den still can't read it.
- Nobody but YouTube learns anything. Its addresses are fixed, so a member can't point anyone's Dens at a server of their own, which is why other links get no previews. With players off, links show as they always have, and nothing on the page contacts YouTube.

*The player's frame.* YouTube's player is a frame from another origin, so the browser keeps it from the page, the page's storage and the local API.

- It's sandboxed to scripts, its own origin and popups: it can't navigate the page, download files or open dialogs. A popup, such as YouTube's "Watch on YouTube", opens in a new tab that can't reach the page, since the page's `Cross-Origin-Opener-Policy: same-origin` cuts it off.
- It's allowed autoplay, encrypted media, picture-in-picture and full screen, and nothing else. The page's `Permissions-Policy` keeps the microphone and camera from it whatever it asks.
- The page builds the frame's address from the ID and start time it checked itself, since a hostile den or sender could skip the den's rewrite. Its CSP takes frames only from `https://www.youtube-nocookie.com`.
- The page listens for no messages from the frame, and doesn't use YouTube's player API, which would need it to.

*The page's address.* YouTube's player needs a `Referer` naming the page that embeds it, and won't play for a page at an IP address: `127.0.0.1`, `[::1]` and LAN addresses all get "This video is unavailable", while host names, `localhost` included, play. So the page lives at `localhost` (see Pairing the browser). The frame sends YouTube the page's origin and nothing more (`strict-origin-when-cross-origin`); everything else the page loads sends no referrer at all.

**Presence at 500 online**

- One WebSocket per member; presence changes are coalesced and broadcast in batches every 1 to 2 seconds.
- Typing indicators are throttled per channel and only sent to members viewing that channel.

## Message list and sync

**Message list**

- A channel view holds one contiguous run of messages, never the whole channel: at most 200, and none that lie more than eight screens beyond what's shown. Scrolling near either end loads the next page (`before` or `after` the edge message) and cuts the far end, so memory stays flat however far back a member scrolls. A page is 50 messages, or as many as fill about three screens when the rows near that end are tall, but at least 10. The cut goes by the rows as they're laid out, so a run of photos, each a few hundred pixels tall, holds a few dozen, where one of short messages holds 200. Rows near the view are never cut, so what the member reads stays put.
- The view is attached to the live tail while it holds the newest message, and new messages append. Jumping to an old message (a reply's quote, a link to a message, a mention, later search) loads the page `around` it and detaches the view. New messages then only update a "new messages, jump to present" bar, and scrolling forward to the newest page reattaches it. A member reading further up a run that's full detaches the same way when the next message arrives, rather than the run growing under them.
- A channel opened with unread messages shows a New divider where they start, which stays there while it's open. One opened with nothing unread shows none, so messages that arrive while it's open get none (M11).
- A load that replaces the run (opening a channel, a jump, coming back after a drop) applies again what changed while it was out, since the service may have taken its page first, so a message that arrives as a channel opens isn't lost.
- With the window bounded, every loaded message is in the DOM. There is no per-row virtualization: it would stop a playing video whose row scrolled out of range, and keep the browser's find from seeing what's loaded.
- The layout doesn't jump. Images carry their dimensions from upload, so placeholders take their final size, and loading older pages keeps the view anchored on the message being read. At the newest message, the view stays there while its box resizes or its text rewraps: a growing composer, a rotated phone. A message's reactions change its height without a page (M15.3): its first gives it a row of chips, and more may wrap it onto another, so as a reaction lands the list holds the message being read where it is, unless the view is at the newest, which it stays at; as does a poll's change, as its bars and voters come (M15.4).
- The read position moves once the newest message has been on screen, at most every two seconds per channel, and at once when the member leaves the channel. A busy channel costs the den one small write every few seconds, not one per message.
- Message IDs increase with time within a den and are never reused, so `before`, `after` and `around` are single index lookups.

**Sync and bandwidth**

Dens usually run on home connections, where upload bandwidth is scarce and every message goes out once per online member. The protocol keeps that fan-out small:

- **Resume, don't refetch.** Every change a member can see is an event with a sequence number. A client that reconnects sends the last one it saw and gets only what it missed, from an in-memory ring of recent events: the last 50,000 within 10 minutes, and from M8 within 64 MiB, so a member sending large messages fast can't grow the den's memory without bound. After a den restart, or a gap longer than the ring, the den sends a fresh snapshot instead (a resync), and the client refetches only what's on screen.
- **Snapshots without a scan.** `ready`'s newest message in each channel comes from a query per channel the member sees, which the `(channel_id, id)` index answers at once. A scan of every message took 38 ms at a million, for each of the up to 500 snapshots a den restart or a channel turning staff-only sends at once (M8).
- **Orderly restarts.** A stopping den closes its listener first, then tells every client it is restarting (WebSocket close 1012). Otherwise a quick client reconnects to the dying process, as the M1 spike showed. Clients wait a random delay of up to a few seconds, so 500 of them don't reconnect at once.
- **Scoped ephemeral traffic.** Presence is coalesced into batches every 1 to 2 seconds, and typing goes only to members viewing that channel.
- **Thumbnails, not originals.** Uploads get a small preview when they arrive. The message list shows previews, and originals load only when opened. The local service keeps both, encrypted on disk (M6; see The client's cache), and serves them to the browser with `Cache-Control: no-store`, so they never sit unencrypted in the browser's cache.
- **Compression, measured.** Compressing each message separately (permessage-deflate) saves only 22% on chat events. Context takeover saves 65%, but costs about 820 KB of den memory per connection, around 400 MB at 500 online. A static dictionary shared by client and den, with deflate per frame, reaches 43% of raw with no per-connection memory, and the den compresses each event once for every recipient. Batching events under load brings it to 29%. From M6 the den socket uses permessage-deflate without context takeover, for frames of more than 512 bytes: `ready`, replays and busy batches shrink as history pages do, to under a third, while a single chat event, too small to gain much, goes as it is. It holds no memory per connection: the WebSocket library takes a compressor from a pool for each compressed write. History pages over HTTP are gzipped, to 29% of raw. A dictionary for small frames waits until measurements show live events matter.
- **The client's cache and the changes since (M6).** A client keeps what it fetched from other dens, encrypted on disk, and after a gap asks the den only what changed, so a client restarted after a day away downloads only that (see The client's cache and What changed while away).

**The client's cache (M6)**

A member's Dens keeps what it fetches from other dens: the files and previews the page showed, and from M6.2 the messages it read and those that arrived while it was connected. A photo seen once never downloads again, a channel read before opens without asking the den, and a restart of the client or the den costs only what changed meanwhile.

- The cache is a SQLite database and a directory of sealed files of its own, the data directory's `cache`, apart from the install's database. It holds only copies of what dens hold, so backups leave it out, a new format of it starts again from empty rather than migrating, and its writes never wait behind the den's on an owner's install.
- Everything in it is sealed with the data key: each message, and each 64 KiB chunk of a file, bound to its den, its ID and its place, so nothing moves between entries unnoticed. A chunk that doesn't open takes its file out of the cache, and the page gets the file from the den. A cache sealed under another key, or of another format, is emptied, not read.
- The install's own den isn't cached: the client reaches it over loopback, and its files are on the same disk, so a cache would only double the owner's storage. The client keeps the small memory cache it always had for it.
- It holds 2 GiB unless the member chooses another size, from 512 MiB to 50 GiB, in the settings' General section, which also shows how much it holds and empties it. Past its size, what was used longest ago goes first: a whole file, or from M6.2 a channel's messages, by when the member last read the channel. A file larger than a quarter of the size isn't kept, and a channel keeps its newest 20,000 messages at most, so a busy one followed for years can't take the whole cache.
- A file is kept as it's read, a chunk at a time, so a video keeps the parts that played, and seeking fetches only the chunks it lacks. The chunks are the 64 KiB the den seals its uploads in, so a fetch decrypts exactly the den's own chunks. They're written in the order they arrive, each with its index, rather than at their place in a sparse file, which Windows would allocate in full. A file being read stays until its last reader closes it, since Windows can't delete an open file.
- What the page asks for comes from the cache first and the den second, and what the den sends goes into the cache on its way to the page. Files reach the page with the headers they always had, so the browser still caches none of them.
- Each cached file records what it's on: a message and its channel, a member's picture, or one of the den's emoji (M15.2), which the client notes from what it passes the page and from the den's state. A deleted message, a file taken off one, a deleted channel or one the member no longer sees, a retention pass, a replaced picture and a deleted emoji each take out exactly their files. A file the client can't place, it doesn't keep, since it couldn't tell when the file goes.
- A den's cache goes when the member leaves it, is removed or banned, or the device is signed out, and when the member forgets the den.
- Messages (M6.2): the cache holds them as the den sends them, a DM's still sealed, and opens them as the page reads them, with the keys the client already holds. Beside them it keeps runs, the ranges of IDs in a channel for which it holds every message the den has. IDs increase with time, so an old range can lose messages but never gain one.
- A message's reactions (M15.3) are kept with it, and change without its revision: each reaction's event changes the copy the cache holds, applied to its emoji's set, and a DM's set replaces the member's where its revision is newer. So does its poll (M15.4), as the member may see it, which each poll's event changes: the totals, and who voted for what with them, where the event carries them, the member's own votes where it's to their sessions, and the final totals at its end, after which no update changes it. What the den sends of a message, a page of history or what changed while away, replaces the copy at its own revision too, with the reactions' and polls' events that came while it was asked for applied again, in order, since the den may have read it before them; an edit's event replaces it at a newer revision alone. A DM's sets each keep the newer of the two copies', by their revision.
- A page of history comes from a run that covers it, and otherwise from the den, which then joins the cache, run and all. While connected, every new message goes into the cache, in every channel the member sees, so a channel that was busy while they were online opens without a request.
- A run that reaches its channel's newest message says so for one connection, the one it reached it in: while that lasts, every new message arrives as an event and joins it, so the newest page comes from the cache. A snapshot starts a new connection, and the runs that still reach their channel's newest message, as its read states say it, carry on in it. A channel that had new messages while the client was away asks the den for those after the newest it holds, a page or two, rather than its whole newest page; past two pages, it takes the newest page, and leaves a gap the member fills by scrolling.
- The cache follows the page's rules for a page fetched while events arrive: the higher revision wins, a message deleted meanwhile never comes back, and nothing retention reached goes in. A reply's quote follows its original as it changes or goes.
- The client keeps the den's state with it: its members, channels and groups, read states and DM keys. It names the version its list of members is current as of on the socket's upgrade, and `ready` then sends only the members changed since, which with 1,000 members is most of its size (M6.2).
- Offline reading (M6.2): a den the client can't reach shows as it last was, and what the cache holds of each channel reads as usual. A note over the messages says the den is offline and these are cached messages; it shows once the den has failed to connect, not while it connects, as at every start. A page the cache doesn't hold says the den is offline at once, rather than waiting out the den. Sending, calls and anything else that needs the den wait for it to come back.
- After a snapshot, the changes since reach the page too, as edits and deletes would: it may have loaded a channel from the cache before they came.
- A message that disappears (M16.4) keeps when it goes beside it, which the cache's fourth format added, so every cache started again from empty as the update landed. The cache lets it go then, by the computer's clock, with its files and the replies' quotes of it, whether the den can be reached or not, on a timer of its own set for the next to go, and keeps out a copy past its time; the client hears which went, and takes them off its pages and out of the notifications (see Disappearing messages).

**What changed while away (M6)**

A client away from a den longer than its ring of events, or across the den's restart, gets a fresh `ready`, and needs to know what changed in what it cached. The den doesn't keep its events for that: they carry every message's text, so a store of them would be a second copy that every delete and retention pass would have to find, and replaying it would send a day of every channel, read or not. It keeps a change log of IDs instead.

- The log records each message edited or deleted, with its channel, from M16.4 those that disappeared among them, and from M6.2 each member whose record changed, and from M15.3 each message whose reactions changed, and from M15.4 each poll whose votes changed or that ended, its revision as it was: one row for each, its latest change, numbered in the order the changes happened. The newest number is the den's version. A retention pass and a deleted channel add nothing, since `ready` says how far retention reached and a deleted channel is missing from it.
- `ready` carries the den's sync epoch, its version, the floor (the oldest version a client may still ask from) and the last message retention deleted. A client that synced in the same epoch at a version no older than the floor asks for what changed since: the messages edited or deleted since then, up to the newest it holds, in channels it can see now. That is usually a few hundred bytes, in one request.
- The den filters by what the member can see now, not when the change happened, so a role change, or a channel that became staff-only and back, needs nothing special: two visibility levels and DMs make that one condition. A channel missing from `ready` takes its cache with it.
- New messages aren't in it. A channel the member opens fetches its newest page, and pages back from there, as it always did, from the cache where it can, so a busy channel nobody opens costs nothing.
- The log keeps 30 days of changes. A client whose version is older than the floor, or from another epoch, drops what it cached for the den and starts again. A client connected all along asks for the changes once a day, which keeps its version within the 30 days, and applies them as it applies events: twice changes nothing.
- A den restored from a backup starts a new epoch. The backup carries its database's counter of message IDs, so the restored den can give a new message an ID that a client holds for another message, one the backup never had; a client of another epoch drops its cache for the den instead.
- Live events still keep a connected client's cache current, and the ring still resumes a short gap without a `ready`. The change log covers what the ring can't.

## Files and media

Members attach files to messages and put pictures on their profiles. Images lose their metadata before they leave the member's machine. Photos and videos go as smaller copies unless the member sends them full size, and everything fits within owner-set limits that members see and manage (M5). Files in DMs are the exception from M1.7: the sender's service strips and encrypts them before upload, and the den stores only opaque blobs (see End-to-end encryption).

**Limits (owner settings)**

There's no warning ahead of a full disk, which would take the den reporting its free space to its members: an upload it refuses for space says so ("This den is out of space for uploads") (M8.3).

- Max size per file, per member total, and den total. A new den starts at 25 MiB per file, 2 GiB per member and 20 GiB for the den. The page checks a file's size before uploading it, and says why when the den refuses one.
- Uploads also stop while the den's disk has less than 1 GiB free, so they never fill the disk the database lives on.
- The limits for calls and screen shares live in the same place (M4.2; see Limits for calls), and from M14.3 the soundboard's (see The soundboard).

**Metadata stripping**

- EXIF, GPS and similar metadata are stripped by the uploading member's own service, as the file streams to the den, so the den never receives a photo's location, and neither does its owner. The den runs the same check and refuses an image that still has any, which holds other clients to it.
- Images: removal without re-encoding, the same code on both sides (`internal/media`). JPEG, PNG, GIF and WebP keep only what decoding, color and animation need, and a JPEG keeps its orientation in a minimal EXIF block of its own; the protocol lists what stays. Stripping never touches pixels, so an image sent full size loses nothing; a smaller copy is a new image (see Smaller copies).
- Video and audio need ffmpeg, to copy their streams into a new container without the metadata (what `ffmpeg -map_metadata -1 -c copy` does), and so do the photo formats that can't be stripped without decoding them: HEIF (as iPhones save), TIFF, JPEG 2000 and Photoshop files, which the sender's machine turns into a JPEG, or a PNG when they have transparency. AVIF, JPEG XL and camera raw are refused, and the member told why, rather than sent with a location nobody took out: AVIF until after v1, when Dens strips it in place, and the other two because the module can't decode them. Dens doesn't strip video containers with code of its own. MP4 and Matroska hide metadata in many places, and ffmpeg already handles them, so there's one stripper to get right, not two.
- A video or audio file is stripped twice: by the sender's service, so the den never receives what it carried, and again by the den, which stores its own copy. The den can't check a container for metadata the way it checks an image, since FFmpeg's demuxers skip boxes they don't know, but nothing comes out of FFmpeg's muxer that the driver didn't copy. A photo in a format the module converts reaches the den as a JPEG or PNG, which the den checks as any image, and its name ends in the new format's extension.
- Video and audio in containers the module doesn't read, such as AVI, ASF, FLV and MPEG transport streams, are refused, as is a TIFF that holds camera raw.
- Other files, such as documents and archives, are sent as they are, with whatever they carry inside; the docs say so.
- Each file the member attaches shows a mark when metadata came out of it, whose tip says what metadata can hold. There is no opt-out in v1.

**Previews**

- The den makes an image's preview from what it stored: a JPEG, or a PNG with transparency, fitting 640 × 640, upright, from an animated image's first frame. One image decodes at a time, within a 256 MiB budget that fits a 50-megapixel phone photo; a larger image goes without a preview and downloads like any other file.
- A video's preview is its first frame, made the same way with ffmpeg from the copy the den stored. The module decodes the H.264 and HEVC phones record, but not VP8, VP9 or AV1, for their size, so a WebM or AV1 video's preview comes from the sender's page, which draws the first frame the browser plays and sends it beside the upload. The den decodes that image and makes its own preview from it, so it keeps nothing the page made as it was. Without one, the list shows a placeholder of the video's shape, which the container states without decoding. Audio has no preview.
- Uploads state a video's size as it displays, after its rotation, and its duration, so the list lays it out before it loads.
- Uploads state an image's size as it displays, so the message list gives a preview its final box before it loads (see Message list).

**Storage**

- A den encrypts uploads with its data key, like message text: 64 KiB chunks of XChaCha20-Poly1305 in the STREAM construction, so files of any size stream in and out in bounded memory, and a file cut short, reordered or swapped for another doesn't open. They sit under random names that only their database rows know, in the data directory's `uploads`, which backups include.
- A sealed file opens at any chunk: each 64 KiB chunk's nonce comes from its position, so reading from an offset decrypts only from the chunk it falls in.
- A file's ID is a den ID like any other, and its bytes never change under it, so clients can cache it by ID. IDs are not content hashes, which would let a member test whether a file they have is somewhere they can't see.
- An upload waits an hour for a message or a profile to use it. Deleting a message, a channel or a member's messages deletes their files, and a member who leaves loses their pictures and unused uploads.

**Smaller copies (M5)**

A phone's photos and videos hold far more than a chat shows. The viewer fits a photo to the window, and a 12-megapixel photo has several times the pixels a screen shows there. So the member's Dens sends a smaller copy of each photo and video unless they send it full size. Each member's space and the den's then last several times longer, and uploads finish sooner on a home connection; Signal, WhatsApp and Telegram send photos smaller by default for the same reasons. The sender's machine makes the copy, never the den, which couldn't make one for a DM anyway: the local service makes it with the media module, or a video's, where the member's browser can, the browser does, with its own encoders, many times faster (M5.5). Nothing changes a file once it's sent, so the choice is made before anyone sees it.

- It's on unless the member turns it off in the settings' General section ("Send photos and videos smaller"), which the browser keeps.
- A copy is made as the file is added to a message, before it uploads, since uploads start then and the message sends as soon as they're done. The local service makes both versions then: the full size, as the photo goes without a copy, which is the file stripped, or for a photo Dens converts, a JPEG or PNG of all its pixels; and the copy. It uploads the one to send, and keeps both, each in a scratch file sealed with a key of its own, until the message is sent, the photo is taken off it, or the hour a den keeps an upload waiting is up. A twelve-megapixel JPEG's copy takes about a third of a second, and each of a HEIC's two versions about half a second.
- Each file's chip shows the size it will send, and whether that's the smaller copy or full size. A photo the browser can't draw, such as a HEIC, shows the preview its upload has.
- A photo's copy fits 2,560 pixels on its longer side, never enlarged, upright, with its ICC profile. JPEGs, and the photos Dens converts (HEIF, TIFF, JPEG 2000 and Photoshop), become JPEGs at FFmpeg's quality 5, or PNGs when they have transparency. A PNG, usually a screenshot, stays a PNG and is only scaled down when it's larger than that, so its text stays sharp. The module's still makes each copy, decoding JPEGs and PNGs for it too. WebP stills, GIFs and anything animated go as they are.
- A copy goes only when it's at most three quarters of the full-size file's size. Otherwise the full-size file goes, as a smaller copy downloaded and sent again does.
- A photo or video over the den's size limit goes as a smaller copy whatever the setting, rather than being refused, when the copy fits, and its chip says why. A video's copy then takes the bitrate that fits the limit (see Smaller videos).
- On the ten phone photos among the media spike's fixtures, copies are 58 to 94% smaller than the full-size files, 82% in all, with an SSIM of 0.96 to 0.99 against the same photo scaled without loss. Lossy WebP came out the same size and a little sharper, which isn't worth an encoder the module lacks. AVIF came out half the size, but dens refuse AVIF until it's stripped in place after v1, and libaom took about 15 seconds a photo without SIMD, on one thread.

*Comparing before sending.* Clicking a chip's size opens the file in the viewer, to compare its two versions and pick one:

- It first shows the photo as the viewer shows everyone, fitted to the window, where the two look the same: at 2,560 pixels, a copy has about as many as the viewer shows on a 4K screen, and more than it shows on smaller ones. A second view shows the full-size photo at 100%, one of its pixels on each of the screen's, with the copy scaled up to match, as someone who downloads it would see it, and the member drags to look around. A click on the fitted photo looks at that spot at 100%, and a click on a zoomed one fits it again. Two fingers or the wheel zoom it anywhere between, and on to twice the full size's pixels, as the viewer zooms (M13.3).
- A switch flips between the versions in place, each labeled with its pixels and size, and holding Space, or a finger on the picture (M13.2), shows the other one while it's held. A difference shows best when the picture changes in place, not side by side, where the eye compares two spots.
- It says how much of their space the member uses on the den, since whether full size is worth it depends on its cost as much as on its quality.
- "Send full size instead" switches the file, and then reads "Send smaller instead". It can do the same for the message's other photos, and it's disabled, with the reason, when the full-size file is over the den's limit. Switching uploads the other version and drops the one waiting. ← and → move between the message's photos.
- The page shows both versions from the local service, which kept them as the photo was added, so a HEIF, TIFF or Photoshop file, which browsers can't draw, compares like any other.
- A video compares as a photo does, its two versions playing in place, paused at the same moment, with each version's size, pixels and frame rate (M5.4). The copy is AV1, which every target browser plays; the full size is what the phone recorded, which a browser plays only if it decodes it: one that doesn't, such as Chromium without graphics that decode HEVC, which iPhones record, shows the copy alone and says why.

*Smaller videos (M5.4, M5.5).*

- The local service makes a video's copy with the media module, which carries libaom's realtime AV1 encoder (M5.4). As the video is added, it strips the video as it does today, which is its full size, plans the copy, and encodes it in chunks, each made in a worker of its own, as many at once as half the computer's cores, at the lowest priority, so calls keep their CPU. Each worker takes one chunk, or as many as keep each within ten seconds, cut at the keyframe nearest each cut where one is near: every chunk starts with a keyframe, which costs bits, so fewer chunks look better, and on the iPhone's HDR video one chunk a worker came out both faster and about two points higher (VMAF) than two. It muxes the chunks with the video's sound, copied as it is, into an MP4 that keeps the video's turn, and from there a video's copy goes as a photo's does: three quarters of the size or less, both versions kept until the message goes, and switching uploads the other. A copy that wouldn't come to three quarters, judged by the full size's bitrate, isn't made.
- Where WebCodecs decodes the video and encodes AV1 well, which in the video spike was Chrome and Edge, the page makes the copy instead, often in the GPU and many times faster (M5.5). A browser built on Chromium says so as it uploads the video; Firefox's encoders run slowly and miss their bitrate too far to fit a limit, so Firefox and Waterfox leave the copy to the module. Once the video is stripped, the local service offers the page the copy: what decoding the video stream's packets takes, and how to encode the copy. A browser that can't decode the video, such as Chrome without graphics that decode HEVC, which iPhones record, or can't encode the copy, declines, saying why, and the module makes it.
- The page takes the packets from the local service as it decodes them, keeps at most the frames a second the copy has, as the module does, crops each as the container asks, and hands it to the encoder at its own size, which scales it: drawing each through a canvas first cost the spike 15 to 20 points (VMAF). The encoder aims at the plan's bitrate, for quality rather than speed, with a keyframe every five seconds as the module's copies have, in hardware where the browser has it. The page sends the AV1 packets back as they come, and the local service checks them as they arrive, into a sealed scratch file, then muxes them with the video's sound as it muxes its own chunks, and checks the MP4 as it checks its own copy.
- The page's copy is at most 1080p (1,920 pixels on its longer side). Fitting a limit, it takes four fifths of the room where the module's takes nine tenths, since a browser's encoder misses its bitrate by more, and steps down through 720p to 480p as the module's does. A copy the page doesn't finish, the module makes: one it declines or fails, an offer it doesn't answer within ten seconds, a copy it sends nothing of for half a minute, packets that break the rules, or a page that goes away. One over the limit is offered once more, lower, as the module makes its own again.
- The module's scan reads a video's packets without decoding them, for its keyframes' times, its frames and its bytes, which the plan goes by. Its encode decodes a chunk from the keyframe before its start, keeps at most 30 frames a second, skipping the frames nothing refers to when a 60-frame video is halved, scales each with a bicubic filter, which costs a twentieth more time than bilinear and scored about two points higher (VMAF), and encodes them with libaom at its fastest realtime speed, starting with a keyframe, at the video's own times, so the copy keeps time with its sound. Its mux writes the chunks' packets and the original's sound into an MP4 with the original's turn, and refuses chunks whose configurations differ.
- The page follows each upload over a WebSocket of its own, opened as the upload starts, which says how far its copy and its upload have come: a request would wait behind the browser's six connections to the local service while uploads hold them, and Firefox reports a request's body sent only once the whole request is over. The page's copy goes over the same socket. The local service offers it, the page accepts or declines, then asks for the video's packets a window at a time, saying how many bytes it can take, and sends the copy's packets back as records: each packet's size, flags, time and duration. The local service takes only whole records, of a size a packet can be, with times only increasing and a keyframe first, within the largest file a den takes.
- The video spike (`spikes/README.md` at commit 98a9441) measured both ways on the media spike's phone videos. The module's encoder, without SIMD or threads, made about 5.5 frames a second at 720p and 2 at 1080p on a fast desktop, and workers side by side scaled to 35 with eight. WebCodecs in Chrome and Edge with an RTX 4090 decoded an iPhone's HEVC in hardware, HLG included, and encoded AV1 at 150 to 370 frames a second in hardware and about 100 in software, within 20% of the bitrate, at VMAF 86 to 93. Firefox and Waterfox decoded no HEVC, and encoded at 6 to 17 frames a second, two to four times over the bitrate. AV1 at 720p and 1.2 Mbps scored VMAF 70 to 84 on the short phone videos.
- On Windows with an RTX 4090, Chrome and Edge made the iPhone's 22-second 1080p HDR video into a 1080p copy in 5.5 seconds, decoding and encoding in hardware, at VMAF 83 and 2.6 Mbps against the 2.5 planned, where the module takes 24 seconds for its 720p at VMAF 66. Chromium on Linux, without graphics, made an iPhone's H.264 video's copy in software in 1.3 seconds, where the module takes 7.6.
- A copy is AV1, which every target browser plays, at 30 frames a second at most and about 0.04 bits a pixel each frame, which is 1.1 Mbps at 720p and 2.5 at 1080p. The module's copy is at most 720p (1,280 pixels on its longer side, never enlarged), since its 1080p is more than twice as slow: on an eight-core desktop it made the iPhone's 22-second 1080p HDR video, at 60 frames a second, into 720p in 24 seconds, about 27 frames a second, a tenth of its size at VMAF 66, and a four-core laptop takes a few times that. A browser's copy is at most 1080p. The video spike measured AV1 against VP9, which came out worse at the same size and overshot its bitrate; H.264 was never in it: x264 is GPL.
- A video over the den's limit gets the bitrate that fits it, with room for its sound and for the encoder overshooting, stepping down to 720p and then 480p as the bitrate falls. Below a floor it's refused as too long for the den, with about the longest it takes. A copy that comes out over the limit anyway is made once more at a lower bitrate.
- The copy keeps the video's turn, as stripping does, and drops a crop the container asks for by cropping each frame before it's encoded. An HDR video, as iPhones record, gets an eight-bit copy keeping its HDR and BT.2020 tags, which browsers tone-map: the spike found it as good as a ten-bit one. The page's encoder takes an HDR video's ten-bit frames as they decode and leaves the tags out of its AV1 stream, so the page's copy carries them in the MP4 alone, which Chrome, Firefox and FFmpeg follow.
- A video the module doesn't decode, VP8, VP9 or AV1 as a WebM holds, gets a copy only from a browser that does (M5.5). Without a copy, a video goes full size if it fits, and is refused otherwise, saying why.
- A copy takes a while, unlike a photo's, so the chip shows its progress, and then how much has gone to the den, and the message waits for it as it waits for uploads. While it's made, the chip offers to send the video full size instead, when that fits the den. A video sent full size by the setting gets no copy at all, which would take minutes to make for nothing, so it can't be compared; one over the limit gets its copy either way.
- The den can't decode AV1 to make the copy's poster, so the sender's Dens makes one from the full-size video's first frame, with the module, and sends it beside the copy. The page draws a WebM's, from its copy where it made one, as it does for a WebM sent full size.

**Animated pictures (M15.1)**

Cropping or scaling an animation means making each of its frames again, which a page's canvas can't do: it draws an animated image's first frame alone. So the member's local service makes an animated picture, in Go: the standard library reads and writes GIF, and `golang.org/x/image`, already a dependency, decodes WebP and scales. The media module has neither codec, and is built without FFmpeg's filters. The den checks what the service makes as it checks any GIF.

- *The budget.* The service reads a file's frames twice: first their places, timing and count, without decoding any, refusing one past its budget; then each frame in turn, as it's drawn. A file is at most 32 MiB, of at most 1,000 frames, its canvas and each frame at most 4 megapixels, and at most 2^28 pixels are decoded and scaled in all: each frame's own, and the framed part's once for each frame. The worst it allows took under 3 seconds on a fast desktop, as 32 frames of 2,048 pixels square or 500 of 512, which a slow laptop takes a few times over. It runs in the service itself, one at a time, not in the media module's worker: Go's decoders can't run past their memory as C's can, and the budget bounds the rest. A panic in the WebP decoder, another's code reading the member's file, refuses the file.
- *Reading frames.* A GIF's frames are read as browsers read them, with Go's `compress/lzw`: a GIF cut short keeps the whole frames before the cut, one with something other than a block after a frame ends there, a frame whose data goes wrong partway keeps the pixels before, and an index past a frame's color table leaves its pixel as it was. A GIF's screen grows to hold its first frame, as browsers grow one too small for it, and later frames are cut to it. A WebP's frames must lie on its canvas, as libwebp requires; each frame's image and alpha are rebuilt as a still, which the decoder reads.
- *Drawing them.* Each frame is drawn on a canvas over those before it, as the format says: a GIF's disposal leaves a frame, clears it to transparent or puts back what was under it once its time is up, 4 reading as 3 as browsers read it, and its transparent index leaves what's under it; a WebP's frame is drawn over what's there or in its place, and cleared to transparent, never to the background color, which browsers ignore. A frame of 10 ms or less shows for 100, as browsers show it, and a GIF's loops and a WebP's plays carry over.
- *Cropping and scaling.* The framed part is scaled to the picture's size with Catmull-Rom, after a part at least four times its sides is shrunk by a whole factor, each square of pixels averaged, which costs a fraction of the filter at that size. The picture is the part's size, never enlarged, within a picture's: 64 to 256 pixels square, a banner 300 to 600 wide, so a small GIF isn't blown up.
- *The GIF.* Each frame gets a palette of its own: its colors, where it has at most 255, as a GIF's frames often do, and otherwise 255 by median cut over its colors at five bits a channel, dithered by Floyd and Steinberg's error diffusion, so a gradient doesn't band: a smooth gradient's patches keep their color within half a level on average, where bands put them two off, at twice the size for such a frame and a few percent more for a busy clip's. A pixel under half opaque becomes the transparent index, and the rest keep their color. Each frame holds only the rectangle that changed, its unchanged pixels transparent: a pixel changes once it moves more than 4 levels in a channel from how it was last drawn, below a palette's error and above the noise of scaling and lossy frames, and measured from that drawing, not the frame before, so a slow fade can't drift. A pixel that turns transparent can be drawn so only by clearing the frame before once its time is up, which grows to take it in; a frame that changes nothing adds its time to the one before. So a GIF of a small part of a busy animation, or of one that mostly holds still, is as large as its motion. Past 2 MiB, or 4 MiB for a banner, it's refused as too long, as it passes the size.
- *The den's check.* A picture is a still, or an animated GIF, square, 64 to 256 pixels and at most 2 MiB; a banner a still, or an animated GIF three times as wide as high, 300 to 600 pixels wide and at most 4 MiB. An animated picture is a GIF alone, since only a member's service makes them. Its preview, from its first frame, as every image's, is what a page shows while it doesn't play, and the client's cache keeps both, as it keeps any picture.

**Managing files (M5)**

- Each den on the home page has a Files section for the member's own uploads there: how much of their space they use, and of the den's, and their files, largest first, each with what uses it: a message, with its channel or DM and the start of its text, a picture on their profile, or nothing while it waits to be sent. Open shows a file's message in its channel.
- A DM's files list the same way: the den knows only their sizes and the message that holds them, and the member's Dens opens the messages for their names and previews. A DM file's preview is a blob of its own, which goes with its file rather than in the list.
- A member deletes a file, which takes it out of its message, or swaps it for another they upload, which takes its place there, made smaller like any other unless they send it full size. A message left with neither text nor files is deleted. A picture on a profile is taken off from the list, and changed on the profile, whose new picture names the one it replaces, so that works at the limit too. A waiting upload is dropped.
- A swap works for a member at their limit: the replacement's upload names the file it replaces, and the den counts it against the space that file frees. It can only take that file's place, and goes within the hour if it doesn't.
- A file's bytes never change under its ID, so a delete or a swap is an edit of the message: it marks the message edited, and lists the file that's gone, so clients drop their copies. In a channel the den makes the change. In a DM, where each file's key and description live in the sealed text, the member's Dens seals the message again without the file, or with its replacement.
- A member drops an upload they won't send, by taking it off the composer or switching it between its smaller copy and full size, and its space is free at once rather than within the hour.

**Spoilers (M17.2)**

- A photo or video waiting in the composer has Spoiler, which its sender turns on or off while it uploads and after, its preview blurred in the composer once it's on. In a channel the page offers it where the den keeps spoilers, as its info says (`spoilers`); in a DM or a group always, since the mark goes inside the seal.
- One sent as a spoiler shows covered: its preview blurred past telling, under Spoiler, at the size the file takes once shown, naming nothing of it, the file's name included. A click or a tap shows it, each time its message is shown, the cover coming back once the message leaves the page; nothing of a video plays before, and the viewer leaves out a message's images still covered.
- The send names which of its files are spoilers. In a channel the den keeps it on the file (`den_files.spoiler`), for everyone who sees the message; in a DM or a group it goes inside the seal, with the file's name, so the den never learns it. Once sent, it stays: an edit keeps it, and a file swapped into a spoiler's place is one too, on the den in a channel and in the member's Dens's new seal in a DM.
- An older Dens shows spoilers uncovered: it doesn't know the mark. A member's cache kept by an older Dens holds a channel's messages without it, which then show uncovered there until they're fetched again; the member saw them uncovered already.

**Serving**

- Everything downloads through the local service, which keeps what it fetched from other dens in its cache (M6). It serves a file inline only when its own look at the bytes finds an image of one of the four kinds, or video or audio in a container the module writes, and then as exactly that type; anything else, SVG and HTML included, goes out as `application/octet-stream` with `Content-Disposition: attachment`. Every file carries `X-Content-Type-Options: nosniff` and `Content-Security-Policy: default-src 'none'; sandbox`, so even one opened on its own can't run anything. The page's policy allows media from itself (`media-src 'self'`).
- Clicking an image opens it in a viewer over the page, fitted to the window: the preview at once, and the original as it loads. When a message holds several images, ‹ and › at the viewer's sides, and the ← and → keys, move between them, and it shows which one it's on, as "2 of 5"; they stop at the ends (M5). On the phone layout it takes the screen, and swipes move between them (see The phone layout). Two fingers or a double tap zoom it, as do the wheel and a double click, until its own pixels show twice as large as the screen's, and at least four times; a finger or the mouse then pans it as far as it reaches, and a pinch that leaves it barely larger fits it again (M13.3).
- Video and audio play as they download: the den answers byte ranges of a file, decrypting only the chunks a range covers, and the local service passes ranges through, from M6 keeping the chunks they cover, so the page's player starts at once and seeks. A DM's video works the same way, the local service fetching the sealed chunks a range covers and opening them with the file's key.
- Videos in the message list play with controls of the page's own, since browsers' own can't be kept up: they stay while a video is paused, showing where it stopped and how long it is, and while it plays they hide once the pointer rests or leaves. Audio keeps the browser's controls.
- A video's volume is a mute button, which shows a vertical volume slider on hover or keyboard focus (M3.3). The level is every video's: the browser keeps it, and every video follows it, playing or still to start, while muting stays each video's own. A thin video keeps its full screen button, where every control shows, as the rest give way to its width: the time first, then the mute, the seek bar, and play, which a click on the video does. The seek bar is the page's own (`seek`): a track 4 pixels thick, its played part as thin, and a thumb of 12, in a box 24 pixels tall for a finger, where DaisyUI's range drew the played part as tall as its thumb (M15).
- The den serves every file as bytes to download, and never states a type a browser would act on.
- A file's name is text from a den: shown as text, and cleaned before it names a download.

**ffmpeg**

- ffmpeg is compiled to WebAssembly and translated to Go with wasm2go, the way the SQLite driver is built. One pure-Go build serves Linux and Windows, with no native binaries to vendor, and it runs under `MemoryDenyWriteExecute`, which rules out a WebAssembly JIT.
- The module holds FFmpeg 9.0's libraries and a small driver of Dens's own in C, not the `ffmpeg` command, which needs threads since FFmpeg 7.0 and parses options Dens has no use for. The driver exports what Dens does with media: probe, strip, still and poster; from M5.4, scan, encode and mux, which plan a video's copy, make it in chunks and put them together; and from M5.5, demux, which passes a video's packets to the page for a copy of its own. FFmpeg keeps only what those need: the containers phones and browsers use, and the image formats' own demuxers; the HEVC, H.264, MJPEG, PNG, TIFF, JPEG 2000 and Photoshop decoders; swscale; and the JPEG and PNG encoders, with zlib, and from M5.4 libaom's AV1 encoder, built for realtime alone in eight bits. VP8, VP9 and AV1 keep their parsers, which give a WebM's size without decoding it.
- Stripping copies the video and audio streams into a new container, and nothing else: no metadata, chapters, attachments, cover art, data tracks or subtitles, which some cameras fill with coordinates. A stream's side data comes along only from a list of what playback needs: rotation, cropping, stereo and 360° layouts, HDR's light levels and mastering display, Dolby Vision's configuration, and the ICC profile, as Dens keeps it in images. The copy names no encoder. A MOV is written out as an MP4, which Chrome plays where it won't play a MOV, and which can carry a Dolby Vision configuration, as FFmpeg writes it into MP4 when allowed unofficial boxes. The SEI user data H.264 and HEVC carry inside the stream, which iPhones write in every frame (19 bytes, no readable text), is dropped by the driver as it copies each frame, since Dens can't tell what's in it.
- An MP4 the driver writes, stripped or a copy, gives its movie a timescale of 1000, which its edit lists count in (M11). Left to itself, FFmpeg 9 takes the least common multiple of the streams' time bases, as 4,410,000 for a phone's 90 kHz video with 44.1 kHz sound, and Firefox reads the empty edit that starts a track late against the track's own timescale instead: a phone's clip, whose sound starts 22 ms in, played its picture and then its sound, 2.2 seconds in. At 1000, FFmpeg's default before, and most files', it plays in time in every browser.
- A still assembles a HEIC's tile grid, crops it, turns it as its display matrix says, the way the ffmpeg command reads one, and keeps its ICC profile. A JPEG's turn is in its EXIF, which FFmpeg's demuxer doesn't read, so Dens passes the orientation `internal/media` found, and the driver picks the JPEG or PNG demuxer by the file's first bytes rather than trust FFmpeg's guess. A PNG stays a PNG, written with each row filtered the way that packs it smallest. The encoder gets a fresh frame carrying only that profile, so nothing else the source carried, EXIF included, reaches the file. It holds at most two full images at a time, so a 48-megapixel photo fits in 512 MB. A poster is a video's first frame, cropped as its container asks and turned. HDR video isn't tone-mapped, so an HDR video's poster looks washed out.
- The module is its own sandbox. It sees only its linear memory and the few functions Dens gives it: the input's bytes in and the output's bytes out, with no files, network or processes. A hostile file that takes over a decoder is stuck in the module's memory, which matters for a library parsing this many formats. The host keeps it so:
  - It answers the WASI calls wasi-libc makes with the clock, randomness and a log, and refuses any that would open a file, list a directory or reach a socket.
  - It reserves the module's memory up to the cap, and hands it over as a slice whose capacity is its length. wasm2go checks `memory.fill`, `memory.copy` and `memory.init` against a slice's capacity, so spare capacity would let them write past the end instead of trapping.
  - It starts the module at the size its memory import declares.
  - It recovers a trap, which wasm2go turns into a Go panic, as the end of the job.
- It runs in a worker process, a hidden command of the same binary, with a memory cap, a deadline, and a Go stack limit well below the default, so a decoder that loops, balloons or recurses ends its job and not the service. The worker holds no keys and opens no files: the module's reads, writes and seeks go to the service over the worker's standard input and output, and the service answers from the files it holds, the den's sealed with its data key. Jobs run one at a time at the lowest CPU priority so calls and streams keep their CPU: `IDLE_PRIORITY_CLASS` on Windows, and on Linux nice 19 plus `SCHED_IDLE`. A video copy's chunks are the exception (M5.4): they run in workers side by side, as many as half the computer's cores, at that same priority, so they take only time nothing else wants. Linux nice values and scheduling policies are per thread, so the service sets them on the thread that starts the worker process (then discards that thread), and every thread the worker starts inherits them. There is no transient cgroup scope: the sandboxed service account can't create one, and a niced child already gets a small share next to the service's own threads.
- A file the sender's service strips or converts spools first into a scratch file sealed with a key that lives only for the job, in blocks it can rewrite in place: the MP4 muxer writes its index last, and the driver then moves it to the front, so a player starts before the whole file arrives. Nothing of a member's file lies on disk in the clear, even for a moment.
- Dens probes and strips media, and makes stills, previews and smaller copies. It re-encodes a photo when browsers can't show its format or Dens can't strip it in place, as a HEIC, which becomes a JPEG on the sender's machine, and for a smaller copy, which the sender can decline (M5). Stripping copies streams, and runs at the disk's pace in the module; a still or a preview decodes one image or frame.
- Dens doesn't run a native ffmpeg confined by the operating system instead. That would take confinement written and tested for each OS, and a second program to build, sign, ship and keep patched, where the module holds a hostile file the same way on every platform, inside the service's own sandbox.
- The module holds a bug inside its memory, but doesn't find it: one that corrupts the module's own heap can still give a wrong result without a trap. So Dens's own C is fuzzed natively under AddressSanitizer with damaged files, beside the module, in its tests.
- The build leaves out GPL-only parts such as x264, so ffmpeg's terms stay LGPL beside Dens's MIT. Its source and build script ship with Dens, which lets anyone rebuild the binary with a changed ffmpeg, as the LGPL requires: each release publishes FFmpeg's exact source beside the binaries. wasi-sdk, binaryen, FFmpeg, zlib, libaom and CMake are pinned like the other build tools, and wasm2go through Go's checksum database.
- The translation is generated Go, which the repository keeps, as go-sqlite3 keeps SQLite's: a script with the pinned tools regenerates it, and CI regenerates it and fails if it differs. Building Dens needs nothing beyond Go, and a change to the module is a reviewed diff of its inputs.
- What it costs, as measured in the spike after M1 (`spikes/README.md` at commit 3cd230d): the module adds about 26 MiB to a binary and compiles cold in 20 seconds within 5 GB. libaom adds about 17 MB more, and takes a cold compile to about 8 GB (M5.4). Stripping runs at 0.5 to 2 GB/s, and a 4 GB phone video takes under 70 MB, since MP4's frame index grows by about 16 MB per GB; Matroska's stays flat. A 12-megapixel HEIC becomes a JPEG in 0.5 to 0.7 seconds, and a 1080p video's poster takes a quarter of a second; the translation runs at 1.3 to 1.9 times native.

## End-to-end encryption

From M1.7, DMs and the photos in them are end-to-end encrypted: the den stores and relays them but can't read them. Channels stay readable by the den, since staff moderate them and the den does the work that needs their text: mentions, reply previews and compact links. From M16.1 each member has an identity in each den, which a DM's keys are sealed to, so a DM opens at once, with nothing to type; members compare codes when they choose, which the den never learns. From M16.2 calls are end-to-end encrypted too: each member's voice, screen and share's sound are sealed frame by frame in their page, under a key their Dens seals to each other member's identity (see Calls). From M16.3 group DMs are, of three to ten members, whose every change its maker signs, so the den can't add anyone (see Group DMs). From M17.3 each member's notes to self are, which only their own devices read (see Notes to self).

**What it protects**

- The den's owner, and anyone holding the den's disk or backups, can't read DMs, group DMs or a member's notes, or see the photos in them, or a group's name.
- Keys pass through the den, so a den could hand out keys of its own and sit in the middle of a DM. It would have to give two members identities of its own the first time they meet, both ways, and go on doing it for as long as they talk; comparing codes, at any time, catches it (see Codes).
- From M16.2, a call's voices and shares: the den forwards frames it can't open. It still sees who is in a call, when, and how much each member sends, and it chooses who's in a call, so a member it added in secret would have to show in the call's list to hear anything.
- Metadata stays visible: the den sees who DMs whom, when, and how much, and it can hold messages back.
- Members who compare codes over a channel the den's owner controls, or with someone pretending to be the other member, aren't protected, nor are those who never compare, with a den that tampers. Nothing here is bulletproof, and the app says so.
- There's no forward secrecy: a stolen device, or a leaked seal together with a copy of the den's data, opens all of that member's DM history. A ratchet's forward secrecy comes from forgetting keys, while a DM's history here opens from the den on each of a member's devices (see loose-ideas.md's Probably not doing).

**The DM seal**

All of it uses Go's standard library, plus the XChaCha20-Poly1305 that already seals data at rest.

- Each member has a DM seal: 256 random bits, made by their Dens the first time they join or create a den, and kept in the vault. It never reaches the den. Their other devices get it through approval (see Approving new devices), sealed to that device.
- One seal serves every den an install joins. Each den's DM keys are sealed under a key derived from the seal and the den's ID, so no den sees the seal, and what one den stores is useless at another. An install that joined dens before it was linked to the member's other devices can hold a different seal for some dens; each den's DMs use the seal they were set up with.
- The page shows the seal beside the recovery codes whenever the member joins a den, saying when it's the one the install's other dens use, and again on request after the local password: "This protects your end-to-end encrypted direct messages. Keep it secret and don't share it with anyone. You'll need it to read your DMs if you lose every device."
- The seal never changes. A member who loses it and every device starts over (see History and starting over).
- The seal is what the member's identity in each den comes from (see Identities), so it guards every DM, from M16.2 every call, from M16.3 every group, and from M17.3 the key of the member's notes (see Notes to self).

**Identities (M16.1)**

- A member's identity in a den is two key pairs: one for HPKE (RFC 9180) with X-Wing, X25519 and ML-KEM-768 together, which keys are sealed to (`crypto/hpke`'s `MLKEM768X25519`), and one for Ed25519, which signs what the member's Dens makes. Both come from their DM seal by HKDF, salted with the den's ID, each under a label of its own, so every device holding the seal holds the identity with nothing new to hand over, and no den sees what the member is at another. The service makes them from the seal as it uses them and clears them after. A member who starts over gets a new identity with their new seal.
- A member's Dens publishes the identity's two public keys, 1,248 bytes, as it signs in, where the den lacks them or holds others; a Dens without the seal, as after a recovery code until the member types it, publishes nothing. A member's record carries the identity's hash (`identity`), SHA-256 of the hash of its X-Wing key and its Ed25519 key, so a change reaches everyone as `member.updated`. The keys themselves come on request, a few members at a time, as a Dens first needs them: as it makes a DM's key. Leaving the den takes the identity, as does starting over, and the member's Dens publishes it again as they come back.
- A member's Dens keeps the identity the den gives for each member the first time it needs it, for the members it shares DMs with and any it compares codes with, trusting it on first use as SSH does a host's key, and checks every one it meets after against what it kept. One that differs is a change: the Dens keeps the new one, the old among those kept before, and says so wherever that member shows (see Changes).

**Codes (M16.1)**

- A member's code is 20 digits in groups of four, made from their identity's hash, their ID and the den's ID by 5,200 rounds of SHA-512, each round taking the identity again, as Signal makes its safety numbers; four chunks of five bytes, each modulo 100,000, make five digits each. An identity made to match one takes about 10^20 tries of those. It's the same for everyone who compares with that member, who reads it out once to a group or a call.
- Comparing is each member's choice, at any time, from the profile beside a DM, a member's profile card and the DM's lock, and from M16.2 and M16.3 a call's lock and a group's members: the member's own code, to read out, and a box for the other's, typed as they read it out, so a check can't pass by copying one's own screen. A match marks the other verified, with a circled check beside their name, as Appearance's Verified status shows it (see In the app), until their identity changes; digits that don't match say someone may be in the middle. Each compares the other. The dialog says only what to do, and names nobody, since a member's name could read as part of its words; its i opens the guide, in a new tab, which says where to compare and why.
- Comparing sends nothing: the code comes from the identity the Dens kept, and the mark stays with the member, so the den doesn't learn who compares, or when, which would tell it whose DMs it could tamper with unseen. Manage's Your code shows the member's own.
- The guide says where to compare, best first: in person; a voice or video call; another den one of them owns, never the den the DM is on, even when it's theirs, since whoever tampered with it could change the digits sent there too; several accounts on other services they know are really the other's; a den neither of them owns, whose owner could change what's there. It also says why: a tampered den can set up an impostor elsewhere ("I'm @Y on Twitter, let's compare there"), and hosting your own den narrows that risk, but nothing removes it.

**The record (M16.1)**

- What a member's Dens kept, and which members they verified, is their record: for each member, the hash of the identity kept, when, whether it's verified, whether DMs with them hold, the identities kept before, eight at most, whose keys still open what was sealed under them, and when it changed, sixteen times at most; and from M16.3 the member's own identities before they started over, eight at most, which signed the changes to their groups they made before. Each device keeps it sealed with the data key, and on the den sealed with the member's seal, bound to the den, the member and the revision it was written at, its length first and padded to a power of four from 16 KiB, so the den learns little from its size. Past 256 KiB, the oldest of what it kept before go, then the oldest changes, then the members kept longest ago who aren't verified.
- Each of the member's connected devices writes it every hour, from a moment picked at random, whether it changed or not, merging what the others wrote, over the revision it merged; one written meanwhile is a conflict, which it merges and writes over in turn. The den tells the member's sessions of each revision, so their other devices merge it at once: a comparison reaches the member's other devices within the hour, while the den sees each device write once an hour whatever happened. A development instance writes it every 20 seconds, for the browser checks.
- Two devices that kept the same identity for a member agree on it: verified where either compared, held only while both are. Two that kept different ones show it as a change: the one kept later stands, the other joins those kept before, and where the member had verified the older, the DM holds.
- Starting over takes the record from the den, since the old seal sealed it; the device that started over keeps its own copy, and writes it sealed with the new seal.

**Changes (M16.1)**

- When a member's identity changes, every DM with them shows a line where it did, "Bob's keys changed", with Compare, and their mark goes. Where the member had verified them, the DM holds: in the composer's place, their keys changed since the two compared, with Compare and Send anyway, and the service sends nothing until one is chosen. The member's own devices show where they started over, as before M16.1.

**DM keys (M16.1)**

1. A DM's key is 32 random bytes, made by either member's Dens when the DM has no live key both can use: before its first message, after a member started over, or after an identity changed. It's sealed with HPKE to each member's identity, bound to the den, the DM, a random 16-byte ID its maker gives it, and the member.
2. Its statement names the den, the DM, the key's ID, a commitment to the key, its maker, and each member with the hash of the identity it was sealed to, and its maker's identity signs it. Anyone can seal a copy to an identity, so the commitment, SHA-256 of the key and its ID, keeps the den from setting a copy of another key beside a member's statement.
3. A Dens uses a key only once the signature checks against its maker's identity, the one it kept for them or its own, and the statement names the identity it kept for each member: for sending, the one kept now; for reading, any it kept, since what was sealed before a change still opens. So a den that gave one side keys of its own must give both, and a key that names another identity for this member says someone may be in the middle. The key carries its maker's keys, the hash of the X-Wing key and the Ed25519 key, which hash to the identity named for its maker, so a key from before its maker's identity changed still checks.
4. A DM has one live key: two made at once see `409 key_exists`, and the slower takes the live one; one made after an identity changed retires the live key, whose statement names an identity no longer published, as replaced. The den checks a key names the identities published now, and its maker's signature, which keeps honest clients in step.
5. So a DM opens at once, with nothing to type, and a member whose Dens is away reads it once back. Its messages, files and reactions are sealed under its key as before M16.1.

**The DMs from before M16.1**

- A DM's key both members checked before M16.1 stays live, both sending and reading under it, each member's copy sealed with their seal, until one of them starts over; then the DM gets a key sealed to identities. Those comparisons checked DM keys, not identities, so nobody starts verified.
- Before M16.1 a DM's key came from an exchange of one-time keys between the two members' Dens, in commit-first order, as approval's does, whose 32-digit check code each member typed the other's half of. An exchange still running at the update, or checked by one member alone, retired as M16.1's migration ran (`restarted`, by whoever started it), so its DM's next message makes a key the new way; what the member who checked sent under it stays theirs alone, as it would have.
- The den starts no exchange for a DM from M16.1 on (`400 invalid_field`), so an older Dens can't start a key a newer one couldn't use.
- An older Dens publishes no identity, so a DM with its member that needs a new key waits, saying their Dens needs an update, while one whose key both members checked goes on both ways. On a den older than M16.1, which keeps no identities, a newer Dens does the same. An older Dens never sees a key sealed to identities, which it would take for the den breaking the protocol, so messages under one don't open there until it updates.

**Messages and photos**

- The local service encrypts a DM's text with the DM key, bound to the channel, the author, the message's nonce and the key's ID, before sending, and decrypts what arrives before the page sees it. The browser never holds a key.
- The den keeps doing everything that doesn't need the text: ordering, history pages, edits with revisions, deletes, read state and unread counts (in a DM every message counts, so the den needn't read one), typing and closing.
- Photos: the sending service strips metadata and makes the thumbnail itself, with the same code the den uses for channel uploads, then encrypts the original and the thumbnail with a fresh key per file. The file's key, dimensions and type travel inside the encrypted message. The den stores two opaque blobs and counts their size against the upload limits.
- Reactions (M15.3): each member's reactions on a message are one set, which their service seals with the DM's live key, padded to one length, and the den keeps whole against its revision; the den learns that a member's reactions changed, and when, never what they are (see Reactions).
- A DM has no polls (M15.4): the den counts a poll's votes, which a DM's would have sealed from it, and no @everyone, which is staff's in a channel.
- Work that moves to the client: reply quotes (the client decrypts the original itself), search, and compact links. Task checkboxes (M1.6) tick as an ordinary edit against the current revision.
- A call's frames are sealed under keys of their own for each call, apart from the DM's (see Calls).

**Group DMs (M16.3)**

- *What they are.* A group DM is a DM of three to ten members of the den, as Discord's group DMs are. Any member makes one from the DM list's + New group, picking two to nine others, or from a DM's profile with Add people, which makes a group of the two and those added and leaves the DM as it is. It may have a name, which any member changes; one without is named by its other members, the first two and how many more past three. Its member pane, where a DM shows the other member's profile, lists its members in the order they joined, each with their mark or Compare, and Add people, Rename and Leave.
- *Who changes it.* Any member adds others, up to ten in all, each a member of the den whose Dens publishes an identity. The first of its members in the order they joined removes others: the one who made it, and once they've left, the one who's been in it longest. Anyone leaves. A member who leaves the den, or is removed or banned, leaves its groups, and a group nobody is left in goes, with its messages. A member removed or gone loses the group from their list: it's no longer theirs to see.
- *Changes the den can't make.* Each change is a statement its maker's identity signs: the group made, with its members; members added; a member removed or leaving; a new name. Each names its number in the group's changes, the hash of the change before it, and the group's members after it, in the order they joined, so the den keeps them in order and can't fork them. Every member's Dens checks each change against the members it had: that it follows the one before, by the group's rules, and that its signer, a member who could make it, is the identity it kept for them, keeping it on first use as for a DM. It knows the group's members from its changes alone: someone the den lists without a member's signed add gets no key and shows nowhere. The den's own changes are removals of members who left the den, which take nobody in. A change that doesn't check marks the group broken: its member's Dens makes no key for it and sends nothing there, and the page says someone may be in the middle. One signed by an identity the Dens can't place, as a member's before they started over, which it never met, is checked again from the group's start as the record brings the identities the member's other devices kept: the record holds, beside those, the member's own identities before they started over. A member's Dens knows a group from the add that took them in, which names its members then, and checks its changes once it read the record since its latest snapshot. WhatsApp's servers could once add members to groups, since its changes weren't signed (Rösler, Mainka and Schwenk, 2018).
- *Its first change.* Every statement binds the den's ID for the group, as a DM key's binds the DM's, so a group is reserved for its maker first, a channel nobody sees until they make it with its first change, its first key and its name. A member who reserved one and didn't make it gets it again, and one reserved an hour ago goes as the next is reserved.
- *Keys.* A group has a key for each membership. The member whose change makes a membership, the group made or members added or removed, makes its key, 32 random bytes sealed to each member's identity and signed, as a DM's key is, and sends it with the change. After a member leaves, or the den takes one out, the live key retires, and the first remaining member's Dens to send makes the next. A member's new identity makes a new key too, as in a DM. A member's Dens sends under a group's key only where its statement names the group's members as its changes make them, with the identities kept now. A member added holds the keys from their joining on, as in Signal and WhatsApp: what the others wrote before, they wrote before that member was there to read it, and the den shows them the group from their joining. One removed holds no key made after.
- *Messages.* Sealed under the group's live key as a DM's are, and signed by their author's identity inside what's sealed, since with a key every member holds, a member and the den together could otherwise write as another: the sealed text starts with its signer's ID, their identity's keys in 64 bytes, and the signature, over the message's binding and its text. An edit, or a tick, is signed by whoever made it, the author or one of the members they named; a message whose signer its key doesn't name, or who isn't its author or an editor, shows as one that doesn't open. Each member's set of reactions is signed the same way, at a DM's sealed size. Files, replies, edits, deletes, tasks and read state work as in a DM, and every message counts toward each other member's unread count and notifies as a DM's does, under Direct messages, its title the group's name; no polls, and no @everyone. A member's reactions stay on the messages they reacted to after they leave, as a member's messages do, up to twenty members' sets a message.
- *The name* is sealed under one of the group's keys, padded to one length, so the den never reads it, nor its length; a naming seals it under the live key, and each new key carries it again, so a member added reads it. A name this Dens can't open, under a key it doesn't hold, stays as the den has it.
- *What the den keeps.* A group DM is a channel of kind `group_dm`, with its members in the order they joined, each with the change that took them in and the group's last message as they joined, and its changes in order, each a member's statement and signature; its keys are in the DMs' tables, which keep a copy for each member. M16.3's migration made `den_channels` again to take the fourth kind (see Versioning and compatibility).
- *Older Dens.* An older Dens closes its connection, as the den breaking the protocol, on a channel of more than two members or a message with more than two members' sets of reactions, so group DMs come with version 2 of the protocol, which the den serves beside 1: a Dens speaking 1 gets nothing of them, in `ready`, events or answers, and an older device of a member in groups goes on without them.

**Notes to self (M17.3)**

- *What they are.* Each member has notes in each den: a conversation with themselves, which only their devices read, for what they want at hand on each of them. They're pinned at the top of the Direct messages tab, and open from Notes on the member's own profile card too. Text, files, replies, edits, deletes and ticks work as in a DM. Reactions, polls, mentions, others' editing, spoilers and disappearing times don't, and nothing in them waits to be read or notifies, since all of it is the member's own; they don't close. The den's retention, and the member's upload limit, apply as to a DM.
- *Their key* is made from the member's DM seal by HKDF, salted with the den's ID, under a label of its own (`dens-notes-v1`), as the identity is. Every device holding the seal has it, with nothing to make, keep or hand over, where a key sealed to the member's own identity would be one more thing kept on the den, and the den holds nothing of it. A note is sealed as a DM's message is, under that binding, naming its key by the notes' channel ID; each file under a key of its own, carried in the sealed text, as in a DM.
- *What the den keeps.* A channel of kind `notes` naming its member, one each, made as they first open it (`POST /api/notes`), which only they see; it takes sealed messages alone, each naming the channel's ID as its key. M17.3's migration made `den_channels` again to take the fifth kind (see Versioning and compatibility). A member who leaves the den, or is removed, takes their notes with them, files and all, since nobody else could read them.
- *Starting over.* A new seal makes a new key, so the notes from before no longer open: they show as lost, as old DMs do, for the member to delete. A Dens can't tell a note sealed under an old seal from one the den changed, so either shows as lost; both are gone for good.
- *Older Dens.* An older Dens closes its connection, as the den breaking the protocol, on a channel of a kind it doesn't know, so notes come with version 3 of the protocol, which the den serves beside 1 and 2: a Dens speaking either gets nothing of them, in `ready`, events, answers or the change log, and a member's older devices show nothing of their notes until they update. The page offers Notes once its Dens and the den both speak 3.

**Calls (M16.2)**

- *Every call sealed.* On a den with M16.2 every call is end-to-end encrypted: each member's page seals every frame its encoders make, their voice's, their screen's and their share's sound's, before it's sent, and opens every frame it receives before it's decoded, with WebRTC's Encoded Transform (`RTCRtpScriptTransform`) in a worker of the page's own, `sframe.worker.js`. The den's SFU forwards each packet's payload under its own headers, as it always has, budgets them by size alone and asks for keyframes over RTCP, so it hears and sees nothing of the calls it carries.
- *The frames.* SFrame (RFC 9605), in its AES-128-GCM suite (`AES_128_GCM_SHA256_128`, 0x0004), with WebCrypto: each frame is its header, the key's ID and a counter in the fewest bytes, then the frame sealed with 16 bytes of tag, the header as the AEAD's data. A member's key goes by a number, the first two bytes of its ID, and each of their tracks by a key ID of its own from it, four times the number and the track's: 0 the voice, 1 the screen, 2 the share's sound. SFrame derives each key ID's AES key and salt from the member's key with it, so no two tracks share a counter. A frame that doesn't open, names a counter its key had among the last 1,024, a key the page lacks or another track's key ID, or comes in the clear, is dropped. A frame with nothing in it, as Opus's DTX hands the transform between its packets of silence, goes on empty, which the browser sends no packet for, as it wouldn't without a transform. Sealing costs a voice about 22 bytes a frame, some 9 kbps at 50 frames a second. The page's tests take RFC 9605's own cases: its 289 headers and its frame under AES_128_GCM_SHA256_128 (`internal/ui/test/testdata/rfc9605.json`).
- *Keys.* A member's Dens makes their key for a call, 16 random bytes, as they join, and a new one whenever anyone joins or leaves, as the den's `voice.state` shows the call, at most one every half second, so a burst of joins and leaves makes one: one who joins can't open what the den might have kept from before, nor one who left what comes after. It seals the key to each other member's identity with HPKE, as a DM's key is, bound to the den, the channel, a random 16-byte ID its maker gives it, and the member, and signs a statement naming the den, the channel, the key's ID and a commitment to it, its maker, and each member it sealed to, with their identity's hash. Copies, commitments and statements have contexts of their own, so none passes for a DM's. The identities come from the den on request as members join, each checked against the one kept for its member, kept on first use as for a DM; a change shows as for a DM (see Changes), and the call seals to the new one, showing it beside them, where a DM would hold.
- *Passing them on.* A key goes over the den socket as `voice.key`, with each member's copy and its maker's identity in 64 bytes, as a DM's key carries it. The den passes each member their copy alone, while both are in the call, and keeps the latest from each while the call lasts, so a member gets theirs as their call starts, as when they move it to another device, or is taken back. A Dens takes another's key only once its statement names this member's own identity, its signature checks against the identity kept for its maker, and its copy opens to what the statement commits to. Each Dens makes its keys afresh as its call is taken back, and they go with the call. `voice.key` has a limit of its own, 30 in 10 seconds a member, which a Dens's one every half second stays within; a key for 29 others, each copy 1,152 bytes, fits in a client's frame.
- *The page's keys.* The member's Dens hands its page the keys: the browser holds a call's keys, and only while it lasts, where it holds no other. The service sends the page the call's keys whole each time one changes, its own and each other member's, without those of members who left, since the page's stream drops what it can't take, and the page asks for them again (`voice.keys`) on a frame under a key it lacks, at most twice a second. A page sends under its own new key a second after it gets it, while the call's other members' Dens pass it to their pages, and opens a member's last key's frames for ten seconds after their new one comes, as it does a member's who left.
- *Whom it seals to.* Every member of the call as the page shows it: the den chooses who's in a call, so a member it added in secret would have to show among them to hear a thing.
- *The soundboard* is the den's own track, which it plays its sounds on, so it stays in the clear: the page opens nothing on the `soundboard` stream, passing its frames as they are, and takes nothing in the clear on any other.
- *Browsers.* Firefox has had `RTCRtpScriptTransform` since 117 and Chromium since 141; Chromium's older `createEncodedStreams` is left out, since every target browser has the newer. On a den that seals its calls the page joins one only where the browser has it, and otherwise says why; Android's WebView has it from 141, so below that the page asks for a newer WebView, with a link to its page in the store, as the app does below its floor of 111. The worker is a module beside the voice worklet, named to the page as the worklet is (`data-sframe-worker`), and the CSP needs nothing more: a worker from the page's own assets falls under `script-src 'self'`.
- *A device without the seal*, as after a recovery code until the member types it, can't open a call's keys, so on a den that seals its calls the page asks for the seal before it joins, with Enter it.
- *The call's lock.* Beside how a call stands, on its channel's row in the list, the phone's bar, Home's box, and the list's block when the call is in another den, a lock says the call is end-to-end encrypted, and opens the member's code to read out and each other member of the call, with their mark or Compare, which compares in place; a comparison made there holds in every call, DM and group with that member after. The call's list marks each member verified, and one whose keys changed since this member verified them, or since the call began, with a warning whose tip says when. On an older den, which doesn't seal calls, the lock is open and says so.
- *Older Dens.* The den's info says it seals calls (`sealed_calls`); on an older den, which doesn't, a call goes unsealed, as it did. A den with M16.2 seals every call: it refuses a `voice.join` that doesn't say it seals (`"sealed": true`) with `update_needed`, since an older page would send in the clear, which every other page drops, and would hear nothing; an older page shows it as a call that failed. A page that couldn't load the den's state joins as one that doesn't seal, which a den that seals refuses; one whose den came to seal its calls since it joined, as by the update, which ended the call as the den restarted, joins it again sealed.
- *What's logged.* By ID alone, never bytes: each key a Dens makes, with how many members it was sealed to, and each it opens or refuses, with why, at debug, refusals at warn; the den, at debug, whom each key went to; and in the page's notes, the frames it sealed, opened and dropped, by count.

**History and starting over**

- A new device gets the seal from the device that approves it, makes the member's identity from it, fetches their DM keys, and reads their DM history. A member signing in with a recovery code, with no other device, types their saved seal.
- DM keys don't change on their own. Keeping history costs forward secrecy: whoever gets a device, or the seal, can read what that member can. Signal makes the opposite choice, giving new devices no old messages; Dens keeps history, since members expect their DMs on every device, as with channels.
- A member who has lost their seal and every device starts over: their Dens makes a new seal, and with it a new identity, and the DM history sealed for the old ones stays unreadable to them, while their partners keep theirs, as do their notes from before (M17.3). Each of their DMs gets a new key, sealed to the new identity, with its next message, and the others see their keys changed. Their own devices show a divider where they started over.
- Starting over takes the den password, so a stolen session can't wipe a member's DM keys. It signs out their other devices there, since those hold the old seal, and they come back through approval. The new seal is the one the install starts new dens with from then on.
- A former member who joins again with another seal than the one they left with starts over the same way; with the same one, their identity, keys and history are as they left them.
- Starting over is also how a member shuts out a stolen device, which holds the seal: signing it out stops it fetching from the den, and a new seal keeps what's written afterwards from it.

Each opens what it lists, so a den channel never sits open beside the DM list, nor a DM beside the channels, and nobody writes in a channel thinking it's a DM (M11). The DM list still puts the most recently active first. The phone layout's list stays on screen as a tab opens what it lists, which shows beside it.

Their header says they're end-to-end encrypted, with nothing beside it to open, and their start that only the member's devices read them; a device without the seal says so in the composer's place, with Enter it (M17.3).

**The phone layout (M13)**

- *Which windows.* Below 768 pixels, Tailwind's `md`, the page has the phone layout, whatever the form: a phone's, and a narrow desktop window's, beside another or in a tiling window manager, which gets a layout that fits it rather than a squeezed desktop one. How it's worked follows the pointer: touch's ways for a coarse one, hover for one that hovers. A phone held sideways, or a tablet, wider than that has the desktop's layout, worked by touch.
- *Three panes.* A den's page lays its list, its channel or DM, and its members, or beside a DM the other member's profile, and beside a group DM its members (M16.3), in a row, one on screen at a time (M13.1). The side panes take 85% of the width, so the channel shows at their edge, dimmed, and a tap there goes back to it, as phone chat apps do. A pane off screen is `inert`, so focus and a screen reader stay on the one shown. The channel header's ☰ and people buttons move between them, the ☰ marked with what waits elsewhere in the den. A wider window shows the three side by side, the members by their toggle, which the browser keeps.
- *Swipes.* The row scrolls sideways and snaps to each pane (CSS scroll snapping): the browser does the gesture, its momentum and its feel at the ends, and the page goes where it settles, watching `scrollend`, or a pause in scrolling in a WebView older than Chromium 114. A settle counts as a swipe only when the member moved the row: while the page slides it to a pane, as Back or a button asks, it settles elsewhere only as a late snap does, which Firefox's can, until it arrives or the member touches it. The channel stops a fling (`scroll-snap-stop`), so a fling from the list rests there rather than at the members. Anything that scrolls sideways itself, as a block of code, keeps a swipe that starts on it while it can scroll, as nested scrollers do. Android's gesture navigation takes a swipe from the screen's edge for Back, which goes up a level as a swipe to the right does, so a swipe starts a little in from the edge, as for any Android app's drawer. In a narrow window a touchpad's sideways swipe moves the panes too.
- *Back.* Each entry the page puts in its history holds its trail: the screens from Home, the history's root, to the one the entry shows. On the phone layout a den's list, its channel and its members are each a level; a wider window shows them together, so a den is one level there, its channel's. Each dialog, the settings and Manage among them, and the viewer, is a layer over the screen it opens on. Back closes what's on top, and otherwise goes up a level: from the members to the channel, from the channel to the list, from the list to Home. A move goes back through the history to where the trail shown and the one it asks for part, then forward a step at a time, so Back walks up the trail whatever brought the page there: a channel opened from the list goes above it, one opened by a link or a notification takes the place of the one shown, and one in another den goes above that den's list. A dialog closed by its ✕, Escape or a click outside it takes its entry back, and Manage with changes unsaved asks before Back closes it, as before its ✕ does. A page opened at a channel, as a notification or a link opens one, puts Home and the den's list beneath it. Back at Home leaves the page: the Android app closes, or goes behind the others during a call, and a browser's tab goes to what it showed before. The address names the channel, and the entry the pane, so Forward, a reload and a notification's click each show what they name; a reload drops the dialogs its entries held. The list's own entry keeps the den's address alone, and shows behind it the channel last shown, or what the tab picked there.
- *Between dens.* Home stays the way between dens, Back from a den's list, as on the desktop another den is Home and Open away. Its rows say what waits in each den: its mentions, counted, or a dot for unread messages, and the list's Home button is marked while another den has something. The service counts each den's channels and DMs with unread messages and their mentions, DMs' messages among them, as it counts them for the den's own page, in the dens list it gives every page, which it sends again as reading or a message changes them.
- *A long press.* On a touch screen, at any width, a message has no ⋯, and its text keeps the 32 pixels the ⋯'s column took: a long press on it opens its actions, as phone chat apps do (M13.2), and on the phone layout its quick reactions with them (M15.3). A long press on a reaction's chip says who reacted, and isn't the message's. A press held half a second within 10 pixels is a long one; one that moves first, as a scroll or a swipe between panes does, or that the browser takes over, isn't, and the click a browser may send as it's let go goes nowhere, nor onto the sheet opened under the finger. The long press takes the place of the browser's own selection and menu on a message, so copying part of one waits for a Select text, should members miss it. A mouse and the keyboard keep the ⋯, as a call's members keep theirs, in lists with room for them. A list's channels and groups have no ⋯ on a touch screen either: staff open a channel's or a group's settings with a long press on its row, as on a message (M13.4).
- *Sheets.* On the phone layout the page's menus open as sheets from the screen's foot, with rows of 48 pixels: a message's actions, headed by its author and its time in full, when it was edited and who else can edit it, so what a hover shows of a message is a long press away, with a row of quick reactions, each 48 pixels square, above them (M15.3); the composer's +; the menu of a member in a call, with their volume; and the emoji picker (M15.2), its emoji in tiles 44 pixels high, nine to a row. A sheet's head, its handle and its title, stays at its top while a long sheet scrolls, so the picker's search and its buttons, which are its title, stay in reach (M15.2). A tap above a sheet closes it, as do a swipe down past a third of its height or a flick, Back, since it takes an entry in the history, Escape, and its rows' actions. A wider window keeps its menus, touch or not, and there a long press opens the message's menu beside it.
- *Targets.* Nothing a finger or a mouse aims at is under 24 pixels each way, WCAG 2.2's minimum, but words within a line of text, which WCAG leaves to the text's size. With a coarse pointer a list's rows are 44 pixels tall, a menu's items, channels, DMs, members, those in a call and those who may edit a message among them, and small buttons that stand clear of others take room around them to 44 pixels without growing to the eye (`touch-room`, an `::after` around the button).
- *What hover shows.* A tip opens on hover, keyboard focus or a tap, and a tap's tip stays until the next tap, on it or anywhere else. It opens within the window, and within a list around it that scrolls, which would cut it off, moved in from an edge it would run past and narrowed to fit, and above where there's no room below. A guide's i, a link to dens.chat, opens its tip on a tap and the guide on the next. Marks whose meaning a title held get tips: the den's state, staff only's lock and a muted bell beside a channel or a DM, which stand beside its row's button rather than in it, the button reaching under them across the row, a member's verified mark (M16.1), the marks of a call's members, LIVE where it does nothing else, the call's state, and who watches a share. A touch screen shows a share's controls in the player, and a video in the list shows its own on a tap, as on a click. A title that repeats a button's label stays a title.
- *Touch's hints.* Where a hint names keys, a touch screen gets its own: the writing guide says the button sends and ↵ starts a line, an edit says nothing of keys beside its Save and Cancel, and comparing a photo's copies says to hold a finger on the picture to see the other one, which does it.
- *The composer.* With a coarse pointer ↵ starts a line, since a phone's keyboard has no Shift, and a send button of 48 pixels shows beside the box once there's something to send; pressing it leaves the keyboard up. Ctrl+Enter still sends, as from a tablet's keyboard, and a window with a mouse keeps Enter sending. The box asks the keyboard for a return key, or a send key where Enter sends (`enterkeyhint`). While a touch screen's keyboard is up the box grows to 5 lines rather than 8, so the list keeps room, and what stands above it, a reply, files and who else may edit, scrolls within 30% of the window's height. ## Voice and screen share

The browser does all client-side media with standard APIs, and the den runs a Pion SFU that forwards packets without decoding. In the client role, the service only relays signaling and never touches media; the Pion SFU runs only when the same install is also acting as a den.

**Calls (M2)**

- Calls happen in voice channels. Clicking one joins its call: the member's browser opens one `RTCPeerConnection` to the den, which carries their microphone to the den and everyone else's audio back.
- Anyone who can see a voice channel can join its call, and sees who is in it, listed by name, and who is muted. A staff-only voice channel's call is staff's, as its text channels are.
- A member is in one call at a time, on one device: joining from another device, or in another channel, moves them there. An install holds one call, which belongs to the page that joined it, so closing or reloading that page leaves the call.
- The den ends a member's call as soon as they can't see its channel: they leave or are removed or banned, the device is signed out, a role change hides a staff-only channel, or the channel is deleted.
- A call rides out a dropped connection (M3): the den holds it for 30 seconds after its socket closes, and the page takes it back once it reconnects (see Riding out a dropped connection). A den restart ends every call, and the page joins again once the den is back, trying a few times before it gives up and says why.
- Mute is the member's own: the page stops sending their microphone's sound, and the others see the mark. So is deafen (M3.3): the page plays nothing of the call and mutes the member, and the others see that they hear nothing either, beside the muted mark, since deafening mutes too (M11). Hearing again puts the microphone back as it was, and unmuting while deafened means hearing again too.
- Each member sets how loud everyone else plays for them (M3), from 0 to 100%, in the call's list. The browser keeps it by den and member, as it keeps the microphone and speaker, so someone turned down stays down in the next call, and the list shows the level beside anyone below full. It's the volume of the media element that plays them, so it stops at 100%: more would mean playing their audio through Web Audio instead. Android's app plays them through Web Audio already (M12, see android.md's Calls), at the same levels. From M12 the voice settings' call volume turns everyone down at once, under their own levels, and the browser keeps it too: a phone's loudspeaker plays a call loud even at its lowest call volume.
- Staff can disconnect a member of a lower rank from a call, or mute them, which the den enforces (M3; see Staff in calls).
- Speaking indicators (M3): each page measures the audio it plays for each member, and its own microphone's as sent, and rings the avatar of whoever is speaking. Only members in the call see them, since only their pages play its audio. From M11 a member holding push to talk rings too, sound or not, which the den tells the call's members alone (see Voice controls).
- A voice channel's call holds 15 members, and a den's calls 30 in all, unless the owner sets other caps (M4.2; see Limits for calls). Each member's audio goes out once to every other member of their call, so the den's upload grows with the square of a call's size: a full call of 15 sends about 3 Mbps while two people talk, and up to 24 if everyone does (see the bandwidth table).
- Each voice channel has a bitrate, which staff set in its settings like the rest of the channel (M4.2): from 16 to 128 kbps in steps of 8, and 96 for a new channel. The den writes it into every offer for the channel's call, so a change reaches the call in progress with each member's next offer. A lower bitrate costs the den less upload: each member talking sends it to every other member of the call.
- Calls are end-to-end encrypted from M16.2: each member's page seals its frames with SFrame before they leave it, under keys their Dens seals to the call's other members, so the den forwards what it can't open (see End-to-end encryption's Calls). Media is encrypted between each browser and the den as well (DTLS-SRTP). On an older den calls go unsealed, as the call's lock says.
- DMs have no calls: calls stay in voice channels until members ask for calls of their own (see loose-ideas.md).
- The den's offer, which carries the fingerprint its DTLS must match, comes over the den socket, so a call reaches the den the member signed in to, as their messages do.
- Members in a call never see each other's addresses: everything passes through the den, which sees the address each member's media comes from, as it sees the address of each of their connections already.

**Client side (browser)**

- Mic: `getUserMedia` with `echoCancellation`, `noiseSuppression` and `autoGainControl` on. The member picks the microphone and the speaker in the call's settings, the speaker through `setSinkId`.
- Opus at up to the voice channel's bitrate, 96 kbps unless staff set another (M3, M4.2), where a browser left to itself sends 32: every offer from the den asks for it (`maxaveragebitrate`), and a browser takes a new offer's parameters for its encoder. Pion builds each offer after the first from the parameters the browser answered with, which don't ask for it, so the den writes its own into every offer.
- Screen: `getDisplayMedia`, which goes through xdg-desktop-portal and PipeWire on Wayland (M4.2; see Screen share).
- The peer connection names no STUN or TURN servers, so a call contacts nothing but the den.
- The page offers calls only where `RTCPeerConnection` exists, and screen share only where `getDisplayMedia` does too, and says so where the browser can't, from what the browser has rather than what its user agent says (M9.1): GNOME Web has had no WebRTC since WebKitGTK 2.54, and Android's WebView, which says it's Linux, has no `getDisplayMedia`. On a den that seals its calls it joins one only where `RTCRtpScriptTransform` exists too, which seals and opens each frame in a worker of the page's own (M16.2, see End-to-end encryption's Calls).
- The page's `Permissions-Policy` grants the microphone and screen capture to the page itself (`microphone=(self)`, and `display-capture=(self)` from M4.2). The camera stays off.
- A page its browser froze plays its call again as it thaws (M12): freezing pauses a page's media elements, and nothing plays them again, so the page plays them on `resume`. Chromium freezes a hidden page a minute after its sound stops, which Android's app keeps from happening to its call's page (see android.md).
- Noise suppression (M3): browsers' own suppressor takes out steady noise such as fans and hum, but not keyboards, a TV or other voices. So RNNoise runs instead unless a member turns it off: Xiph's small noise-suppression network (BSD), compiled to WebAssembly and run in an AudioWorklet between the microphone and the call, at 48 kHz in 10 ms frames. The browser still cancels echo first, since that needs the raw microphone and what the browser plays. Only one suppressor runs at a time, so the browser's is off (`noiseSuppression: false`) while RNNoise is on, and the encoder gets the cleaned audio. Others' audio keeps playing through media elements, which the echo canceller hears; in Android's app, through an audio context, and a phone's own echo canceller hears what the phone plays (M12). The browser keeps the choice, as it keeps the microphone and speaker, and a change applies mid-call by replacing the track the call sends, without a new offer. Where RNNoise can't start, as in a browser that won't run its audio, the call goes on with the browser's own suppressor, and the call's settings say so; the member's choice stands, for the next call or another browser.
- `scripts/rnnoise.sh` builds RNNoise with the wasi-sdk and binaryen the media module pins, and writes the module into the page's assets. Its code is RNNoise 0.2's release, which matches the v0.2 tag's file for file; its model is the one that tag names, since the release carries another, without the float copies the model keeps for debugging. RNNoise's generic vector code, which WebAssembly builds take, includes Opus's `os_support.h`, which RNNoise doesn't carry, so `internal/ui/rnnoise` supplies the one macro it needs. The module is committed, as the media module is: CI builds it again and fails if it differs.
- The module is 1.47 MB and imports nothing. The page loads and compiles it only when a member turns RNNoise on, and posts the compiled module to the worklet, which every target browser takes. Compiling WebAssembly takes `'wasm-unsafe-eval'` in the page's CSP, which allows WebAssembly but not `eval`, and gives a script in the page nothing it couldn't do already; under today's CSP every target browser refuses to compile it.
- It costs about 220 µs of one core per 10 ms frame in every target browser, about 2%, and adds 30 ms to a call: RNNoise's own 20 ms, and 10 ms of gathering 480-sample frames from the browser's 128-sample blocks. It takes steady noise down by about 50 dB and keyboard clicks by 20, and keeps other voices, since it tells speech from noise, not one speaker from another. Dens uses the regular model: RNNoise's "little" one costs 40% less and is half the size, but does less on steady noise, and 2% of a core is cheap. The spike that measured all this is `spikes/README.md` at commit c5a882b.

**Signaling**

The page signals over its event socket to its local service, which relays on the den socket. The den makes every offer and the page only answers, so the two never offer at once:

1. The page asks its local service to join a voice channel, and the service sends `voice.join` to the den.
2. The den sets up its side and sends an offer: one audio section that receives the member's microphone, and one that sends each other member's audio, labeled with their member ID.
3. The local service checks the offer, replaces any candidates it carries with the den's media addresses (see below), and hands it to the page that joined.
4. The page attaches its microphone and answers. The local service takes the browser's candidates out of the answer, and from M8 the addresses in its connection lines, and passes it to the den.
5. Media flows directly between the browser and the den, over UDP or, where UDP is blocked, TCP, encrypted with DTLS-SRTP.
6. When someone joins or leaves the call, the den sends each other member a new offer, which adds or retires that member's section. One offer is outstanding per member at a time, and one not answered within 15 seconds ends that member's call.
7. When the page's connection to the media ports breaks, as on a move to another network, it asks for an ICE restart (M3). The den's next offer carries new ICE credentials, the local service writes the den's addresses into it afresh, and the browser checks its new paths. DTLS carries on, so the call keeps its keys and its sections.

The messages are in [protocol.md](protocol.md#voice-m2).

**Riding out a dropped connection (M3)**

A call's signaling and its media take different paths: the den socket runs from the member's local service through Caddy, and the media from their browser straight to the den's media ports. Losing one needn't end the call.

- When a call's socket closes, the den holds the call for 30 seconds. The member stays in it, their audio flows both ways if its path still does, and offers wait. That covers a Caddy reload, a blip that broke only the socket, and at least five of the client's reconnects.
- The page keeps its peer connection while its den is away, and asks to resume the call once the den is back. The den moves the held call to the new socket, which must be the same device's, and makes a new offer if the call changed meanwhile. The others notice nothing but a gap in that member's audio, if there was one. They see no mark during the hold, since the den can't tell whether the audio still flows.
- A socket the den closes for good ends its call at once: a removal, a ban, a revoked device, a new password, starting over, leaving and logging out all do. Any other close holds the call.
- An offer that was out when the socket closed waits, since Pion can't take an offer back, and goes again once the call is resumed. A page that already answered it sends the same answer again.
- A call that ends while held stays ended, and the resume says why, so a member disconnected by staff in that window isn't brought back by their own page. A call that can't be resumed, because the hold ran out or the den restarted, is joined again from the start.
- When the browser's own connection to the media ports breaks, as when a laptop moves from Wi-Fi to Ethernet or leaves home, the page asks for an ICE restart (step 7 above). The local service looks the den's addresses up again for it, since split DNS can give another address on another network.
- The local service holds nothing of a call across a dropped connection: it passes the page's resume on to the den, and writes the den's addresses into its offers afresh. So a call also rides out the service restarting, as on an update, if it's back within the hold. A service that stops closes its pages' sockets but leaves their calls alone, since only a page that goes away leaves its call.
- Closing or reloading the page ends its call at once, and a call whose media stops answering connectivity checks for 30 seconds ends as failed.

*What's logged (M11).* The logs say which connection broke first, and when: the page's stream to its service, the media path, or the den socket. They name dens, channels, members and pages by ID, never by address, a den by its ID as Manage den shows it, with its name beside its connection's lines.

- In the member's service, at `info`: each close of a page's stream, with how long it was open and why (the page went away or closed it with a code, the connection broke, a ping went unanswered, a write stalled or failed, it fell behind, or the service is stopping); each close of a den connection, with how long it was up and why (the den closed it with a code, the connection broke, a ping went unanswered, a frame didn't decode, or the den broke the protocol); and a call ending because the stream of the page that held it closed. A page whose stream drops while its tab stays open loses its call this way, while the page expects the den to hold it, so it looks like a leave and a join.
- In the den, at `info`: each close of a member's socket, with how long it was open and why, the same causes from the den's side, and the den's own closes with their codes; and, as since M2, each call's start, end and why.
- In the member's service, at `debug`: each den connection coming up, and one that fails to; the call's signaling as it passes (joins and resumes asked for, offers in, answers out, ICE restarts, and the den taking a call back), each with how long it took; and the page's notes, which it sends over its stream: each change in its connection to the den's media ports, with ICE's, each restart it asks for, each time it joins again or gives up, and why; whether its tab is hidden, and from M12 whether its browser froze or thawed it; and at each change and every 10 seconds of a call, a summary from `getStats`: the path's protocol and its candidates' types, packets and bytes each way, packets lost on the way in, jitter and round-trip time, and from M12 what it sent a second since the last, what its encoder aims at, and how far its voice processor runs behind the clock. The service logs a note's fields alone, its words only from fixed lists, so no text the page holds reaches the log, and at most two notes a second from a page after the 64 it may keep while its stream is down, which go once it's back, saying how long before.
- In the den, at `debug`: each call's ICE and DTLS states, its path's protocol and its candidates' types, offers sent, answered with how long it took, and unanswered, and restarts asked for.
- The settings' Logging card downloads the newest 32 MiB of the logs, oldest first, as one text file (`GET /api/logs`, for a paired browser alone, as an attachment not kept), and says what they hold, since an installed Dens keeps them in the service's data directory, out of its desktop user's reach. The service writes out what it holds first, and reads under the lock rotation takes, so no file is renamed under it. A server has no page, so `dens logs` saves the same file into the folder it's run in, and `dens logs --level` sets the level as the card does, over the control endpoint.

**Staff in calls (M3)**

- Staff disconnect members of a lower rank from calls, and mute them, under the rank rules of removal: moderators act on members, and the owner on anyone else.
- A disconnected member can join again at once. One who keeps coming back is removed or banned like anyone else.
- A staff mute makes the den forward nothing from the member, whatever their page sends. It belongs to the member, not the call, so leaving and joining again doesn't shed it. It lasts until staff lift it, the member leaves the den, or the den restarts, since the den keeps it in memory. Everyone who can see the call sees the mark.
- The member's page says what happened, without naming who did it.
- Staff move a member from one call to another (M17.2): with a mouse, by dragging the member's row in a call onto another voice channel, and anywhere by Move to in the member's menu in the call, which lists the den's other voice channels the member could join, a staff-only one only for a member who's staff. The den checks what disconnecting checks, that staff outrank the member, and what joining does, that the member sees the channel and its call has room, then sends `voice.move` to the socket holding the member's call. Their page joins the channel as if they'd picked it, sealed, muted and deafened as they were, which ends the old call, a share with it, as picking another channel does; the den doesn't count that join toward the member's limit on joining. A call held while its socket is away can't be told, so the move is refused, and a Dens from before M17.2 ignores the event, its member staying where they are. A member in no call, or in that channel's already, stays as they are. The page offers it where the den says it moves members (`move_members`).

**Voice controls (M3.3)**

- A member sends their voice one of two ways, chosen in the settings' Voice section. With voice activity, the default, the page sends only while they speak. With push to talk, it sends only while they hold their key. Where the page has the call's button, a touch screen's, its role takes that choice's place (M13.4, see The phone layout).
- Voice activity is automatic by default: it opens for whatever RNNoise, which judges every 10 ms frame, takes for speech. A member can instead set a level with a slider, against a live meter of their microphone, and the page sends what's above it. Without RNNoise, the level is the only way.
- The gate runs in the audio thread on every 10 ms frame, after the noise suppressor, so it opens on a word's first syllable, not up to 100 ms late as a meter on the main thread would. It holds for 300 ms after speech stops, so words' ends and the gaps between them aren't cut, and it fades in and out over a few milliseconds, so it doesn't click. With RNNoise off, the same processor runs without it, for the gate alone. Where the processor can't run at all, the microphone goes as it is, with the browser's own suppressor: voice activity then sends everything, push to talk holds the track back itself, and the settings say so.
- Keys: push to talk, toggle mute (which serves as toggle to talk), and push to mute, which mutes while held. They work while the page has focus, as with any web page, and in other programs through the helper (M7; see Global shortcuts). A key held when the page loses focus counts as released, unless the helper covers it. They don't fire while the member types in a text field, unless the key types nothing there: a function key, or one held with Ctrl, Alt or Meta. A key fires while its own modifiers are held, whatever else is, so a player holding Shift to run still pushes to talk; of two actions on one key, the one with more modifiers takes the press. From M7 a mouse's side button can be a key too, and from M13.4 the call's button presses them on a touch screen.
- What the gate holds back goes as silence, and every offer asks for Opus's discontinuous transmission (`usedtx=1`), so a member who isn't speaking sends a packet every 400 ms instead of 50 a second. That keeps big calls cheap for the den's upload: silent members cost almost nothing.
- Speaking rings follow what's sent, since they measure what each page plays: a member the gate holds back rings nowhere, and the member's own ring lights while their gate is open. A deafened member's page lights no ring, by the meters, push to talk or a sound, since they hear none of it.
- Push to talk rings while the key is held (M11), sound or not, as Discord's does: a member holding it, unmuted, rings for everyone in the call. Their own page lights it at once. For the others, the page tells the den as the key goes down and up (`voice.talk`), and the den passes on who in the call holds it, to the call's members alone (`voice.talking`). The den lights a member's ring at most five times a second, and never while they're muted or staff muted; letting go, muting, leaving the call or the socket closing puts it out, and a page that takes a call back, or joins again, says once more whether the key is down. Voice activity's rings stay as they are, from what each page plays.
- At the foot of a den's channel list, the member's panel shows their picture and name, which open their profile to edit, and the cog for the settings. The panel is as tall as the message bar beside it, which it lines up with, and keeps the channel list's color.
- While in a call, a block sits above the panel and holds the call in a row: the soundboard, where the den has one (M14.3), and buttons to share their screen (M4.2), where the browser can (M13.4), mute, deafen, open the Voice settings and leave, spread evenly. How the call stands shows on the speaker of its channel's row in the list, green once connected and yellow while joining, connecting or reconnecting, with the state in the row's label, as "Lounge, connected"; the block shows a line saying how the call stands only while it isn't connected, or while it's in another den, which the line names, a tap on it going there (M14.3). What the member should know of the call, such as a staff mute, shows in the block above the row, and while they share, the share's controls show there in a row of their own (M4.2). The block and the message bar are a shade lighter than the page, or as light where the page is already white, so a call shows at a glance against the channel list and the panel. On the other pages the call shows boxed, with the channel and how it stands spelled out, and the soundboard to mute's left.
- Settings open as a dialog over whatever the page shows, so a den and a call stay in view. It closes with its ✕, Escape or a click outside it, as every dialog does; a click whose press started inside, as a selection let go past its edge, doesn't count (M11). While the settings wait for a key, Escape or a click elsewhere cancels the wait. Its General section starts with the theme, and its Voice section holds the microphone and speaker, noise suppression, how the member sends, the keys, and how they share their screen (M4.2), which the call's cog opens.
- Joining waits on the microphone, which the browser may first ask the member for, and the call says so after a moment. The voice processor's audio context is made in the click that joins, since a browser may hold back one made after its prompt until another click. Where the service says the page's audio may start without a click, as in Android's app, it's made once the microphone is open, as is the call's, so the phone plays them as a call's (M12). The browser keeps all of these, as it keeps the microphone and speaker, except the keys, which the service keeps from M7 so the helper can watch them.

**Screen share (M4.2)**

A member in a call shares a screen, a window or a browser tab with the others in it, and each of them chooses whether to watch. A share passes through the den's SFU like the call's audio, and costs the den's upload once for each member watching it, so watching is opt-in, and the owner sets how much shares may cost (see Limits for calls).

*Sharing*

- The call's row has a button with a screen on it, just before mute, as the box on other pages has, where the browser can share a screen (M9.1, M13.4). It opens the browser's own picker, which offers a screen, a window or a tab and, where the browser can share sound, a choice to include it. The page asks the browser to leave the Dens tab out of the picker (`selfBrowserSurface: 'exclude'`), since sharing it would show the page inside itself.
- While the member shares, the button shows pressed, and stops the share, and a row of the share's own sits above the call's: it says they're live and how many watch, with their names in its tip, and holds a button that shows their own screen in the player or hides it, and one to stop. The browser's own stop button, and closing what was shared, stop the share too.
- The member's own screen shows in the player as a preview, from what their browser captures, not sent back by the den: when they start sharing if the player holds nothing else, and otherwise once they ask, with the share's row or their own LIVE mark. It plays without sound, since they hear what they share already, and its ✕ hides it while they go on sharing. A whole screen shared with its preview in sight shows the preview inside itself, as in any app that has one.
- The share goes at the quality the member chose in the settings' Voice section, within the den's limits: its size (720p to 2160p) and frame rate (15, 30 or 60 a second), each at most the den's, and whether it suits text, which keeps detail and drops frames first, or motion, which keeps frames and drops detail first (the track's `contentHint`). The page caps the capture to the den's size and frame rate, and the sender to the den's bitrate. Before the member picks anything, the button says so when the den has as many shares as it allows, or allows none.
- A share nobody watches sends nothing: the page pauses its encoder (`active: false`) until someone watches, which saves the member's upload and processor. Chromium starts again with a keyframe and Firefox doesn't, so a new viewer's first picture comes from the den's keyframe request as their section opens (see The den's part).
- Sound depends on the browser. Chromium-based browsers share a tab's sound on every platform, and the whole system's on Windows; on Linux, the whole system's only behind a flag (`chrome://flags/#pulseaudio-loopback-for-screen-share`). Firefox shares no sound at all. The page asks for sound unless the member turned it off in the settings, and asks the browser to leave the page's own sound out of a system's (`restrictOwnAudio`, from Chrome 141), so the call's voices don't go back out to the call. A share without sound says so in its row.
- A share ends when its member stops it, leaves the call, moves to another, or their call ends, and when staff mute them. A call the page joins again after it dropped, as when the den restarts, takes back the share and the shares the member watched, which the den checks again.

*Watching*

- Everyone who can see the voice channel sees who in its call is sharing, with a LIVE mark beside their name, and who watches them. In the same call, the mark is a button that watches; anywhere else it joins the call and watches, as clicking the channel joins it.
- What a member watches plays in a player that floats over the page, above it and below dialogs such as the settings, so it stays as they open other channels, dens or home, and they keep reading and writing around it. On the phone layout it docks above the channel instead (M13.4, see The phone layout). They drag it by its bar and resize it from its corners; the browser keeps where it was and its size, and it stays inside the window as the window changes size.
- The player holds every share the member watches, and their own while it shows, side by side, two by two for three or four, and three across two rows for five. Clicking a share makes it fill the player while the others go on out of sight, which the bar counts, and clicking it again puts it back beside them. The bar's ✕ stops watching all of them, hides the member's own, and closes the player.
- Each share in the player has controls of the page's own, the same in every browser, which show on hover or focus, and always on a touch screen: who is sharing, the volume of its sound (a mute button with a slider beside it, like the video player's), full screen, and a button to stop watching it. Deafening mutes shares' sound too, and the call's speaker plays it.
- A member watches shares in their own call only, and at most four at once. Leaving the call stops watching.
- A share they watch opens in a window of its own from a button in its controls (M11), which they can move to another monitor, resize and make full screen. The window is a blank page of the page's own origin, which the page fills with the share and its controls, as the player has them, from the page's own render: it plays the stream the page already receives, so it needs no sign-in and no connection of its own, and the voice keys work while it has focus. The player keeps its place meanwhile, saying where the share went, with a button that brings it back. Closing the window brings it back; stopping watching, leaving the call or the share ending closes it, and so does closing or reloading the page. A browser that opens no windows to move between monitors, as phones' and Android's WebView, gets no button; one that blocks the window says so in the player. A window may hold back the share's sound until it's clicked, which it then asks for.

*The den's part*

- A share is VP9, the one video codec the den offers. Every target browser has sent and received it for years, and it keeps fine detail at bitrates a home connection can send, where VP8 blurs it. AV1 keeps more for the same bits: in the test container, held to 200 kbps, Chromium 154's 1080p test screen kept 20 frames a second with AV1, 14 with VP9 and 6 with VP8. But it costs more to encode, few machines have an encoder for it in hardware, and it's newer in Firefox (on by default from 136). H.264 is out: Firefox has it only through Cisco's OpenH264 plugin, which it downloads on its own, and the test container's Firefox ESR 153, without it, offered none.
- When a member first shares in a call, the den adds two sections to their offers: a video section it receives the screen on and, if the share has sound, an audio section for it. They stay for the rest of the call, for any later share. A member who watches gets the share's video, and its sound if it has any, in sections of their own, which come and go as they start and stop watching, and which later shares reuse as members' audio sections are reused.
- The den forwards a share as it forwards voice: only VP9, only from the member's own screen section and only while they share, each packet's payload under headers of the den's own, without the sender's header extensions.
- A viewer's browser can start decoding only at a keyframe, a whole picture, and the sharer's browser sends one only when asked. So the den asks the sharer for one, with a picture loss indication (PLI), as soon as a viewer's answer opens their section, and again when a paused share's packets start again, since a request that reaches a paused encoder may be lost; and it passes on as a PLI each PLI or full intra request (FIR) that a viewer's browser sends, as after packets it lost. A keyframe goes to every viewer and is many times the size of other frames, so the den sends a share at most one PLI every half second; a request inside that time gets one at its end.
- The den asks the sharer again for packets it lost (NACK), and sends viewers again what they lost from a buffer of what it sent, so a viewer's lost packets cost the sharer a keyframe only when the den no longer has them.
- The den can't decode a share, so it can't see its size or frame rate: those are the page's to keep, in the member's browser. What the den holds a share to is its bitrate. Its offer asks for no more than the den's share bitrate, in both forms browsers read: Firefox honors only `b=TIAS`, and Chromium `b=AS` and `b=TIAS` alike, as the test container showed. The den also drops whatever goes past the bitrate and a quarter more, with room for a keyframe's burst, so a page that ignores the offer gains nothing. A share's sound is held to a voice's limits, 256 kbps and 500 packets a second, and its Opus asks for stereo at 128 kbps, where voice is mono.
- Every viewer gets the one stream the sharer sends: a viewer on a slow connection loses frames, rather than getting a smaller stream. Simulcast, where the sharer sends two sizes and the den picks one for each viewer, comes after v1.

*Staff, and who sees what*

- A staff mute ends a member's share and keeps them from sharing until it's lifted, as the den forwards nothing from them. A staff disconnect ends the share with the call.
- A share is sealed as the rest of a call is (M16.2): its screen and its sound go under keys of their own from the sharer's key, which its viewers hold as the call's other members do, so the den forwards frames it can't open. On an older den shares go unsealed, as the call does.
- Everyone who can see the voice channel sees who is sharing and who watches, as they see who is in the call.

**Limits for calls (M4.2)**

The owner sets these in the den's settings, in a section of their own beside the upload limits:

| Limit | New den | Range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Members in one call | 15 | 2 to 30 |
| Members in calls across the den | 30 | 2 to 100 |
| Shares at once, across the den | 1 | 0, for none, to 10 |
| Viewers of one share | 8 | 1 to 29 |
| A share's bitrate | 2 Mbps | 0.25 to 50 Mbps |
| A share's size | 1080p | 720p, 1080p, 1440p or 2160p |
| A share's frame rate | 30 a second | 5 to 60 |

- A share's size caps its pixels, whatever its shape: 1080p allows as many as 1920 × 1080, so an ultrawide window comes out wider and shorter, and a portrait one taller and narrower.
- Beside the limits, the settings show what they cost the den's upload at most: shares times viewers times bitrate for video, and for voice, a full call at the den's busiest channel bitrate while two people talk, and from M14.3 a sound to everyone in that call. At a new den's limits, video takes at most 16 Mbps, which most home connections can send; an owner with a faster one raises them, and the guide shows how. The ceiling, 50 Mbps, leaves room for a 4K share at 60 frames a second.
- A call holds at most 30 members, since each offer has a section for every other member and must fit in 32 KiB: one for a member of a call of 30 who shares and watches four shares, with the soundboard's section (M14.3) and the den's candidates for four addresses, is 24.4 KB, which a test measures.
- The den sends its limits to members with its name and upload limits, so each page knows them before it asks, and checks them itself all the same. A lower limit applies to what starts afterwards: nobody is taken out of a call or a share.

**The soundboard (M14.3)**

Staff add sounds to the den's soundboard, and a member in a call plays one for everyone in it. The den streams a sound into the call on a track of the call's own, which every member's page plays as it plays a voice, so members never fetch the files: the track goes the way voices go on every form, to the speaker chosen, along Android's call route, on with the screen off, and silent while deafened, and the browser's echo cancellation hears it as it hears voices, so a member on speakers doesn't send it back into the call. Everyone hears a sound at once, and a member joining partway hears the rest. Each listener's page playing the den's file, as Discord's soundboard works, would take each install fetching each file, a list of what plays kept in step on every page, and a player of the page's own beside the call's; mixing the sound into its player's voice would have noise suppression and push to talk cut it. The cost is the den's upload while a sound plays: one Opus stream to each member of the call, about 1 Mbps for fifteen, small beside their voices.

- *The den's named files.* A sound is one of the den's named files: its name, at most 32 characters, unique in the den in any case and cleaned as display names are; its file; and who added it and when. The den keeps its named files in one table with a kind, `den_named_files`: sounds, the den's emoji from M15.2 (see Emoji), and events' badges' pictures from M17.5 (see Events); the kind is the den's to check, so a new one needs no new table. A sound takes its file as a profile takes its picture, so retention never removes it, nor the sweep of uploads nobody used: the file counts toward the den's space but not its uploader's, since it's the den's, isn't among the uploader's files, stays as its uploader leaves, and no message, picture or replacement takes it. Members get the sounds' names, lengths and who added them, in `ready` and by `sound.created`, `sound.updated` and `sound.deleted`; their files open for staff alone, since only the den plays them. Staff, the moderators and the owner, add a sound in Manage's Soundboard section, under This den, by a file and a name, and there hear, rename or delete each.
- *Prepared by staff's page.* The den sends a sound's Opus packets as its file holds them, since re-encoding would take an audio encoder the media module doesn't have. So the page of the staff member adding a sound prepares it before it goes up: it decodes the file at 48 kHz, any audio the browser plays, a video's sound too, in mono or stereo, folding more channels into two: 5.1 and 7.1 as Web Audio mixes 5.1 for speakers, the centre into both sides and each surround into its own at -3 dB, the LFE left out, and any other count's extra channels into both sides, since a file names no layout; evens its loudness to -20 LUFS, as ITU-R BS.1770 measures it, its peaks under -1 dBFS; and encodes it as Opus with WebCodecs (Chromium from 94, Firefox from 130), at 64 kbps, constant where the browser has it, in Ogg, which a small writer of the page's makes, each packet on a page of its own, the last page's granule position trimming the encoder's padding. A browser without WebCodecs' audio encoder can't add sounds, and says so, as does a silent file, one the browser can't decode, and one longer than the den's longest, before it's encoded. The one level takes the place of a volume for each sound, and the one bitrate makes a file's size follow its length. The page's writer and the Go tests share one file of cases, `internal/media/testdata/ogg.json`: the page writes it from its packets, and the Go tests read it through Pion's Ogg reader and the den's.
- *Checked by the den.* A sound arrives as any upload does, stripped by the media module, which writes Ogg's pages anew, up to about a second of packets in each. The den reads the stored file itself (`media.OggOpus`): one stream of Opus, in mono or stereo, in pages whose checksums, numbers and serial check out, each packet ended by its lacing values, which Pion's reader keeps to itself as it hands out whole pages, at most 4 KiB and 120 ms. It takes only that, no longer than the owner allows, give or take its last packet, which the encoder pads, and no larger than its length at 64 kbps makes, with a quarter more for the encoder and Ogg's pages and 4 KiB for its headers; the den's file size limit still bounds each upload. Its length is what plays, its packets' samples after the pre-skip, which the file's length becomes too.
- *Playing.* A member in a call plays a sound from the soundboard (`voice.sound`). The den checks that they're in the call and not staff muted, the soundboard is on, no sound plays in the call, and the wait after their last sound has passed, counting from when it ended, so a member can't play sound after sound; then it reads the sound's packets and sends them on the call's track at their pace, by each packet's table of contents. One sound plays in a call at a time, since mixing two would take re-encoding: a sound asked for while another plays is refused, as are the rest, with `voice.refused`'s reasons (`playing`, `wait`, `off`, `staff_muted`, `not_found`). The den ends a sound early only as its player leaves the call, as when staff disconnect them, or staff mute them, so a long one can't be used to wear a call down; there's no button to stop one, and a member who'd rather not hear it turns the soundboard down. A sound deleted as it plays plays to its end. While a sound plays, its player's ring lights for everyone in the call, through `voice.sounding`, since the page lights no muted member's ring by push to talk's list, and a member playing a sound is often muted; it goes to the call's members as a sound starts and ends, and as a socket's call starts or takes a held call back, as `voice.talking` does.
- *The call's track.* Each call has one more audio track, the soundboard's, in the stream `soundboard`, which the SFU adds to every member's connection as they join, so a sound needs no new offer; it already makes a track of its own for each share's sound. Its section asks for stereo, as a share's sound's does. Its packets carry headers of the den's own, their RTP timestamps going on from the last sound's by the time between, so a browser's jitter buffer takes the silence as time gone by, the first of each sound marked as after silence. The largest call's offer grows by one audio section and still fits `MaxSDP` (see Limits for calls).
- *Heard.* The page knows the track by its stream and plays it as it plays a member, through an element on a computer and through the call's context in the Android app, at the member's soundboard volume, under the call's volume, and silent while deafened; its level measures nobody speaking. An older page plays the track as it would a member's voice, at full volume with no slider, and lights no ring; an older den sends no track, and its info has no soundboard to show.
- *The soundboard.* A button opens it: in the call's block, in place of the call's speaker, which moved to the channel's row; to the left of mute on the phone's bar and in Home's box. At its top are a search field and a slider for how loud the soundboard plays for the member, 0 muting it, which the browser keeps by den, as it keeps each member's volume; below them are the den's sounds as tiles by name, with their lengths, a tap on one playing it for the call. While a sound plays, or the member must wait, or is deafened, since there's no playing what they wouldn't hear, the tiles say so and play nothing, the wait counting down by the page's own clock from when the member's sound ended. For staff, a cog beside the search opens Manage's Soundboard. It opens up and to the right from the list's block and down from Home's box, standing over the page past the list's edge, framed as the share player is, and on the phone layout as a sheet from the screen's foot, as the page's menus are there. With no sounds in the den, members have no button, and staff's opens Manage's Soundboard to add them. With the soundboard off, nobody has one.
- *Manage's Soundboard,* for staff: how many sounds the den holds of how many it allows, each up to its longest; a sound added by a file, any the browser plays, and a name, which the page suggests from the file's; and the den's sounds by name, each with its length and who added it, ▶ to hear it here, from its file, which opens for staff, Rename, and Delete, which asks first. With the soundboard off it says so, and points the owner to Limits.
- *The owner's limits,* in Manage's Limits, a Soundboard section beside Calls, each conservative by default, as Discord's soundboard is short, 5.2 seconds:

  | Limit | New den | Range |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | Sounds | 50 | 0 to 500; 0 turns the soundboard off, keeping its sounds |
  | Longest | 10 seconds | 1 second to 5 minutes |
  | A member's wait between sounds | 5 seconds | 0 to 10 minutes |

  A sound's size follows its length, so it has no limit of its own. Limits hold as a sound is added: lowering one leaves the sounds already added, which staff remove as they choose. The upload Calls estimates at full load counts the soundboard's stream to each member of a full call. The den keeps them in three columns of its own row, `sounds`, `sound_longest` and `sound_wait`, and sends them in its info as `sound_limits`, as its call limits.

**Global shortcuts (M7)**

A page sees keys only while it has focus, so push to talk can't reach it from a full-screen game. Discord's web app has the same limit, and its desktop app doesn't. Nor can the local service see keys: on Windows it runs in session 0, where Windows blocks all keyboard input to services, and on Linux it runs as its own account, outside the desktop session.

*The helper*

- A small helper, `dens shortcuts`, the same binary, runs as the desktop user in their session from login. One helper serves every instance on the machine: it finds them in the instance roots, which users may list, and holds a stream on each one's control endpoint, which answers only that instance's desktop user.
- The service sends the helper the member's keys, and whether one of its pages is in a call. The helper sends back each press and release as the action it's bound to: push to talk, toggle mute or push to mute. The service passes them to its pages, and the page in the call acts on them.
- The helper watches keys only while a page is in a call. Outside calls it watches nothing and costs nothing.
- The service keeps the keys, since the helper needs them, so every browser paired with the instance shares them. How the member sends, and the level, stay with the browser.
- A key is a keyboard key with any modifiers or, on Windows and in the page, one of the mouse's side buttons, which many players push to talk with. The page names a key by its place on the keyboard (`KeyboardEvent.code`), and the helper finds what the current layout makes of it.
- When the service comes back from an update at another version, the helper starts the new binary and exits.
- The helper's stream is the control endpoint's `shortcuts` request: JSON lines go both ways until either side closes it, each pinging every 15 seconds and giving up on the other after 45 without a line. The service sends `hello` with its version, then `state`, its keys and whether it holds a call, as either changes; the helper answers each with `source`, what it covers, and sends `press` for each press and release. An instance holds one stream from its desktop user's helper: a newer one replaces it, and the old one, told `replaced`, exits.
- The keys are the service's setting `voiceKeys`, which the settings' API takes whole. The page hears of them, and of the helper, over its socket with the service: `keys`, with the keys, whether a helper is connected, what it covers and its note, as any of them changes, and `key` for each press during a call, which the service drops outside one.

*Windows*

- The helper reads the bound keys' state every 10 ms (`GetAsyncKeyState`), with no keyboard hook, so it sees nothing else typed.
- While a program run as administrator has focus, Windows hides every key from the helper, as it does from Discord (User Interface Privilege Isolation). The settings say so.
- The installer registers a scheduled task that starts the helper at the desktop user's logon, with their interactive token, at normal priority, since a task's default of below normal could fall behind a game, and with no time limit. It runs the task at once, so nobody signs out after installing. Uninstalling the last instance ends the task and deletes it.
- The Windows spike (`spikes/README.md` at commit a03d820), on Windows 11 24H2 with a Ryzen 7 9800X3D: the reads saw a key pressed with a game full screen in front and in Steam, and nothing with an elevated terminal in front. At normal and below-normal priority, with the game running, they came every 10 ms at the median and the 99th percentile, one gap in some 15,000 reaching 20 ms, for 0.2 to 1% of one core. No game with anti-cheat was at hand.
- `dens.exe` carries a manifest whose `consoleAllocationPolicy` is `detached`, which Windows 11 24H2 and later honor: started outside a console, as at logon, it gets none, not even one `CREATE_NEW_CONSOLE` asks for, so the helper opens no window, while in a shell it runs as any console program does. Go doesn't embed manifests, so a resource file generated for each architecture sits beside `cmd/main.go`, and CI checks that it's current.

*Linux*

- Wayland: the desktop's global shortcuts portal, in GNOME 48 and later, KDE Plasma and Hyprland, reports a shortcut's press and release. There the desktop owns the keys. The member sets them up with it from the settings: the helper binds the three actions in a session of the portal, with the member's keys as the preferred triggers, and the desktop asks them to confirm or change each. The service keeps that they did, and as the helper starts it binds again, which the desktop answers without a dialog, since it remembers the keys for the app across sessions and sign-ins. A dialog turned down isn't asked again until the member asks, and stopping ends the session, which a new one replaces, unbound: a session binds once.
- The settings then show the desktop's names for the keys. Changing them opens the desktop's own settings for them where its portal can (`ConfigureShortcuts`, version 2, as KDE's), and otherwise says where they are (GNOME's portal is version 1: its Settings, under Apps, then Dens). The shortcuts' IDs never change, so the desktop asks only once.
- GNOME repeats a held key's press as the keyboard repeats, and reports one release; the helper passes one press and one release.
- The Linux spike (`spikes/README.md` at commit 0f99022) drove the portal as the helper does, on Fedora 44's GNOME 50 and KDE Plasma under Wayland. Both took the app ID once the `.desktop` file was installed, showed their dialog on the first bind with the preferred triggers, each changeable, and later bound without one in a few milliseconds, across sign-ins and restarts. Presses reached the session with a game full screen in front.
- A key bound on the desktop reaches no program but the helper, a game included, and the settings say so. A keyboard whose top row sends media keys unless Fn is held never sends a function key it doesn't hold Fn for.
- The portal refuses an unsandboxed app whose app ID no installed `.desktop` file backs (xdg-desktop-portal 1.21 and later), so the helper registers as `chat.dens.Dens` before anything else (`org.freedesktop.host.portal.Registry`), and the installer adds `chat.dens.Dens.desktop` to `/usr/local/share/applications`, with Dens's mark as its icon. The file also opens the page (`dens open`) from the desktop's menu, and GNOME keeps the app's shortcuts on its page in Settings. Older portals take the app ID from the helper's systemd unit, which the autostart entry's name sets.
- Desktops without the portal (GNOME before 48, Sway, COSMIC) keep the page's keys.
- X11: the helper reads the bound keys' state every 10 ms (`XQueryKeymap`), over a small X11 client of its own that connects to the local display with the X authority's cookie. A key's place maps to the X server's keycode through Linux's input codes. A mouse's buttons aren't keys to the X server, so side buttons stay the page's there.
- The installer adds an XDG autostart entry (`/etc/xdg/autostart/chat.dens.Dens.desktop`), which GNOME and KDE run as the systemd user unit `app-chat.dens.Dens@autostart.service`, and starts or restarts that unit in the desktop user's session through their systemd user manager, when they're signed in to a desktop. Uninstalling the last instance removes the entries and the icon; a helper still running stops once no instance answers it.
- The helper speaks D-Bus through `godbus/dbus/v5`, which needs nothing beyond `golang.org/x/sys`.

*Notifications (M12.3)*

- The helper shows the member's notifications where no page can (see Notifications): the stream carries `notify`, a notification that replaces its channel's, and `unnotify`, those to take back by their keys, and the helper's `source` says whether it shows them (`notifies`). Its `hello` names the page's origin, for the address a click opens. It shows each instance's apart, under an ID of its own.
- Linux: through the freedesktop notification service on the session bus, as Dens (the `desktop-entry` hint names `chat.dens.Dens`, whose icon the desktop then shows), with its text escaped where the service reads markup, since it's a member's. A click on its body invokes its `default` action, which the service hands back to the helper: the helper asks the instance for a pairing link, as `dens open` does, and opens it at the message. A helper that starts where no service answers shows none, and the settings say notifications show only in a Dens tab.
- Windows: as toasts, through Windows Runtime, under the app ID `chat.dens.Dens`. Windows shows a desktop app's toasts only under an app ID a Start menu shortcut carries, so the installer makes one, `Dens.lnk` in the Start menu for every user, which opens the page as `dens open` does; the helper shows none without it. A toast's click opens its address through the browser by itself (protocol activation), so it works from the Action Center too, whatever runs then.
- The Flatpak's helper, inside its service, shows them through the desktop's Notification portal, which knows the app by its Flatpak ID; a click comes back as the portal's `ActionInvoked` signal, for an action without the `app.` prefix, which the portal would otherwise take for one of the app's own D-Bus actions, and the service opens the page paired through the OpenURI portal.

*The page*

- The settings' keys say whether they work in other programs, with an unobtrusive mark. Where the desktop owns the keys, the keys card offers to set them up with it, says to confirm them in its dialog while that's up, and once they're set up shows the desktop's names for them, with a way to change them and one to stop using them. A session binds once, so where one that bound them holds none of them now, as when they were taken out in the desktop's settings, the card says so, with where to change them and a way to start over (M11).
- While the helper covers a key, during a call the page ignores its own presses of it and acts on the helper's: on Windows and X11 the helper sees a key pressed in the page too, and toggle mute would fire twice. Outside a call the helper reads nothing, and the page's own keys work as before, except those the desktop owns, whose keys may be others. A key that types something still doesn't fire while the member types in a text field; one the desktop owns never reaches the field.
- A side button the member bound is the page's while it has focus: its press and release don't take the browser back or forward.
- The helper tells the service what its keys can do (whether the desktop confirms and names them) and what the system keeps from them, as a note the settings show, never which system it runs on, so no platform difference reaches the local API.

**Media addresses**

The den's media ports carry every call: one UDP port, and one TCP port as the fallback for networks that block UDP.

- The den runs ICE-lite. It never sends connectivity checks, only answers the browser's, so it needs no candidates of its own. Each port takes every call, which Pion's UDP and TCP muxes tell apart by the ICE username in the first packet.
- The member's local service writes the den's candidates into each offer itself: each address the den's name resolves to, with the den's two media ports. The browser sends media where the install already reaches the den, so the den needn't know its public address, and asks no outside STUN server for it. A member whose lookup gives the den's local address, as with split DNS at home, calls over the LAN.
- For a den on the same machine, which is the install's own den or one whose name resolves to loopback, the local service writes the machine's addresses on its other interfaces instead, since browsers don't use loopback for WebRTC. The owner's own calls then don't go through their router.
- So a den points a member's browser only at the addresses its name resolves to, at the media ports it gives. A den in control of its own DNS can make those any address, such as a device on the member's own network, but all the browser sends there is ICE's connectivity checks and DTLS, which don't act on anything.
- The den never learns members' local addresses. The local service takes the browser's candidates out of every answer, and puts the unspecified address (`0.0.0.0`, or `::`) in its connection, RTCP and origin lines (M8). A browser's first answer has it there, but each one after it has gathered names its default candidate in the `c=` line, often the computer's address on its local network, as Chromium and Firefox both do once a page may use the microphone. The den answers checks from wherever they arrive, so it needs none of them.
- Calls need the den's name to point at the den's machine, or the router in front of it, as the networking guide says. A tunnel or CDN that carries a den's HTTPS doesn't carry its calls.

**Den SFU (Pion)**

- `SettingEngine.SetICEUDPMux` puts all media on one UDP port; `SetICETCPMux` adds a TCP fallback port. Both listen on every interface, IPv4 and IPv6. The service binds them at start with the den listener, and doesn't start without them, as with its other ports.
- `SetLite`, with multicast DNS off: the den neither looks up members' `.local` names nor sends queries on its own network.
- A TCP connection that doesn't name a call within 5 seconds is closed, and the TCP port holds at most 128 at once, so a flood of idle connections costs bounded memory. Packets for no call are dropped.
- Voice is Opus only. The den reads a member's audio only from the section it asked them to send on, and forwards each packet's payload under headers of its own, without the sender's header extensions, at most 256 kbps and 500 packets a second from each member. Screen shares are VP9 only, forwarded the same way within the den's share bitrate (M4.2). The SFU never reads a payload, so it forwards sealed frames as it did plain ones (M16.2): a voice's sealing, some 9 kbps, and a share's, a header and tag a frame, fit within those limits.
- Interceptors: NACK, RTCP reports and TWCC for bandwidth estimation.
- A share's keyframe requests: one as each viewer starts watching, and every PLI and FIR from a viewer passed on to the sharer as a PLI, at most one every half second per share (M4.2; see Screen share).
- A held call keeps its peer connection without a socket, and its offers wait until it's resumed. An ICE restart is Pion's own: the den's offer carries new credentials, which the UDP and TCP muxes match from then on (M3).
- A staff mute drops the member's packets before they're forwarded (M3).
- Pion's own log messages name addresses, so they reach the log only in development instances. The den logs each call's start, end and the reason it ended, by member and channel ID, and from M11 its own notes on each call at debug (see What's logged).
- No TURN in v1: the den is directly reachable, so clients behind NAT connect outward. Add `pion/turn` on TCP 443 later if restrictive networks need it.
- Pion is the only WebRTC stack in Go, and DTLS-SRTP and ICE aren't things to write. `pion/webrtc` v4 brings 15 more of the project's modules, plus `google/uuid` and `wlynxg/anet`, all MIT-licensed, and adds about 5 MB to the binary, including parts Dens doesn't use, such as data channels and a TURN client.
- Start from Pion's SFU-over-WebSocket example rather than a blank file.

**Bandwidth at target scale** (approximate)

| Case | Per stream | Den upload |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 people in voice (Opus at 96 kbps) | about 124 kbps talking, sealed (M16.2), 1 silent (M3.3) | about 2 Mbps while two talk, up to 11 Mbps |
| 1 screen share at 2.5 Mbps, 10 viewers | about 2.6 Mbps | about 26 Mbps |
| 2 screen shares at 2.5 Mbps, 10 viewers each | about 2.6 Mbps | about 52 Mbps |
| 2 screen shares at 2.5 Mbps, 20 viewers each | about 2.6 Mbps | about 104 Mbps |

Screen share cost scales with viewers, not sharers, which is why watching is each member's choice and the owner caps shares, viewers and bitrate (M4.2; see Limits for calls). Simulcast (sender uploads high and low layers, SFU picks per viewer) is the later fix.

**Known risks**

- A share's sound depends on the browser (M4.2): Chromium-based browsers share a tab's sound everywhere and the whole system's on Windows, on Linux only behind a flag, and Firefox shares none. Accepted for v1; a share without sound says so.
- `restrictOwnAudio` keeps the call's voices out of a shared system's sound only from Chrome 141; the manual test checks it on Windows, where it matters.
- Firefox and Chromium differ in small WebRTC details; each milestone is tested on all four target browsers.
- Calls depend on the den's name resolving to the den. If dens behind tunnels need calls, an owner setting for a separate media address could come later.
- A sealed call needs Encoded Transform (M16.2): Firefox 117, Chromium 141, and Android System WebView 141. A WebView between the app's floor of 111 and 141 runs Dens but joins no call on a den that seals them, and says to update it.
- A member who joins hears the others a second or so after their keys come, as each sends under a new key a second after making it, and frames under a key a page doesn't hold yet are dropped; a burst of joins and leaves makes one key a half second at most, which can leave a moment's gap.

## Networking and deployment

A den needs a domain, Caddy, and four forwarded ports. Caddy handles only HTTP and WebSocket traffic; WebRTC media must reach the den directly.

| Port | Protocol | Purpose | Required |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 80 | TCP | ACME certificate challenges (Caddy) | Yes |
| 443 | TCP | HTTPS and WSS to the den listener (Caddy) | Yes |
| Media port, default 7881 | UDP | WebRTC media via UDP mux | Yes, for voice |
| Fallback port, default 7882 | TCP | ICE-TCP media fallback | Recommended |

The docs list which ports to forward but not how, since routers vary too much. They mention a small VPS as the easy alternative when forwarding isn't possible.

Members' calls reach the den at the addresses its name resolves to (see Media addresses), so the name must point at the den's machine or the router forwarding to it. The owner's own calls go to the machine's own addresses and work before any port is forwarded, so the guide has owners test a call from outside their network.

On Windows, the installer adds inbound Windows Firewall rules for the media ports at every install, scoped to `dens.exe` and those ports, and removes them on uninstall. Nothing listens there until the owner turns hosting on, so until then they let nothing in (M9). The Caddy guide covers ports 80 and 443 on both platforms.

**Caddy**

- The guide ships a minimal Caddyfile: the den domain reverse-proxies to the den listener.
- Access logging stays off (Caddy's default), in line with collecting less.
- A second den on the same machine is another instance with its own ports and a second site block for its domain or subdomain.
- Caddy runs as a service on both platforms: its packaged systemd unit on Linux, and on Windows as an SCM service, which Caddy supports natively (`sc.exe create caddy binPath= "…\caddy.exe run --config …"`).
- Reloading or restarting Caddy closes den WebSockets (close 1001). Clients resume without losing events.
- Distributions' packages of Caddy lag (Debian 13 ships 2.6), so the den e2e runs the Caddy `scripts/vendor.sh` pins.

**Den-side trust of proxy headers**

- The den listener trusts `X-Forwarded-For` only from loopback, and uses it for rate limiting in memory only.

## Platform support

Dens supports Linux and Windows for both the client and the den. Linux is recommended. A Flatpak (M9) brings the client to other Linux distributions, and an Android app (M12) to phones; neither hosts a den.

### Deployment forms (M9)

Besides the system, Dens differs by how it's deployed: installed (the service under its own account), development (as the developer, with a plaintext key), the Flatpak (as the member, in their session) and, from M12, Android (under the app's shell). `layout.Form` names the form, and `app.FormOf` chooses it at runtime: on Android (`GOOS=android`) Android, for a development build too, since the app's shell runs either; elsewhere a development build is Development, a sandbox whose `/.flatpak-info` and `FLATPAK_ID` name `chat.dens.Dens` the Flatpak, and anything else Installed, so the Flatpak runs the release binary as it is. Each seam that differs by form takes it, as a `switch` on the form rather than a type per form, since there are few forms and each differs from the others in a few lines: where the paths are, how the data key unwraps, whether the instance checks the release host for updates, which an installed one with a release host does, and the Android app, whose release names its host (`App.ChecksForUpdates`), the settings' update options with it, what the update notice says and what installs the update, how the CLI checks the service it talks to and what it advises when none is running, whether the helper runs inside the service, and whether the instance may host. A form `layout` doesn't know has no paths, so no process runs as one. Forms other than Installed are session forms (`Form.Session`): they run as their user with no service manager, write their own instance config and lifecycle state, and take no maintenance command but `restore`, which the Android app refuses too, since its shell would have to wrap the backup's key. As with a platform difference, a form never reaches the protocol or the database, and reaches the page only as what it can do.

| Form | Runs as | Its data key | Installed and updated by | Hosts a den | Its doc |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Installed | A service under its own account: a systemd unit on Linux, an SCM service on Windows | Bound to the host: `systemd-creds` or DPAPI | Its maintenance commands, which the installer scripts run | Yes | [installed.md](installed.md) |
| Development | The developer, from a terminal | A plaintext file | The developer's own builds | Yes | [lifecycle.md](lifecycle.md)'s Development instances |
| The Flatpak (M9) | The member, in their session | None that opens without them, unless they ask it to remember | Flatpak | No | [flatpak.md](flatpak.md) |
| Android (M12) | The app's shell, as its child process | A Keystore key | Android's installer: the APK from the release host, then each update the app's updater checks | No | [android.md](android.md) |

### Platform layer

All OS-specific runtime code lives in one package (`internal/platform/host`). Each seam is a small function pair in `_linux.go` and `_windows.go`. Everything above it (listeners, `Host` and `Origin` checks, rendering, auth, crypto, rate limits, the SFU and the database) is shared code with no build tags.

| Seam | Linux | Windows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Service host | sd_notify, `SIGTERM` | `x/sys/windows/svc` handler |
| Host-bound data key | Read `$CREDENTIALS_DIRECTORY` | DPAPI unprotect |
| Control endpoint | Unix socket, `SO_PEERCRED` | Named pipe on `x/sys/windows` (no third-party module), client SID by identification-level impersonation, server PID check |
| Private directory check | Owner and mode 0700 | Owner and a protected DACL naming only the allowed SIDs |
| Locked key memory | `mlock` | `VirtualLock` |
| Low-priority child process | nice 19 and, where the sandbox allows it, `SCHED_IDLE` on the forking thread, set with `sched_setscheduler`, whose policy systemd's `RestrictRealtime=` can read, since it refuses `sched_setattr` | `IDLE_PRIORITY_CLASS` |
| Open a URL | `xdg-open` | `ShellExecute` |
| Keys for the helper (M7) | The global shortcuts portal under Wayland, `XQueryKeymap` under X11 | `GetAsyncKeyState` |
| Notifications for the helper (M12.3) | The freedesktop notification service, or from the Flatpak, the Notification portal | Toasts, through Windows Runtime |
| The Flatpak's portals (M9.3) | Secret, Background and Notification, Flatpak's own `Spawn` and update monitor, and the app's name on the session bus | None |

Go builds `_linux.go` files for Android too, so where Android differs, an `_android.go` file takes the seam's place and its Linux one says `!android` (M12): locked memory falls back to ordinary memory past the 64 KiB Android lets an app lock, and the host-bound data key comes from the app's shell on standard input, as an installed service's comes from systemd. Every other seam works on Android as on Linux, the control endpoint in the app's files among them.

The control endpoint stays up whatever its clients do (M8). On Windows, a pipe instance whose client connected and closed before the service's `ConnectNamedPipe` reports `ERROR_NO_DATA`; the listener disconnects that instance and waits on it again, so it keeps holding the pipe's name, as go-winio does. The control server waits a moment and accepts again after any error but its listener closing, as `net/http` does, and serves at most 64 connections at once. On Linux it refuses another account's connection before reading its request, and since that can close the socket before the request is written, the client reads the service's answer even when sending fails.

Everything else that differs is install-time: the two installers, the unit file and SCM registration, account creation, ACLs and firewall rules, and how the helper starts at login: a scheduled task, or an autostart entry and a `.desktop` file (M7).

Rules that keep the two implementations from drifting:

- A platform difference never reaches the protocol, database or HTTP layers. A feature that seems to need one is a design problem.
- Each seam has a contract test that runs on both CI runners.
- Windows code compiles only under `GOOS=windows`, and Android's under `GOOS=android`; vet and build-test them on every change that touches them. Android's seams have their tests run in the emulator, by the Android e2e.

## Versioning and compatibility

Clients and dens negotiate an integer protocol version, separate from the release version, so most client updates never force a den update.

- Each release declares `protocol_current` and `protocol_min`, the oldest protocol it still speaks.
- The client sends `Dens-Protocol: <current>` on every request and the WebSocket upgrade; the den replies with its own range.
- If the ranges overlap, both use the highest shared version: a client whose request a den refuses as newer than it speaks asks again, once, at the den's newest, and goes at that until a response's range says the den speaks a newer one, as once it updates. A socket keeps the version it opened at, so one whose upgrade's answer shows another shared version, as a den's that updated since the client last heard from it, opens again at that one, once, rather than going without group DMs until it next reconnects. If not, the client shows "this den needs a newer or older Dens" with both versions.
- Version 2 (M16.3) brought group DMs, whose channels, messages and events would close an older client's connection as the den breaking the protocol: a den serves 1 and 2, and a client speaking 1 gets nothing of groups, on its socket or in answers.
- Version 3 (M17.3) brought notes to self, a channel of a kind an older client doesn't know, which would close its connection the same way: a den serves 1 to 3, and a client speaking 1 or 2 gets nothing of notes. Each kind of channel comes with a version, and a den gives a request or socket only the kinds its version has.
- Protocol bumps only for breaking changes; additive fields and message types don't bump it, and unknown message types are ignored.
- Aim to keep `protocol_min` one version behind current, so a den can lag one breaking release without locking members out.

**Data across versions.** Installed copies hold data that every later version reads, so from v1.0.0 formats move forward rather than change in place:

- Database migrations go at the end, and a released step never changes. A step that remakes a table, as SQLite changes a table's checks, runs with foreign keys off, as SQLite's procedure for it says, since dropping the old table with them on would delete every row that refers to it; SQLite can't turn them off within a transaction, so the migrator turns them off on its connection for the whole run that includes such a step, checks every foreign key before it commits, and turns them on again. M16.3's group DMs made `den_channels` again so, keeping its counter where it was, and M17.3's notes again, to take a fifth kind and the member whose notes it holds. v1.0.0 through v1.6.0 shipped the initial step alone; M14.2's own sounds add the second, and M14.3's soundboard the third: the den's named files, and the den's limits for them, the first columns added to a table since v1.0.0, with defaults a den made before them takes; v1.7.0 shipped those three. M15.2's emoji add the fourth, the den's limit for them, a column whose default an older den takes; M15.3's reactions the fifth, their tables, a channel's reactions and a DM's sealed sets, which start empty; and M15.4's polls the sixth, their tables, a poll and its votes, which start empty, and a message's mark of staff's @everyone, a column whose default, unmarked, an older message takes; v1.8.0 shipped those six. M16.1's identities add the seventh, M16.3's group DMs the eighth, and M16.4's disappearing messages the ninth: each DM's and group's time, Off, and who set it, none, and each message's time to go, none, columns whose defaults an older DM and message take; v1.9.0 shipped those nine. M17.2's spoilers add the tenth, a channel's file's mark, which an older file goes without, M17.3's notes the eleventh, `den_channels` made again, M17.4's events the twelfth: their tables, events and who's interested, which start empty, and the den's limit for members' events, a column whose default, 0, an older den takes; and M17.5's badges the thirteenth: their tables, badges and their awards, which start empty, and an event's staff-only mark, a column whose default, everyone's, an older event takes; and M17.6's hidden badges the fourteenth, an award's mark, whose default, shown, an older award takes, a step of its own since test builds of M17.5 ran the thirteenth. A test makes each released schema by running its steps alone, v1.6.0's, v1.7.0's, v1.8.0's and v1.9.0's, puts a row in every table, migrates it, and a backup made of it, and finds every row and table counter as it was, in every column the release had, the new columns at their defaults.
- `instance.json` and `state.json` decode strictly, so every field a release wrote stays readable, and a change of meaning gets a new field rather than an old one's name. An older binary never meets a newer file, since downgrades are refused.
- A backup from any release restores, and migrates as the service starts.
- A change to one of these comes with a test that reads its released form.
- The lifecycle harnesses install the first release, v1.0.0, host a den with it and update to the build under test, which must serve the den as it was; the release host keeps v1.0.0 for good (M9.1). The Linux harness then keeps a member's own sound, in the table M14.2's migration made, and reads it back, and finds the den's soundboard's limits at their defaults and a sound added to it, in what M14.3's made.

An install that is both client and den still updates as one binary. If that blocks someone, the documented answer is to move the den to its own install.

## Security hardening

The localhost page is the most valuable target: an XSS there reaches every joined den, the vault and the local API. Any den member can send you content, so all of it is hostile input.

Before the first release an audit checked every item here against the code (2026-10-11), with a probe where a claim needed one, such as the Windows control pipe closed early, run natively, and the addresses in Chrome's answers, from headless Chrome on Windows; it also measured the markdown parser on hostile text and the snapshot's scan at a million messages. What it found was fixed for v1.0.0 (M8). An item a later milestone adds is checked as it's built.

**Client page and listener**

- [x] CSP: `default-src 'none'`, with scripts, styles, images, fonts and connections limited to `'self'`, no inline scripts, `frame-ancestors 'none'`.
- [x] Messages rendered by an escaping markdown subset; names and channel descriptions treated as untrusted text.
- [x] Filenames treated as untrusted text, and files shown inline only as the image type the local service found them to be, sandboxed and never cached by the browser (M1.4).
- [x] Exact `Host` check (`127.0.0.1:<port>`, `[::1]:<port>` or `localhost:<port>`) against DNS rebinding.
- [x] `Origin` check on every write and on the WebSocket upgrade.
- [x] Session cookie `HttpOnly`, `SameSite=Strict`; pairing tokens single use and short-lived.
- [x] Listener bound on both `127.0.0.1` and `::1`.
- [x] Everything from a den is hostile input: the client service checks every den response against the protocol's types and limits before storing or forwarding it.
- [x] A den's error codes reach the page only when they're codes the page knows, and a client follows a den to a new address at most once a minute, one check at a time, never to plain `http` or loopback for a den elsewhere, and follows no redirect (M8).
- [x] The page parses a message in time proportional to its length, and once per text (M8).
- [x] The CSP allows WebAssembly for RNNoise (`'wasm-unsafe-eval'`), but not `eval` (M3).
- [x] Rewritten links stay on their own site: hosts match exactly, as a URL parser reads them, so a lookalike is never rewritten into a real link (M3).
- [x] A link to a message names what this member's Dens knows of where it points, never what its sender wrote, and opens in Dens, never in the browser, unless the member asks the browser to (M11).
- [x] The page's links to the public guides open in a new tab, with no opener and no referrer (v1).
- [x] The page frames nothing but YouTube's player from `https://www.youtube-nocookie.com` (`frame-src`), only for a member who turned players on and clicked, sandboxed without top navigation, at an address the page built from an ID it checked; it takes no messages from the frame, and its popups can't reach the page (M4.1).
- [x] The local service fetches nothing from YouTube but the oEmbed and picture addresses it builds from a checked video ID, follows no redirects, caps what it reads, cleans titles as names, and serves a picture only as a JPEG it read and stripped, with a member's file's headers (M4.1).

**Den listener**

- [x] Serves only den routes; no client or admin routes compiled into its router.
- [x] Challenge answers sign the den's address, and clients send nothing to an address the den didn't sign, so no relay can pass a sign-in through (M1.5).
- [x] A password alone doesn't add a device: another of the member's devices approves it, or a recovery code stands in (M1.7).
- [x] A DM starts only after both members type each other's check digits, and the DM seal never reaches the den (M1.7).
- [x] Request size limits, WebSocket message size limits, and rate limits per IP and per member, with one budget for a member's messages across every channel and DM (M8). A request's body arrives within a minute (M8).
- [x] Invite codes: 128-bit random, single use unless staff allow more (at most 100), expiring, stored hashed.
- [x] Bearer tokens only, never cookies, so no web page can make a browser act on a den.
- [x] The den's ring of recent events is held to 64 MiB as well as its count and age (M8).
- [x] Staff powers stop at DMs, and a ban is lifted only by staff of the rank of whoever placed it, or higher (M8).
- [x] Only the desktop user of the den's machine, with the local password, makes an owner invite, which is used once within an hour (M8).

**Control endpoint**

- [x] Every connection authorized by OS-reported peer identity against the recorded desktop user.
- [x] The CLI verifies the server's identity before trusting a response.
- [x] Windows pipe: `FILE_FLAG_FIRST_PIPE_INSTANCE`, `PIPE_REJECT_REMOTE_CLIENTS`, one listening instance at all times, DACL granting SYSTEM and the service SID full access and the recorded user only `0x12019b` (never `GENERIC_WRITE`).
- [x] The endpoint keeps answering after a client that opens it and closes at once, and on Windows a service that fails after starting is restarted, as systemd restarts it on Linux (M8).

**Service sandbox (Linux)**

- [x] `NoNewPrivileges`, `ProtectSystem=strict`, `ProtectHome=true`, `PrivateTmp`, `PrivateDevices`, `RemoveIPC`.
- [x] `ProtectKernelTunables`, `ProtectKernelModules`, `ProtectKernelLogs`, `ProtectControlGroups`, `ProtectClock`, `ProtectHostname`, `ProtectProc=invisible`, `ProcSubset=pid`.
- [x] `RestrictNamespaces`, `RestrictRealtime`, `RestrictSUIDSGID`, `LockPersonality`, `MemoryDenyWriteExecute`.
- [x] Empty `CapabilityBoundingSet` and `AmbientCapabilities`, `SystemCallArchitectures=native`, `SystemCallFilter=@system-service`, `SystemCallErrorNumber=EPERM` so a blocked call fails and is logged instead of killing the service.
- [x] `RestrictAddressFamilies=AF_INET AF_INET6 AF_UNIX AF_NETLINK`. Netlink is needed for interface enumeration, which Pion's ICE gathering uses; without it `net.Interfaces` fails.
- [x] `LimitCORE=0`, `UMask=0077`, `StateDirectory=dens/%i/data` (mode 0700) and `RuntimeDirectory=dens/%i` (mode 0755, for the socket) as the only writable paths.
- [x] `systemd-analyze security` rates the unit 1.5 ("OK") on every supported distro; the lifecycle e2e fails above that.

**Service sandbox (Windows)**

- [x] Virtual account, `SERVICE_SID_TYPE_RESTRICTED`, required privileges limited to `SeChangeNotifyPrivilege`.
- [x] Protected DACL on the state directory and data key blob: SYSTEM, Administrators and the service SID only.
- [x] `dens.exe` excluded from Windows Error Reporting.

**Media**

- [x] ffmpeg runs only as the translated module, in a worker process with a memory cap, a deadline and a lower Go stack limit, reaching nothing but the functions Dens gives it.
- [x] The module's memory is a slice with no spare capacity, so bulk memory operations trap past its end.
- [x] Dens's C in the module is fuzzed with damaged files under AddressSanitizer in CI, beside the module.
- [x] Stripped files of every kind Dens takes are checked by a reader independent of FFmpeg, exiftool, in CI.

**Voice (M2)**

- [x] Only members who can see a voice channel join its call, and a call ends as soon as that stops.
- [x] The local service writes the den's media addresses into every offer, so a den points a member's browser only where its name resolves, and takes the browser's candidates out of every answer, and from M8 the addresses in its connection, RTCP and origin lines, so the den never learns members' local addresses.
- [x] The page's peer connection names no STUN or TURN server, and its `Permissions-Policy` grants the microphone to the page alone.
- [x] The local service passes the page only offers within 32 KiB, of audio and, from M4.2, the video below, and the den takes only answers that fit its offer, within 32 KiB.
- [x] The den forwards only Opus, under its own headers, at most 256 kbps and 500 packets a second from each member.
- [x] The media ports drop packets for no call, close a TCP connection that doesn't name one within 5 seconds, and hold at most 128 TCP connections.
- [x] Pion's log messages, which name addresses, stay out of release logs.

**Voice (M3)**

- [x] Only staff of a higher rank disconnect or mute a member, and the den enforces a mute by forwarding nothing from them.
- [x] A held call resumes only on a socket of the device that held it, within 30 seconds, and a socket the den closes for good ends its call at once.
- [x] A call that ended while held stays ended: its resume gets the reason, never a new call.
- [x] Push to talk's ring reaches the call's members alone, never lights for a member muted or staff muted, and lights at most five times a second (M11).
- [x] A den with M16.2 takes no join that doesn't say it seals, and passes each member of a call their copy of another's key alone, while both are in it, from a member's frame for the call their socket holds, at most 30 in 10 seconds a member (M16.2).
- [x] A member's Dens takes a call key only once its statement names their own identity, its signature checks against the identity kept for its maker, and its copy opens to the key it commits to; copies, commitments and statements have contexts of their own, apart from DM keys' (M16.2).
- [x] The page seals a sender before its track goes on, so nothing leaves in the clear, sends nothing before it has a key, and drops every frame on a member's track that doesn't open under their key, on its track, once, the soundboard's alone passing in the clear; its tests take RFC 9605's own cases (M16.2).
- [x] The browser holds a call's keys only while it lasts, and the service and the logs name keys by their IDs alone (M16.2).

**Screen share (M4.2)**

- [x] The page's `Permissions-Policy` grants screen capture to the page alone (`display-capture=(self)`), and the page asks the browser's picker to leave out the Dens tab.
- [x] The local service passes the page audio only as Opus and video only as VP9 or AV1, so a den can't have the browser decode any other codec. The den offers VP9; AV1 passes too, so a later den can offer it without breaking older clients.
- [x] Only members of a call watch its shares, and the den forwards a share only while its member shares, only VP9 from their screen's section, under its own headers, within the den's share bitrate.
- [x] A staff mute ends a member's share, and the owner's limits cap the shares at once, a share's viewers and its bitrate, which the den enforces.
- [x] A share's window of its own is a blank page of the page's origin that the page fills, under the page's CSP, and loads nothing but the page's stylesheet; it closes with the share, the call and the page (M11).

**Files and retention (M5)**

- [x] Smaller copies are made on the sender's machine, a photo's by the module and `internal/media` and a video's by the module, or from M5.5 by the browser and muxed by the module, and the den checks them like any upload: images for metadata, and video stripped again.
- [x] Only a file's uploader deletes or swaps it, and a replacement counts against the space its file frees only to take that file's place.
- [x] Retention deletes for real, in channels and DMs: rows with `secure_delete` on, files off the disk, and clients drop what passed the period.

- [x] An animated picture is made on the member's machine, in Go, and refused past its budget before any of its frames is decoded; the den checks it as any upload, and takes one only as a GIF of a picture's sides and size (M15.1).

**The client's cache (M6)**

- [x] Everything cached is sealed with the data key, each message and file chunk bound to its den, its ID and its place; a chunk that doesn't open drops its file, and a cache under another key or of another format is emptied, never read.
- [x] Deleted messages and files leave every client's cache: at once while it's connected, and from the changes since its version when it comes back. The den's change log holds IDs and channels only, for 30 days.
- [x] The den sends a member only changes in channels they can see now, and a client drops a channel's cache when it can no longer see it, and a den's when the member leaves, is removed or banned, or the device is signed out.
- [x] A restored den starts a new sync epoch, and a client never keeps a cache across epochs or from below the floor.
- [x] A den never gives an ID out twice, even after a restore, which moves its counters on past every ID it gave out (M11), so a link names one message for good.
- [x] Compressed frames are held to the same limits as plain ones, as they inflate, and the cache keeps no file over a quarter of its size.

**Global shortcuts (M7)**

- [x] The helper watches only the bound keys, and only while a page is in a call, with no keyboard hook, and sends nothing but the bound actions' presses and releases; neither it nor the service logs them.
- [x] The helper's stream is a control request like any other: it answers only the instance's desktop user, by the identity the system reports.
- [x] The page acts on the helper's keys only in a call it holds, and never while the member types a key that types something.
- [x] Each desktop user's helper task runs as them, with their limited token; nothing of the helper runs elevated.

**Notifications (M12.3)**

- [x] Notifications stay off until the member turns a kind on, and the settings are the service's, which only a paired browser changes.
- [x] A notification's text is plain text from the start of the message: no markup reaches a notification service, whose markup a member's text is escaped for, a spoiler never shows, and control and format characters go; the member can leave the text out.
- [x] Nothing about a notification is logged but its IDs.
- [x] A click opens only the page, at an address the service builds from IDs the den gave it; the Android shell loads only a path of the page's form, and a pairing link is made at the click, never kept in a notification.

**Sounds (M14.2)**

- [x] A member's own sound stays on their install: the client router alone serves and takes it, its writes behind the `Origin` check, and it's served as the audio it is, sandboxed and never cached.
- [x] The service takes only audio, by its own look at the bytes, of at most 1 MiB, and keeps it sealed with the data key, bound to its sound.
- [x] Nothing about a sound is logged but which sound changed.

**Soundboard (M14.3)**

- [x] A sound's file opens for staff alone, by the den's check of who asks, and members get its name; the page hears a sound only on the call's track.
- [x] The den takes only Opus in Ogg, stripped by the media module as any audio is, which it reads itself, one stream in mono or stereo, every page's checksum checked and every packet at most 4 KiB and 120 ms, within the owner's longest and the size its length allows; and it plays only what it checked, under headers of its own.
- [x] One sound plays in a call at a time, a member's next only after their wait, and playing counts toward their writes; a staff mute ends their sound, and leaving ends it.
- [x] A sound's file is the den's: no message, profile picture or replacement takes it, and deleting a member or a message, retention and the sweep of waiting uploads leave it; a database bug that would delete it fails on its foreign key rather than leave a sound with no file.
- [x] Nothing about a sound is logged but IDs: who added or deleted which, and who played which in which channel.

**Emoji (M15.2)**

- [x] An emoji's name is the den's rule's, `a-z`, `0-9` and `_`, which a client checks again as it arrives, and the page shows it as text and as its image's words; it builds an emoji's image address from the image's ID alone.
- [x] The den takes as an emoji only a still image every page draws, or an animated GIF, at most 128 pixels each way and 256 or 512 KiB, an upload of staff's own waiting to be used; and refuses one past the owner's count.
- [x] An emoji's image is the den's: deleting the emoji takes it, and retention, the sweep of waiting uploads and its uploader leaving leave it.
- [x] A page shows the den's emoji from its install's cache alone, which fetches all of them ahead of their use, so the den doesn't learn which showed, in a DM or anywhere.
- [x] The list of Unicode's emoji is made from inputs pinned by SHA-256, and CI makes it again and fails on a difference.
- [x] Nothing about an emoji is logged but IDs: who added or deleted which.

**Reactions (M15.3)**

- [x] A channel's reaction carries no text: the den takes one of Unicode's emoji from the list, or one of its own emoji by ID while emoji are on, and a client checks each again as it arrives; the page shows a den's emoji by its image's ID alone.
- [x] A DM's set is sealed with the DM key, bound to the den, the DM, the message, the member, the key and its revision, and padded to one length; a client keeps only emoji from the list and names a den's emoji may have, and drops anything else, so a hostile partner or den puts nothing in a chip but an emoji, or a name of `a-z`, `0-9` and `_`, as their DM's text could hold.
- [x] A message holds at most 20 different emoji and 10,000 reactions, and each reaction counts toward its member's writes; staff take only the reactions of the members they outrank, and a DM's are its members' alone.
- [x] A DM's set changes only against the revision it was made against, so two devices never drop each other's reactions.
- [x] Nothing about a reaction is logged but IDs, and only staff's: who took how many off which message.

**Polls and @everyone (M15.4)**

- [x] The den sends a poll's totals and who voted for what to nobody sooner than its rules allow: in history and what changed while away, as the member asking may see it, and in its events, each batch to an audience of those who may see it, until the end; and a vote after voting is final.
- [x] A poll's choices are plain text, cleaned as names are, which a client checks again as it arrives, with its tally, and the page shows as text; an event whose tally doesn't fit its poll changes nothing. Its choices are sealed at rest with the data key, as its question is, bound to its message.
- [x] A vote and an early end each count toward the member's writes; only a poll's author ends it, and nobody edits one.
- [x] Only staff's @everyone mentions everyone, and only while staff put it there; nobody new takes the username `everyone`.
- [x] Nothing about a poll is logged but IDs: who ended which early.

**Disappearing messages (M16.4)**

- [x] A message's time is fixed as it's sent, from its channel's as the den reads it within the send's transaction, and an edit leaves it; a client takes a time only on a DM's or a group's message, and only after its sending.
- [x] The den deletes each at its time with `secure_delete` on, its files off the disk and its reactions with it, and as it starts what passed while it was stopped; each member's Dens lets its copies go at their time by its own clock, connected or not, and keeps out a copy past it, and the page drops one as well, neither waiting on the den.
- [x] Setting a time counts toward the member's writes, and is logged by IDs and the time alone.

**The Android app's updates (M12.4)**

- [x] The updater downloads an APK only once the release's checksums check out, against the release workflow's identity and Sigstore's trusted root, as cosign would check them, and offers it only matching them, newer than itself; a build without the identity updates from nothing.
- [x] Android's installer installs it only once the member confirms, and only signed with the release's key; the app asks for `REQUEST_INSTALL_PACKAGES` alone.
- [x] The APK's key reaches only the release job, from `main`, and a release's build refuses an APK that key alone didn't sign. The release pipeline checks each release's signature as the app will before publishing it.

**Hosting and the Flatpak (M9)**

- [x] Nothing of the den listens until the owner turns hosting on, with the local password, and turning it off closes the den listener and the media ports at once.
- [x] The Windows firewall rules admit only `dens.exe`, on the media ports alone.
- [x] The Flatpak keeps no copy of its key that opens without the local password unless the member asks it to remember, and never keeps the password itself. It takes the Secret portal's secret only once it has answered the same twice, and never replaces a remembered copy that doesn't open.
- [x] The Flatpak refuses data a newer version wrote.
- [x] The Flatpak's repository is GPG-signed and built only from release binaries whose cosign signatures CI has checked, and its key reaches only the job that publishes it, from `main`.

**Logs and data**

- [x] No message content, tokens, keys or IPs in logs; log IDs and event types only. From M8 that holds for the addresses Go's network errors name too, of dens and of the release host. Pion's own messages, which name addresses, reach the log only in development instances.
- [x] A page's notes on its call reach the log by their own fields alone, words from fixed lists and numbers bounded, at most two a second after a burst of 64; the logs' download is a paired browser's alone (M11).
- [x] SQLite `secure_delete=ON`.
- [x] Signed releases: the installers and `dens update` verify cosign signatures against the release workflow's identity.
- [x] No release older than the installed one installs, and only command-line flags, never the environment, change where releases come from or skip their verification, which a binary the release pipeline built refuses (M8).
- [x] Nothing a crash leaves in the data directory's `tmp` outlives the next start, and a backup holds every file its snapshot names (M8).
- [x] The binary carries the license notices of everything it includes, and CI fails when they're stale (M3).

## Public site

dens.chat is a landing page and a few pages of docs for people using Dens, built with Hugo and Hextra in `docs/` (see `docs/README.md`), and published with v1.

**The landing page** says what Dens is in one plain paragraph, then argues for it in three sections, hosting it yourself (01), encrypted and pseudonymous (02) and free (03). Its tone is confident and plain, not combative: the hero's three lines are what Dens isn't (on their terms, under their watch, their product), section 01 says in one paragraph what a den is for and what it isn't, a place where the people running it set the rules and keep them with the moderation tools, and not a place for threats or for material whose making hurt someone, and section 02's table compares Dens with Discord, Signal and Matrix on who sits in between, conceding where they do better. Four notes on how it's built fold under "under the hood". It links into the docs at the install guide (`/docs/install/`, from an Install button beside the hero's caption), the hosting guide (`/docs/host/`) and Privacy (`/docs/privacy/`).

**The docs** cover what the app can't show for itself: what happens before Dens runs, outside it (a domain, a router, Caddy), and across devices and time (passwords, recovery, backups), and, once, who can read what.

- They say what things are for rather than which buttons to press, so the page can change without them going stale, and they hold no screenshots, for the same reason.
- They don't repeat the page's settings, which explain themselves a line each (M8.3), or the commands, which `--help` lists.
- Each fact lives on one page, and the others link to it.
- Readers skim: a step, or a section's first lines, say in a line or two what to do, and why, and what to check, fold under it in Hextra's details, closed, for whoever wants them (M17.1). What could catch a reader out, such as what removing Dens deletes or who can read what, stays out of the folds.

What the rest of this document commits the docs to, by page:

- Install:
  - Downloading the installer and running it, with its signature check as an optional step (see Privileged steps and updates, in [installed.md](installed.md)).

  - The Flatpak, on a page of its own (`/docs/install/flatpak/`), which `dens install`'s refusals on NixOS and SteamOS link to; releases before M17.1 link `/docs/install/#the-flatpak`, which lands on the choice, the Flatpak's card in view: its link, `flatpak install` as the fallback, an alias for its commands (`alias dens='flatpak run --command=dens chat.dens.Dens'`), and that it hosts no den; that installing for the whole system asks for the admin password, and that GNOME Software calls Dens potentially unsafe for X11, which push to talk under X11 needs; that updates come through the software center, not the link again, which Discover fails to open once Dens is installed; and that a KDE with no wallet asks to make one at the first Remember, where Classic, with the sign-in password, is the choice that works ([flatpak.md](flatpak.md)).

- Host a den:
  - Hosting starts from the settings, with nothing to choose at install (Install and service account, in [installed.md](installed.md)).
  - Which ports to forward but not how, since routers vary too much, and a small VPS as the easy alternative (Networking and deployment).
  - A minimal Caddyfile, and Caddy as a service on both platforms, which covers ports 80 and 443 (Caddy).
  - Calls need the den's name to point at its machine, or the router in front of it: a tunnel or CDN carries a den's HTTPS but not its calls. The owner's own calls work before any port is forwarded, so the test is a call from outside the network (Media addresses).

  - A backup keeps what the den held when it was made, past the auto-delete period, until the backup is deleted (Retention).

  - A second den is a second instance, with ports of its own (Install and service account, in [installed.md](installed.md)). An install that hosts updates as one binary with its client, and a den that can't wait on its owner's updates moves to an install of its own (Versioning and compatibility).
- Privacy:
  - The den can read channels and the files in them, could listen to calls and watch screen shares, and sees who DMs whom and when; DMs and the photos in them are end-to-end encrypted (Privacy model, End-to-end encryption, Calls). The docs say the den rather than its owner: whoever or whatever gets hold of the den's computer, someone else who uses it or malware on it, can do the same.

  - The installed service keeps its data under its own account, out of reach of the member's programs; the Flatpak runs as the member, so their programs can read it ([flatpak.md](flatpak.md)).
  - The short philosophy section and its recommendations: Linux over Windows, Ungoogled Chromium, no identifying details for low-stakes use, and Signal, Qubes OS and similar tools for higher stakes, none of them bulletproof (Privacy model).
