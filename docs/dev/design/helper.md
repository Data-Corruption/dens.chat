**Global shortcuts (M7)**

A page sees keys only while it has focus, so push to talk can't reach it from a full-screen game. Discord's web app has the same limit, and its desktop app doesn't. Nor can the local service see keys: on Windows it runs in session 0, where Windows blocks all keyboard input to services, and on Linux it runs as its own account, outside the desktop session.

*The helper*

- A small helper, `dens shortcuts`, the same binary, runs as the desktop user in their session from login. One helper serves every instance on the machine: it finds them in the instance roots, which users may list, and holds a stream on each one's control endpoint, which answers only that instance's desktop user.
- The service sends the helper the member's keys, and whether one of its pages is in a call. The helper sends back each press and release as the action it's bound to: push to talk, toggle mute, push to mute or, from M19.3, toggle deafen. The service passes them to its pages, and the page in the call acts on them.
- The helper watches keys only while a page is in a call, or from M20 while Relay hands them to the call another of the member's devices holds (see Relay). Otherwise it watches nothing and costs nothing.
- The service keeps the keys, since the helper needs them, so every browser paired with the instance shares them. How the member sends, and the level, stay with the browser.
- A key is a keyboard key with any modifiers or, on Windows and in the page, one of the mouse's side buttons, which many players push to talk with. The page names a key by its place on the keyboard (`KeyboardEvent.code`), and the helper finds what the current layout makes of it.
- When the service comes back from an update at another version, the helper starts the new binary and exits.
- The helper's stream is the control endpoint's `shortcuts` request: JSON lines go both ways until either side closes it, each pinging every 15 seconds and giving up on the other after 45 without a line. The service sends `hello` with its version, then `state`, its keys and whether it holds a call, and from M20.2 whether to read the computer's sound, as any changes; the helper answers each with `source`, what it covers, and sends `press` for each press and release, and from M20.2 `sound`, what keeps it from reading the sound once that has lasted two seconds, and that nothing does once it reads. An instance holds one stream from its desktop user's helper: a newer one replaces it, and the old one, told `replaced`, exits.
- While Relay shares the computer's sound (M20.2), the helper reads it and sends it on a second request, `sound`, as binary frames, each a 4-byte length and its bytes, in place of lines; in the Flatpak it's a pipe inside the service, as the stream is. The service takes one such request at a time, the newest. See Relay for what's read and how.
- The keys are the service's setting `voiceKeys`, which the settings' API takes whole. The page hears of them, and of the helper, over its socket with the service: `keys`, with the keys, whether a helper is connected, what it covers and its note, as any of them changes, and `key` for each press during a call, which the service drops outside one, or hands to Relay. From M20 a press from another of the member's devices comes `relayed`.

*Windows*

- The helper reads the bound keys' state every 10 ms (`GetAsyncKeyState`), with no keyboard hook, so it sees nothing else typed.
- While a program run as administrator has focus, Windows hides every key from the helper, as it does from Discord (User Interface Privilege Isolation). The settings say so.
- The installer registers a scheduled task that starts the helper at the desktop user's logon, with their interactive token, at normal priority, since a task's default of below normal could fall behind a game, and with no time limit. It runs the task at once, so nobody signs out after installing. Uninstalling the last instance ends the task and deletes it.
- The Windows spike (`spikes/README.md` at commit a03d820), on Windows 11 24H2 with a Ryzen 7 9800X3D: the reads saw a key pressed with a game full screen in front and in Steam, and nothing with an elevated terminal in front. At normal and below-normal priority, with the game running, they came every 10 ms at the median and the 99th percentile, one gap in some 15,000 reaching 20 ms, for 0.2 to 1% of one core. No game with anti-cheat was at hand.
- `dens.exe` carries a manifest whose `consoleAllocationPolicy` is `detached`, which Windows 11 24H2 and later honor: started outside a console, as at logon, it gets none, not even one `CREATE_NEW_CONSOLE` asks for, so the helper opens no window, while in a shell it runs as any console program does. Go doesn't embed manifests, so a resource file generated for each architecture sits beside `cmd/main.go`, and CI checks that it's current.

*Linux*

- Wayland: the desktop's global shortcuts portal, in GNOME 48 and later, KDE Plasma and Hyprland, reports a shortcut's press and release. There the desktop owns the keys. The member sets them up with it from the settings: the helper binds the actions in a session of the portal, with the member's keys as the preferred triggers, and the desktop asks them to confirm or change each. The service keeps that they did, and as the helper starts it binds again, which the desktop answers without a dialog, since it remembers the keys for the app across sessions and sign-ins. A dialog turned down isn't asked again until the member asks, and stopping ends the session, which a new one replaces, unbound: a session binds once.
- The settings then show the desktop's names for the keys. Changing them opens the desktop's own settings for them where its portal can (`ConfigureShortcuts`, version 2, as KDE's), and otherwise says where they are (GNOME's portal is version 1: its Settings, under Apps, then Dens). The shortcuts' IDs never change, so the desktop asks only once. An action added later, as toggle deafen (M19.3), joins the bind as the helper starts, and stays the page's until the desktop names a key for it.
- GNOME repeats a held key's press as the keyboard repeats, and reports one release; the helper passes one press and one release.
- The Linux spike (`spikes/README.md` at commit 0f99022) drove the portal as the helper does, on Fedora 44's GNOME 50 and KDE Plasma under Wayland. Both took the app ID once the `.desktop` file was installed, showed their dialog on the first bind with the preferred triggers, each changeable, and later bound without one in a few milliseconds, across sign-ins and restarts. Presses reached the session with a game full screen in front.
- A key bound on the desktop reaches no program but the helper, a game included, and the settings say so. A keyboard whose top row sends media keys unless Fn is held never sends a function key it doesn't hold Fn for.
- The portal refuses an unsandboxed app whose app ID no installed `.desktop` file backs (xdg-desktop-portal 1.21 and later), so the helper registers as `chat.dens.Dens` before anything else (`org.freedesktop.host.portal.Registry`), and the installer adds `chat.dens.Dens.desktop` to `/usr/local/share/applications`, with Dens's mark as its icon. The file also opens the page (`dens open`) from the desktop's menu, and GNOME keeps the app's shortcuts on its page in Settings. Older portals take the app ID from the helper's systemd unit, which the autostart entry's name sets.
- Desktops without the portal (GNOME before 48, Sway, COSMIC) keep the page's keys.
- X11: the helper reads the bound keys' state every 10 ms (`XQueryKeymap`), over a small X11 client of its own that connects to the local display with the X authority's cookie. A key's place maps to the X server's keycode through Linux's input codes. A mouse's buttons aren't keys to the X server, so side buttons stay the page's there.
- The installer adds an XDG autostart entry (`/etc/xdg/autostart/chat.dens.Dens.desktop`), which GNOME and KDE run as the systemd user unit `app-chat.dens.Dens@autostart.service`, and starts or restarts that unit in the desktop user's session through their systemd user manager, when they're signed in to a desktop. Uninstalling the last instance removes the entries and the icon; a helper still running stops once no instance answers it.
- The helper speaks D-Bus through `godbus/dbus/v5`, which needs nothing beyond `golang.org/x/sys`.

*Notifications (M12.3)*

- The helper shows the member's notifications where no page can (see Notifications): the stream carries `notify`, a notification that replaces its channel's, and `unnotify`, those to take back by their keys, and the helper's `source` says whether it shows them (`notifies`). It opens the desktop's notifications beside its run (M22.2), so a notification service that hangs, as D-Bus lets one for two minutes, holds back neither the keys nor Relay's sound: until they open it says it shows none, and keeps what it's sent, showing what wasn't taken back meanwhile once they do. Its `hello` names the page's origin, for the address a click opens. It shows each instance's apart, under an ID of its own.
- Linux: through the freedesktop notification service on the session bus, as Dens (the `desktop-entry` hint names `chat.dens.Dens`, whose icon the desktop then shows), with its text escaped where the service reads markup, since it's a member's. A click on its body invokes its `default` action, which the service hands back to the helper: the helper asks the instance for a pairing link, as `dens open` does, and opens it at the message. A helper that starts where no service answers shows none, and the settings say notifications show only in a Dens tab.
- Windows: as toasts, through Windows Runtime, under the app ID `chat.dens.Dens`. Windows shows a desktop app's toasts only under an app ID a Start menu shortcut carries, so the installer makes one, `Dens.lnk` in the Start menu for every user, which opens the page as `dens open` does; the helper shows none without it. A toast's click opens its address through the browser by itself (protocol activation), so it works from the Action Center too, whatever runs then.
- The Flatpak's helper, inside its service, shows them through the desktop's Notification portal, which knows the app by its Flatpak ID; a click comes back as the portal's `ActionInvoked` signal, for an action without the `app.` prefix, which the portal would otherwise take for one of the app's own D-Bus actions, and the service opens the page paired through the OpenURI portal.

*The page*

- The settings' keys say whether they work in other programs, with an unobtrusive mark. Where the desktop owns the keys, the keys card offers to set them up with it, says to confirm them in its dialog while that's up, and once they're set up shows the desktop's names for them, with a way to change them and one to stop using them. A session binds once, so where one that bound them holds none of them now, as when they were taken out in the desktop's settings, the card says so, with where to change them and a way to start over (M11).
- While the helper covers a key, during a call the page ignores its own presses of it and acts on the helper's: on Windows and X11 the helper sees a key pressed in the page too, and toggle mute would fire twice. Outside a call the helper reads nothing, and the page's own keys work as before, except those the desktop owns, whose keys may be others. A key that types something still doesn't fire while the member types in a text field; one the desktop owns never reaches the field.
- A side button the member bound is the page's while it has focus: its press and release don't take the browser back or forward.
- The helper tells the service what its keys can do (whether the desktop confirms and names them) and what the system keeps from them, as a note the settings show, never which system it runs on, so no platform difference reaches the local API.

**Relay (M20)**

A member's installs on one network link straight to each other, and the den only introduces them. A link carries the voice keys of one install to the call another holds, and from M20.2 one computer's sound to play on another; from M20.3 the Android app is one of them, its keys its call's button, and listening. The spike that tried it all first is `spikes/relay/README.md` at commit 7e608d6. Relay leaves out a link across two networks or the internet, a microphone relayed, the Android app as a source, the sound compressed, a volume of its own, and muting the source's speakers for the member.

*The introduction*

- `relay.signal` carries a box of 1,024 bytes from one of a member's devices to their others, or to one by its key ID. The den passes it to that member's sockets alone, never back to the sender's device, keeps nothing of it, and limits it to 30 in 10 seconds a member, as it does `voice.key` (see protocol.md's Relay). Its info says `relay`, and an install sends boxes only through a den that does, connected, where it holds the member's seal.
- A box is sealed under a key made from the member's DM seal by HKDF, salted with the den's ID under a label of its own (`dens-relay-v1`), as the notes' key is, bound to the den, the member, the device it came from and the one it's for, and padded to its size: the den learns that two of a member's devices spoke, and when. A device without the seal opens nothing, and isn't listed.
- Inside is a note of `internal/relay`'s, in JSON: the install's Relay ID, 16 random bytes kept in its configuration, by which two installs that share more than one den know they're one pair, and which a restore makes afresh, since it names the computer; its name, the label its dens list it under; what it offers, `keys` while Keybind relay is on and `sound` while it shares its sound (M20.2); its time, which the other side takes within a minute; and its kind. `who` asks the member's other devices to say they're here, which `here` does, and again as Keybind relay changes or the install takes a call; `hello` asks for a link with this side's half of it, and what it `wants` of it: `keys`, of whichever device holds the member's call, to every other device, or `sound`, to the one device whose sound it listens to, by its key ID where it last said it was here. `answer` agrees with the other half, naming the hello's attempt, from a device that can give what's wanted. A device waiting on an unanswered hello for keys asks again at once on a `here` that offers keys, as from the device that took the call back after the den held it.
- A half is a DTLS certificate's SHA-256, ICE credentials made for the attempt, a UDP port, and up to 8 of the install's IPv4 addresses on private networks. A box naming any other address is refused, so none aims an install at the internet. Offers a newer Dens makes are left out, not refused.

*The link*

- One Pion peer connection between two installs, by Relay ID, through whichever den introduced them. The service holds it: Windows raises a firewall prompt for a desktop program holding a port, and none for the service. The spike linked, with no rule and no prompt, through Windows' firewall on a Public network from a service, firewalld's rejecting zone, NixOS's firewall, the systemd unit's sandbox, the Flatpak's and the Android app, each up 20 to 260 ms after its second side started; guests behind VMware's NAT never linked.
- Each side writes the other's description from its half, the lower Relay ID offering: host candidates alone, over IPv4, on an ephemeral port held on every interface, which names to ICE only the half's addresses. No STUN, nothing listening, no firewall rule. A development instance takes loopback from the other side too, and offers it only where it has no private address, since any other install refuses a half that names it.
- Both sides send checks from the answer on, for the 30 seconds an introduction is good, 150 binding requests at Pion's 200 ms, since each side's sending is what lets the other's packets through its firewall. A link that never comes up says the two aren't on one network.
- ICE calls a link disconnected after a second without a packet, and checks every half second once connected. The link fails itself 3 seconds after it's disconnected, and an attempt once its introduction runs out: Pion's own failed timeout would end the first checks too, after 4 seconds.
- Two negotiated data channels: `ctl`, reliable, carries each side's state, whether it holds a call and whether Keybind relay is on, and from M20.2 whether it shares its sound now and whether it listens to the other side's, and a listener's end, `taken` or `stopped`; `now`, unordered and never sent again, carries a ping every 250 ms and keys. SCTP's retransmission timer is capped at 100 ms, without which a cut of a fifth of a second stops every channel for a second.
- Beside the channels, an audio section carries the sound as RTP (M20.2), each side's description naming the other's SSRC, which its role fixes: SRTP under the link's own DTLS, one payload type of the link's own, named `L16/48000/2`, which carries the helper's piece as it came, and no interceptors, so nothing is reported, acknowledged or sent again, and a packet the path loses is 5 ms the player fades across. RTP took the phone's receiving from 31% of a core to 22% against a third data channel, and Windows' sending from 3.8% to 2.3%, and leaves SCTP to the keys (the spike's Sound as RTP).
- A link closes once neither side has needed it for 2 seconds, which a call moving from one device to the other rides out, and closing tells the other side at once. One lost fails within 4 seconds, and the side that wants it asks again a second later; an unanswered hello, or a link that never came up, waits 2 seconds before the next, then twice as long each time, up to two minutes.
- A device asks only while it has no link to the call, or to the device it listens to, so a hello from one this side links with finds that link stale, as when the device started again, and replaces it; a device that asks again gives up its earlier attempt, whose link the new one replaces too. Two hellos that cross, each side asking the other at once, as a device asking for keys while the one holding the call asks for its sound, are both answered, and both sides keep the link of the greater attempt.
- Local addresses stay in memory. Pion's messages, which name them, never reach the log, nor do errors that could; nothing writes them or gives them to the page.

*Keys*

- With Keybind relay on, an install that sees its member in a call it doesn't hold, in the den's `voice.state`, says `hello` there, and the install holding the call answers if its switch is on too. Once they're linked, the first one's helper is told a call is on, so it reads its own bound keys as for a call of its own, and its hub hands Relay what it would have dropped.
- Keys cross as states: every action held, and each toggle's count of its presses, every 20 ms while a key is held or for 200 ms after a change, and every 100 ms otherwise.
- The other hub takes them as a second helper's: a hold lasts while the helper or any device holds it; a toggle fires for each step its count moves, at most 8 a state and never on a link's first; and 300 ms after the last word from a device, of any kind, what it held is let go.
- The page acts on a relayed press, whatever its own helper covers, and only in a call it holds.
- A change reached the other side in a fraction of a millisecond on a wire and 3.2 ms at the median from the S24 over Wi-Fi, 82 ms at worst with its screen off, losing no state in some 16,000 (the spike).

*Sound (M20.2)*

- A computer shares its sound with Share this computer's sound on, and one of the member's other devices listens to it from its settings. The sound goes to one listener at a time: a device that starts listening takes it from the one before, which is told (`taken`). An install plays or sends, never both, since two devices playing each other would loop: listening pauses its own sharing, which its `here` stops offering, and a device listening to it is told it stopped.
- The helper reads it, since it runs in the desktop session, while the service holds the link, since a desktop program holding a port raises Windows' firewall prompt. The service asks for it in the helper's `state` only while a device listens, and the helper sends it on its `sound` request in pieces of 240 frames, 5 ms at 48 kHz: 4 bytes of rate, then 16-bit stereo, little-endian. It fills with silence where the system gives nothing for 60 ms, as Windows does while nothing plays, up to 10 ms ago, and then every 10 ms until it gives again, so a listener's buffer rides the silence as it does the sound; and it skips a second it stood still, as a computer asleep does. The service sends each piece as an RTP packet, its timestamp the piece's place in the stream, in frames.
- Windows: WASAPI's loopback of the default output, in shared mode, which converts it to 16-bit stereo at the output's own rate and mixes more channels down, written on the COM vtables the notifications use, no module of its own. It asks what's waiting every 5 ms, and which output is the default each second, opening the new one as that changes, or 200 ms after its device goes. Muted, the loopback still carries the sound; at volume 0 it carries silence.
- Linux: the default output's monitor (`@DEFAULT_MONITOR@`) over PulseAudio's protocol, which PipeWire speaks too, through `jfreymuth/pulse`'s protocol layer, pure Go with nothing behind it, on a connection the capture makes itself, at 48 kHz in 5 ms pieces. PipeWire moves the recording to a new default's monitor itself; PulseAudio leaves it on the old one's, silent, so the capture asks each second where its stream sits, by a property of its own, and opens again once it has sat astray for two. PipeWire's monitor is taken before the output's volume, so muted it still carries the sound; PulseAudio's is taken after. Its socket sends its credentials with every message (`SO_PASSCRED`), by which PulseAudio knows a client that can't read the member's cookie, as in the Flatpak's sandbox: the library's own client sends none, and PulseAudio refused it there as often as not. Closing the connection ends the recording, and the requests each second find a server gone. The Flatpak reaches the socket through `--socket=pulseaudio` (see [flatpak.md](flatpak.md)), and the Android app reads nothing.
- The listening device's service hands each piece to the page that listens, on a WebSocket of its own (`/api/relay/listen?device=<Relay ID>&batch=N`): binary messages of several packets, each a 4-byte length and the packet, its place, 4 bytes, then the piece; a message waits for its last packet at most a packet's time past them all. Four packets to a message on Low and eight on High: on the phone, four took the app's three processes from 82% of a core to 70%, and eight to 65%. As the listening ends the service says why in a text message, then closes: another tab took over (`page`), another device did (`taken`), the member stopped it on the other end (`stopped`), the device no longer shares (`unshared`), it didn't answer for 10 seconds (`noanswer`), nor once its link was lost (`lost`), the link never came up (`apart`), or the service stopped. A lost link is asked for again once, after a second. The listening logs at debug the packets it heard and those lost, by the gaps in their places.
- Listening starts at the click of Listen, which a browser needs before it plays anything, belongs to the page that clicked, as a call does, and ends with Stop or with the page; it never starts by itself. The page makes an audio context at 48 kHz in the click, and plays through an audio worklet (`relay.worklet.js`), fed by a worker that holds the socket (`relay.worker.js`) over a channel of their own, so the page's thread carries none of the sound. The worklet keeps a buffer, the member's choice, Low's 40 ms or High's 150, or one and a half of the longest its output has lately waited between calls and 10 ms more where that's more; plays up to 0.5% fast or slow to stay there, and skips back to it from 150 ms past it; fades out before a gap the path left and in after it, and starts again where its places jump half a second either way, as on a link made again, whose places start over; and where it runs dry fades out what's left, and in again once its buffer is back. It reports what it counts every two seconds: packets, those lost, late and dry, its buffer and its level. The browser keeps the choice of buffer, High on a phone until the member makes one.
- A bar where the call's sits says so on both ends, with Stop: on the page that listens, how its listening stands, connecting, listening or waiting for sound, and why the last one ended; and on every page of the computer whose sound it is, the device it goes to, and why no sound comes, as no helper running, from Relay's `relay` events on the page's stream. Stop there (`/api/relay/stop`) ends that listening, telling the device (`stopped`), and leaves the switch on.
- The spike measured the sound on Windows, Fedora's GNOME and KDE, NixOS's Flatpak and the S24: nothing lost in two million packets on its wire and Wi-Fi, about 90 ms from capture to output in software, 150 ms on a phone's speaker and 435 ms on Bluetooth headphones, most of that the headphones'. Windows' capture and sending took 2.3% of a core, Linux's capture 2 to 2.5%, and a desktop browser's page played an hour without running dry on 40 ms, in front, behind another tab and minimized.

*The phone (M20.3)*

- The Android app relays as a computer does but for what a phone lacks: its keys are its call's button, and it shares no sound, so `relay.can` says `keys` alone; it listens, as every install may. It lists its addresses as Pion does there (`wlynxg/anet`), since Android refuses an app the netlink request Go's own listing makes.
- Android 17 keeps an app off its own network unless the member allows it (`ACCESS_LOCAL_NETWORK`): without it every packet to the other side fails with `EPERM`. So the app links with nothing until Android lets it, while its `who` and `here` go through the dens as ever. Relay says what it wants of Android on the shell's stream (`relay`): `on` while Keybind relay is on or the page listens, for which the shell asks the member once, as they first want it, and `listening` while the page plays. The shell says whether Android lets the app reach its network, and whether the member refused when asked (`local`), as its stream opens and as that changes; refused, the Relay section says where to allow it, and a listening ends saying so (`local`). Earlier Androids ask nothing.
- While another of the member's devices holds their call and this install's keys go there, Relay's `relay` events name that device and whether the member is muted there (`call`), and the page shows the call's button under its name, where the page has the button: a touch screen, or a page without keys. Its presses go to the service on the page's event socket (`relay.key`, an action and whether it's down), and the hub hands them to Relay as it hands it a helper's, in the role the member gave the button; outside a call held elsewhere they go nowhere.
- While the page listens, the shell gives the service media playback's type beside its own, holds the page as shown, as for a call, and holds a Wi-Fi lock, letting them go as the listening ends or Dens's stream closes; Back at the page's root sends the app behind the others. Android plays the page's player as media: under the media volume, on headphones paired to the phone, beside other apps' sound.
- The player's own context on the phone is opened for playback (`latencyHint: 'playback'`), not as a call's context is: Chromium keeps a closed context's output stream a few seconds and hands it to the next context opened like it, which would play as the call's, or not at all (see android.md's Calls).
- A relay playing as media goes silent on Bluetooth headphones once a call's microphone opens on the phone, as Android moves them to their call link. So while the phone is in a call the player plays through the call's context, which the page makes once the microphone is open: a worklet node of its own there, which the worker then feeds, on the call's route, in a call's quality on Bluetooth. As the call ends it moves back into a context of its own, before the call's closes. Where the relay plays on the loudspeaker, a call made it louder and kept its quality.
- The spike measured the S24 Ultra on Android 17, its WebView 157, playing Windows' sound: nothing lost over Wi-Fi, but with the screen off or another app in front Wi-Fi hands packets over in bunches, hundreds in ten seconds 20 to 50 ms late and some 110 to 160, which ran a 40 ms buffer dry 8 times in 25 minutes with the screen off, and 19 in seven minutes as the app went behind another, hence High's 150 ms on a phone; the Wi-Fi lock the shell holds counts only while the app is in front with the screen on. On Bluetooth headphones over an hour with the screen off, the buffer still ran dry four times, each just after the headphones' output stalled 134 to 187 ms. The app's three processes took about 65% of a core with eight packets to a message, and the phone about 5% of its battery an hour, against 27 mAh an hour idle.

*The settings*

- Relay, a section after Voice where the install relays: Keybind relay's switch, off unless set; from M20.2 Audio relay, with Share this computer's sound, off unless set, where the install can share it, its line on what muting does, and the Buffer, Low or High; and Your devices, the member's other installs that answered `who`, by name, with how each one's link stands or what it offers, Listen beside each that shares its sound, and a line to the guide. While the section is open it asks again every few seconds, the service asking the member's devices every 10 at most, and lists one heard from in the last minute.
- The switches are the service's settings `relayKeys` and `relaySound`, kept beside `relayID`, and `/api/settings` says what the install relays, as `relay.can`: `keys`, and `sound` where its helper reads the computer's. On a phone, Keybind relay's switch is Relay the call's button, and `/api/relay/devices` says whether the member refused the app its network, which the section then says, with where to allow it (M20.3).
